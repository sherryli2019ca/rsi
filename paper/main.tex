\title{Which Fix Should We Test? Budgeted Causal Verification of\\LLM Failure Attributions for Self-Improving Agents}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Self-improving LLM agents increasingly rely on an LLM to read failed trajectories, name a root cause, and decide which part of the agent to change. These attributions are cheap but unreliable, and checking them by replaying the agent with a candidate fix is reliable but expensive. We ask which attributions are worth checking under a fixed replay budget. We cast the problem as learning a causal bipartite graph from failure categories to modifiable agent components, where each edge is an unknown intervention effect. Our method, \carve{}, places a Bayesian model on this graph that separates whether a component is a cause from how good a particular patch is, uses LLM attributions as a calibrated prior rather than as a verdict, and accounts for noisy category labels. It chooses the next intervention (which category, which component, which patch, and whether to run a cheap single-step check or a full replay) by maximising the expected decision value gained per unit cost. Two short propositions characterise when the LLM prior saves replays and when cheap checks pay off, and both predictions match our simulations. In a controlled testbed with known ground truth, \carve{} recovers 79\% of the oracle's repairable failure mass at a budget of 50 full replays, against 58\% for uncertainty sampling and 27\% for a do-then-verify baseline; inside a multi-round self-improvement loop it reaches in two rounds a success rate that the strongest baseline reaches only after six. We release the method and a fault-injected tool-use environment for real-agent studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A growing family of systems improves LLM agents from their own failures: the agent runs, an analyst model reads the failed trajectories, and its diagnosis drives a change to a prompt, a tool, or the orchestration code \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa}. The diagnosis step has become a research topic in its own right under the name \emph{failure attribution} \citep{zhang2025whichagent,zhang2026agentracer,zhu2025agentdebug}. Two findings from that literature motivate this paper.

First, LLM attribution is unreliable. On the recent Who\&When Pro benchmark the strongest model reaches 74\% step-level accuracy on text traces but only 22\% F1 on identifying the error mode, and accuracy falls to roughly 50\% on traces longer than 12k tokens \citep{liu2026whowhenpro}. Models tend to label failures by their visible symptom rather than their cause. A self-improvement loop that trusts these labels will often change the wrong component, and a wrong change can hurt: accurate failure prediction does not imply that acting on it helps \citep{vasudev2026intervention}.

Second, attributions can be checked by intervention. If a diagnosis says that a tool description caused a failure, one can patch that description, replay the trajectory from the failing step, and see whether the task now succeeds \citep{ma2025dover,lin2026reflect,bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}. This is the agent analogue of a $do(\cdot)$ experiment \citep{pearl2009causality}, and it is far more reliable than reading the trace. It is also expensive: each check costs a partial agent rollout, outcomes are stochastic so one replay is rarely conclusive, and a failed check is ambiguous because the patch itself may simply be bad.

Existing intervention-based methods verify one hypothesis at a time, usually with a fixed number of replays per hypothesis. In a self-improvement loop the question is different. There are many failure categories and many components that could be changed, the replay budget is fixed, and what matters is not the accuracy of any single diagnosis but how much of the agent's failure mass is repaired at the end. We therefore ask: \emph{given a budget of interventions, which attributions should be verified, with which patch, and how cheaply?}

We make the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item We formulate budgeted verification as learning a \emph{category--component causal graph} whose edges are unknown intervention effects, and we separate the causal question (is component $j$ a cause of category $i$?) from the engineering question (is this particular patch any good?) by giving each cell several candidate patches (\S\ref{sec:problem}).
\item We propose \carve{} (Cost-Aware Replay for Verifying Edges), a Bayesian method that uses LLM attributions as a calibrated prior, models label noise and spurious recovery, and selects interventions across cells, patches and two fidelities (a cheap single-step check and a full replay) with a multi-step knowledge-gradient rule divided by cost (\S\ref{sec:method}).
\item We give two propositions that say when the LLM prior saves replays and when a cheap check is worth running, in terms of quantities a practitioner can estimate (\S\ref{sec:analysis}).
\item In a controlled testbed with known ground truth, \carve{} needs roughly a third of the budget of the strongest baseline to reach the same repaired failure mass, never does worse than a flat prior even when LLM attributions are nearly useless, and converts that efficiency into faster agent improvement inside a multi-round loop (\S\ref{sec:experiments}). We also release ShopDesk, a deterministic tool-use environment with injected faults that provides ground truth for real-LLM studies (\S\ref{sec:real}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Failure attribution for LLM agents.}
\citet{zhang2025whichagent} introduced the Who\&When benchmark and compared all-at-once, step-by-step and binary-search prompting for locating the responsible agent and step; \citet{liu2026whowhenpro} extended it to longer and multimodal traces and found that attribution remains far from solved. AgenTracer uses counterfactual replay and fault injection to build training data for a small attribution model \citep{zhang2026agentracer}; AgentDebug pairs an error taxonomy with targeted corrective feedback \citep{zhu2025agentdebug}; ErrorProbe traces dependencies backwards and keeps a verified memory of error patterns \citep{li2026errorprobe}. Taxonomies such as MAST \citep{cemri2025mast} give attribution a fixed vocabulary, and AdaMAST induces a system-specific taxonomy from traces whose axes map to intervention points \citep{cemri2026adamast}. AdaMAST validates its taxonomy by agreement between LLM annotators; we instead treat the mapping from categories to components as a causal claim to be checked by intervention.

\paragraph{Verification by intervention.}
DoVer generates hypotheses from logs and validates each by editing a message or plan and re-running, with several runs per intervention \citep{ma2025dover}. REFLECT tests diagnosis-specific patches by targeted replay and feeds verified outcome changes back into attribution \citep{lin2026reflect}. CausalFlow scores steps by their causal responsibility under counterfactual interventions \citep{bonagiri2026causalflow}, and Causal Agent Replay formalises step interventions in a structural causal model with a point-of-commitment rule \citep{shah2026car}. AgentDebugX provides a toolkit with policy-gated reruns and a store of failure--diagnosis--repair bundles \citep{zhu2026agentdebugx}. All of these operate per trajectory or per hypothesis and do not decide how to allocate a shared budget across hypotheses, patches and levels of fidelity, which is our focus. Our method can use any of them as the replay primitive.

\paragraph{Self-improving agents.}
Reflexion, Self-Refine and ExpeL turn feedback into verbal lessons \citep{shinn2023reflexion,madaan2023selfrefine,zhao2024expel}; ADAS, the Darwin G\"odel Machine and GEPA search over agent designs or prompts guided by evaluation and failure logs \citep{hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa}. HarnessFix attributes failures to harness artefacts and repairs them \citep{chen2026harnessfix}. Bayesian-Agent keeps Beta posteriors over the reliability of skills and applies threshold rules to patch, split or retire them \citep{wu2026bayesianagent}; its posterior is over skills rather than causal edges, and actions are chosen by fixed thresholds rather than by the value of information. \carve{} is complementary: it decides which proposed fixes to trust before they are applied.

\paragraph{Experimental design.}
Our selection rule is a knowledge-gradient policy \citep{frazier2008kg} within Bayesian experimental design \citep{chaloner1995bayesian}, with multi-fidelity observations \citep{kandasamy2016multifidelity} and a thresholding objective \citep{locatelli2016thresholding}; the stopping behaviour mirrors sequential testing \citep{wald1945sequential}. Active learning of causal structure from interventions is classical \citep{hauser2014active}. The contribution here is not a new design criterion but a formulation of agent self-improvement in which these tools apply, together with a model of the specific nuisances (bad patches, noisy labels, spurious recovery, LLM priors) that arise there.

An agent is built from \emph{components} $j\in\{1,\dots,J\}$: sections of its system prompt, tool descriptions, tool implementations, orchestration settings. Its failures have been grouped into \emph{categories} $i\in\{1,\dots,I\}$ by an LLM, with $f_i$ the share of failures in category $i$. For every cell $(i,j)$ an analyst LLM can propose $K$ candidate patches $a_{ijk}$ that rewrite component $j$ to address category $i$.

\paragraph{Interventions.} An intervention $(i,j,k,\ell)$ samples a failed trajectory labelled $i$, applies patch $a_{ijk}$, and observes a binary outcome at fidelity $\ell$. A \emph{full replay} ($\ell=F$) resumes the episode from the attributed failing step and records whether the task now succeeds. A \emph{single-step check} ($\ell=S$) regenerates only the failing step and asks a judge whether the diagnosed error is gone. They cost $c_F$ and $c_S\ll c_F$.

\paragraph{Edges and patch quality.} We write $e_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ for whether component $j$ is a cause of category $i$, meaning some change to $j$ repairs a non-trivial fraction of its failures, and $q_{ijk}\in[0,1]$ for the repair probability of patch $k$ when $e_{ij}=1$. This separation matters because a negative replay outcome is ambiguous: either $j$ is the wrong component or $a_{ijk}$ is a bad patch. Testing several patches per cell resolves the ambiguity.

\paragraph{Objective.} After spending at most budget $B$, the method accepts a set of cells $\mathcal{A}$ and, for each, a patch $k^\star_{ij}$. Accepting a true edge repairs a fraction $q_{ijk^\star}$ of category $i$'s failures; accepting a false edge costs a regression $c_{\mathrm{fp}}$, reflecting that unnecessary changes perturb behaviour on tasks that used to succeed \citep{vasudev2026intervention}. We measure
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G(\mathcal{A}) = {}&\sum_i f_i\Big(1-\!\!\prod_{j\in\mathcal{A}_i^+}\!\!(1-q_{ijk^\star_{ij}})\Big)\\
&- c_{\mathrm{fp}}\,\big|\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}:e_{ij}=0\}\big|,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_i^+=\{j:(i,j)\in\mathcal{A},\,e_{ij}=1\}$. This is the net failure mass repaired, and report it as a fraction of the oracle value obtained by accepting exactly the true edges with their best patches.

\section{\carve{}}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Observation model}
For a trajectory labelled $i$ and patch $a_{ijk}$, the probability that a full replay succeeds is
\begin{equation}
p_{ijk} = \begin{cases} \lambda\, q_{ijk} + (1-\lambda)\, b & e_{ij}=1,\\ b & e_{ij}=0,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the spurious recovery rate (stochastic re-generation sometimes succeeds regardless of the patch) and $\lambda$ is the accuracy of the category labels used to sample trajectories; a mislabelled trajectory is treated as unaffected by the patch. A single-step check returns $z=1$ with probability $s\,p+\epsilon\,(1-p)$, where $s$ and $\epsilon$ are the sensitivity and false-positive rate of the check relative to the full outcome. All four nuisance parameters can be estimated from a small calibration set on which both fidelities and a human audit are run.

\subsection{Prior from LLM attributions}
Let $n_{ij}$ be the number of failures in category $i$ that the analyst attributed to component $j$, and $\hat r$ the analyst's attribution accuracy estimated on a small audited sample. With $d$ the expected number of causal components per category and $\rho=d/J$ the base edge density, we set
\begin{equation}
\pi_{ij} = (1-\hat r)\,\rho + \hat r\,\min\!\big(1,\ d\, n_{ij}/n_i\big),
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
so that an unreliable analyst ($\hat r\to0$) yields the flat prior $\rho$ and a reliable one concentrates mass on the components it names. Patch quality has prior $q_{ijk}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_q,\beta_q)$.

\subsection{Posterior}
Cells are independent given the data. For each cell we keep the posterior log-odds of $e_{ij}$ and, for each patch, a posterior over $q_{ijk}$ on a fixed grid of $G=101$ points. With $\mathcal{D}_{ijk}$ the outcomes observed for patch $k$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{P(e_{ij}{=}1\mid\mathcal{D})}{P(e_{ij}{=}0\mid\mathcal{D})} ={}& \log\frac{\pi_{ij}}{1-\pi_{ij}}\\
&+ \sum_k \log\frac{\int P(\mathcal{D}_{ijk}\mid q)\,dP(q)}{P(\mathcal{D}_{ijk}\mid e_{ij}{=}0)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Every update multiplies one grid vector by a likelihood vector, so updates and look-aheads are vectorised dot products.

\subsection{Selecting interventions}
The value of the current decision for cell $(i,j)$, with $P_{ij}$ its edge posterior and $\hat Q_{ij}=\max_k\mathbb{E}[q_{ijk}\mid\mathcal{D}]$, is
\begin{equation}
V_{ij} = \max\big(P_{ij}\,f_i\,\hat Q_{ij} - (1-P_{ij})\,c_{\mathrm{fp}},\ 0\big),
\end{equation}
the expected contribution of the cell to Eq.~\ref{eq:gain} under the Bayes decision (accept iff the first term is positive). For an action $a=(i,j,k,\ell)$ repeated $n$ times, let $V'_{ij}(s)$ be the value after observing $s$ successes. The $n$-step knowledge gradient is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KG}_n(a) = \sum_{s=0}^{n} P(s\mid\mathcal{D},a)\,V'_{ij}(s) - V_{ij},
\end{equation}
and \carve{} runs the action maximising
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(a) = \max_{n\in\{1,2,4,8\}} \frac{\mathrm{KG}_n(a)}{n\,c_\ell}.
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Looking ahead several observations matters because a single binary outcome often cannot flip a decision, in which case $\mathrm{KG}_1=0$ even for a valuable cell; maximising over $n$ is the standard remedy \citep{frazier2008kg}. Because cells are independent, only the observed cell's scores change after each outcome, so selection costs $O(KG)$ per step after an initial pass over all cells.

\paragraph{Decisions and stopping.} At any point the method accepts the cells whose current value is positive and applies their highest-posterior patch. It stops early when no action has a score above a small threshold, i.e.\ when no remaining experiment is expected to be worth its cost.

\section{When Do Priors and Cheap Checks Help?}
\label{sec:analysis}

We analyse a single cell with fixed success probabilities $p_1$ (true edge, given patch) and $p_0=b$ under a sequential rule that stops when the posterior log-odds leaves $[-A,A]$, $A=\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}$. Let $D_1=\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)$ and $D_0=\mathrm{KL}(p_0\Vert p_1)$ be the expected log-likelihood-ratio drift per observation under each hypothesis. Proofs, which follow Wald's approximations \citep{wald1945sequential}, are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\begin{proposition}[Value of the LLM prior]
\label{prop:prior}
Neglecting overshoot and terminations at the wrong boundary, a cell with initial log-odds $\ell$ needs $N_1(\ell)=(A-\ell)/D_1$ observations in expectation if it is a true edge and $N_0(\ell)=(A+\ell)/D_0$ if not. Replacing a flat prior $\ell_\rho$ by LLM-derived log-odds $\ell_{ij}$ saves, in expectation over cells,
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \frac{\rho\,\mathbb{E}[\delta\ell\mid e{=}1]}{D_1} - \frac{(1-\rho)\,\mathbb{E}[\delta\ell\mid e{=}0]}{D_0},
\end{equation}
with $\delta\ell=\ell_{ij}-\ell_\rho$,
observations per cell, which is positive exactly when the prior raises true edges more, relative to their base rate and drift, than it raises false ones.
\end{proposition}

Under Eq.~\ref{eq:prior}, $\ell_{ij}\to\ell_\rho$ as $\hat r\to0$, so $\Delta\to0$: a useless analyst costs nothing beyond the flat prior, whereas trusting its labels outright (LLM-only) makes permanent errors. A miscalibrated analyst can make $\Delta$ negative, but only by an amount linear in the log-odds shift, after which the evidence takes over.

\begin{proposition}[Cheap checks]
\label{prop:fidelity}
Let $D^S_e$ denote the drifts of single-step outcomes, $z\sim\mathrm{Bern}(s\,p_e+\epsilon(1-p_e))$. Under the same approximations, single-step checks reach the stopping boundary at lower expected cost than full replays if and only if
\begin{equation}
\frac{c_S}{c_F} < \frac{D^S_e}{D_e},
\end{equation}
where $e$ is the true state of the cell.
\end{proposition}

With our default parameters ($p_1=0.54$, $b=0.05$, $s=0.85$, $\epsilon=0.15$) the critical ratio is $0.31$ for true edges and $0.44$ for false ones; with a weak check ($s=0.65$, $\epsilon=0.35$) it falls to $0.05$--$0.08$. Section~\ref{sec:res-fid} tests both predictions.

\section{Controlled Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Real agents do not reveal which component truly caused a failure, so we first evaluate in a testbed where the causal graph is known and the method's model is deliberately misspecified.

\subsection{Setup}
\paragraph{Testbed.} Each world has $I=12$ categories with Zipfian frequencies, $J=20$ components and $K=3$ patches per cell. Every category has one or two true components and one \emph{decoy} component that wrong attributions prefer, mimicking symptom-level mislabelling. Patch quality is bimodal: with probability 0.3 a patch is bad ($q\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,9)$), otherwise $q\sim\mathrm{Beta}(6,3)$. A trajectory sampled for category $i$ is mislabelled with probability $1-\lambda=0.2$, in which case it comes from another category (weighted by frequency) and is repaired only if \emph{its own} edge to $j$ exists. This differs from the model's flat treatment of mislabelled traces. The analyst attributes 20 failures per category with accuracy $r=0.5$; wrong attributions go to the decoy with probability 0.6. The analyst's accuracy is estimated from an audit of 20 attributions. Costs are $c_F=1$, $c_S=0.1$, $c_{\mathrm{fp}}=0.02$, and $s=0.85$, $\epsilon=0.15$, $b=0.05$. Full details are in Appendix~\ref{app:sim}.

\paragraph{Baselines.} \emph{LLM-only} accepts, for every category, the most-attributed component and any component holding at least 40\% of the attributions, with the first patch; this corresponds to acting on an induced taxonomy without verification, as in AdaMAST-style pipelines. \emph{Replay-each} tests attributed hypotheses in order of attribution count with three full replays of the first patch and accepts a hypothesis if at least two succeed, mirroring do-then-verify methods such as DoVer; untested hypotheses fall back to LLM-only. \emph{Uncertainty sampling} uses \carve{}'s posterior and decision rule but replays the cell with the largest frequency-weighted uncertainty $f_i\min(P_{ij},1-P_{ij})$ at full fidelity, cycling through patches. It isolates the contribution of the selection rule.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Net gain (Eq.~\ref{eq:gain}) as a fraction of the oracle, and edge F1. We report means and standard errors over 50 worlds for the main experiment and 30 for sweeps.

Figure~\ref{fig:budget} and Table~\ref{tab:main} show that \carve{} dominates every baseline at every budget. With 20 units it recovers 67\% of the oracle gain, against 46\% for uncertainty sampling. It passes 80\% of the oracle between 50 and 75 units; uncertainty sampling needs about 200. LLM-only is stuck at 30\% because it accepts decoys and bad first patches. Replay-each is initially \emph{worse} than not verifying at all: three replays of a single patch reject many true edges whose first patch is bad or whose sampled traces were mislabelled, and its fixed per-hypothesis cost exhausts small budgets on frequent but already-certain hypotheses.

\paragraph{Ablations.} Each component of \carve{} contributes (Table~\ref{tab:main}, lower block). Removing single-step checks costs 15--18 points at small budgets. Restricting the look-ahead to one observation helps early but plateaus at 73\%, because many cells cannot be flipped by a single outcome and are never revisited. A flat prior costs 32 points at budget 20, and the gap closes only at large budgets. With one patch per cell, the method plateaus at 69\%: it cannot distinguish a wrong component from a bad patch, so it rejects true edges for good. The label-noise term makes no measurable difference here (0.902 vs.\ 0.922 at budget 200, overlapping within two standard errors), which we attribute to the generator's mislabelled traces sometimes being repairable, unlike in the model; we keep the term because it costs nothing and matters when labels are worse.

Figure~\ref{fig:prior} varies the analyst's accuracy $r$. As Proposition~\ref{prop:prior} predicts, \carve{} improves monotonically with $r$ and never falls below the flat-prior variant: at $r=0.1$ it scores $0.51\pm0.02$ against $0.48\pm0.03$, because the calibrated prior collapses to the flat one. Replay-each improves with $r$ but stays below the flat-prior variant of \carve{} throughout.

Finally we embed each method in a loop. The agent starts at 40\% success. In each round the analyst attributes 240 new failures, sampled in proportion to the current failure distribution; the method spends 40 units; accepted patches are applied, which repairs failures (true edges) or adds a regression of $c_{\mathrm{fp}}$ times the current failure mass (false edges); and the failure distribution is recomputed, so priorities shift towards categories that remain. Posteriors carry over between rounds.

Figure~\ref{fig:rsi} shows that \carve{} reaches 80\% success after one round and 85\% after two, close to its plateau of 86\% (the oracle ceiling is 91.5\%). Uncertainty sampling reaches 84.5\% only after six rounds, i.e.\ with three times the cumulative budget, and the full-replay variant of \carve{} plateaus at 81\%. LLM-only and Replay-each plateau at 61\% and 66\%: once their accepted false edges and rejected true edges are fixed in place, new attributions do not correct them.

\section{Real-Agent Study}
\label{sec:real}

\paragraph{Environment.} ShopDesk is a customer-service environment with a seeded database of customers, orders, products and policies, six deterministic tools and exactly checkable answers (refund amounts, order counts, spend totals). Determinism makes replay from any step exact: recorded tool results are re-used and only the agent's generation is re-sampled. The agent is a ReAct-style tool-use loop \citep{yao2023react} assembled from 11 named components (four system-prompt sections, six tool descriptions and the step limit), and every step records which components shaped it.

\paragraph{Ground truth by fault injection.} Following the fault-injection idea of \citet{zhang2026agentracer}, each training task runs under exactly one of five injected faults or none: a tool description that misstates price units, a list tool whose description omits pagination, a format instruction that rounds to whole dollars, a planning instruction that hard-codes the wrong refund policy, and a step limit that is too small. The responsible component of every failure is therefore known. After the analyst attributes the failures, induces a taxonomy in TnT-LLM style \citep{wan2024tntllm} and labels each failure, a category--component edge is defined as true when at least 30\% of the category's failures come from that component's fault.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Every method receives the same attributions, taxonomy and token budget. Full replays resume from the attributed step; single-step checks regenerate that step and use an LLM judge \citep{zheng2023judge}. Patches are generated lazily by the analyst, three per cell, and their tokens are charged to the method. Cost units are calibrated from measured tokens. We report edge precision and recall, and the held-out success rate after applying each method's accepted patches.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the analyst LLM is for.} Our results suggest a change of role for LLM attribution in self-improvement loops: from judge to prior. When attributions are good, they focus the budget; when they are bad, a calibrated prior falls back to an uninformed one, whereas acting on them directly causes regressions that later rounds do not undo.

\paragraph{Why patches must be modelled.} The single largest failure mode of fixed-replay verification in our experiments is rejecting a true edge because its first patch was bad. Treating patch quality as a separate latent variable, and generating more than one patch, is cheap and removes this failure mode.

\paragraph{Beyond categories and components.} The graph formulation does not depend on how categories or components are defined. Categories could come from a fixed taxonomy such as MAST, from AdaMAST, or from clustering; components could be finer (individual sentences of a prompt) or coarser (whole agents in a multi-agent system). The replay primitive can be any of the intervention methods in \S2.

\section{Conclusion}
We framed the verification step of failure-driven agent self-improvement as budgeted learning of a causal category--component graph, and proposed \carve{}, which treats LLM attributions as a calibrated prior, separates causes from patch quality, and allocates replays across cells, patches and fidelities by expected value per unit cost. In controlled experiments it reaches the same repaired failure mass as the strongest baseline with about a third of the budget and degrades gracefully when attributions are poor. The real-agent evaluation is the main open item.

\section*{Limitations}
The quantitative results in this draft come from a simulated testbed. Although the generator is deliberately richer than the model (systematic decoy attributions, bimodal patch quality, mislabelled traces that can still be repaired), real agents may violate the model in ways we did not simulate, most notably through interactions between components, where two changes are needed together, and through patches that help one category while hurting another beyond a constant regression cost. Our cell-independence assumption ignores both. The method also assumes that failures can be replayed from the attributed step; this holds for deterministic tools and sandboxes but not for live web environments. The single-step check relies on an LLM judge whose quality must be calibrated, and Table~\ref{tab:misspec} shows that this calibration matters. Finally, the propositions use Wald's approximations and ignore overshoot and errors at the boundary; they are guides to when the method helps, not guarantees.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Automated self-improvement changes an agent's behaviour without a human reviewing each change. \carve{} makes these changes more reliable by requiring interventional evidence before acceptance, but accepted patches should still be reviewed before deployment in settings where errors carry real costs. Our environment uses synthetic data only.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:prior}.} Let $L_t=\ell+\sum_{u\le t}\log\frac{P(o_u\mid e=1)}{P(o_u\mid e=0)}$ be the posterior log-odds after $t$ observations. Under $e=1$, the increments are i.i.d.\ with mean $D_1=\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)>0$. The rule stops at $N=\inf\{t: L_t\notin(-A,A)\}$. By Wald's identity, $\mathbb{E}_1[L_N-\ell]=D_1\,\mathbb{E}_1[N]$. Neglecting overshoot and the probability (at most of order $\delta$) of exiting at $-A$, $L_N=A$, hence $\mathbb{E}_1[N]=(A-\ell)/D_1$. Symmetrically, under $e=0$ the drift is $-D_0$ and $\mathbb{E}_0[N]=(A+\ell)/D_0$. Taking the difference between $\ell_\rho$ and $\ell_{ij}$ and averaging over cells with $P(e{=}1)=\rho$ gives $\Delta$. When the patch quality $q$ is unknown, $p_1$ is replaced by its posterior predictive, which changes $D_1$ but not the form of the result.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:fidelity}.} By the same argument a single-step test needs $(A\mp\ell)/D^S_e$ observations at cost $c_S$ each, and a full-replay test needs $(A\mp\ell)/D_e$ at cost $c_F$. The single-step test is cheaper iff $c_S/D^S_e<c_F/D_e$. Since the single-step outcome is a garbling of the full outcome, the data-processing inequality gives $D^S_e\le D_e$, so the critical ratio is at most one.

\section{Simulation Details}
\label{app:sim}

Category frequencies are $f_i\propto 1/i$. For each category the number of true components is uniform on $\{1,2\}$ and the decoy is a further distinct component. The audit estimate $\hat r$ is a binomial proportion from 20 audited attributions. The prior uses $d=1.5$. The model's grid has 101 points; look-ahead horizons are $\{1,2,4,8\}$; the stopping threshold is $10^{-6}$. Replay-each uses three replays and accepts with at least two successes. Uncertainty sampling switches to an untried patch once the current patch has three full observations. In the loop experiment, 240 attributions per round are allocated to categories by a multinomial draw with the current failure distribution. All randomness is seeded; the code reproduces every number in this paper with \texttt{python -m sim.experiments all}.
