\begin{abstract}
Self-improving agent loops change an agent's harness (its prompts, tool descriptions and control code) and keep a change only when verification evidence says it helps: a replay of the failure, an LLM judge's verdict, reruns of solved tasks, or a full re-evaluation. We ask which evidence predicts a change's gain when it is deployed on independent tasks, and whether the evidence is worth its cost, which we account for with $\mathrm{Net}(N)$, the deployment gain over $N$ future episodes net of running, verification and change costs. In one round of attribution-driven repair of a DeepSeek-V4-Flash agent on $\tau^2$-bench, applying the attributed patches without verification raised held-out success from 75.8\% to 87.5\% (retail) and from 68.3\% to 75.0\% (airline), and no set chosen by up to ten verification policies did better ($-4.3$ to $+0.3$ points; 90\% intervals reach $+6$ and $+9$). Replay evidence ranked the deployed sets poorly, but the differences it predicted were below the noise of our deployment measurements, so this test had little power. Verification cost at most ten episodes, negligible once a success is worth a few episodes of compute, so its worth rests on whether it selects better changes. To test that with adequate power, on natural failures and with code-level changes, we build on RRSI, a regularised self-improvement loop for agent harnesses, replace its selection step with each kind of evidence at equal budget, and deploy every candidate on held-out tasks. We report this registered study's design and power analysis.\todo{Add the results of Study 2.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A growing family of systems improves LLM agents from their own failures. The agent runs, an analyst model reads the failed trajectories, a proposer changes the agent's \emph{harness}, the prompts, tool descriptions and code around a frozen model, and the loop keeps the change or discards it \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa,chen2026harnessfix,zhang2026ace,rrsi2026}. The check that decides is where these systems differ most: some replay the failure with the change applied \citep{ma2025dover,lin2026reflect,shah2026car}, some ask an LLM judge, some re-run tasks that used to pass \citep{chen2026harnessfix}, and RRSI re-evaluates every candidate twice on its whole training set \citep{rrsi2026}. What every check is meant to estimate is a change's \emph{deployment gain}, how much it raises success on tasks the loop has not seen, and that gain can differ from what the loop observes on its own tasks: RRSI reports in-distribution gains that shrink out of distribution \citep{rrsi2026}.

This paper asks two questions about verification evidence. \emph{Which evidence predicts a change's deployment gain?} And \emph{is the evidence worth what it costs?} Answering the first needs ground truth that verification does not provide: deploying the changes, from the first step, on independent tasks. Answering the second needs an account of what verification costs and what the change it selects is worth over a deployment horizon, which we write as $\mathrm{Net}(N)$ (\S\ref{sec:problem}). We price every piece of evidence in agent episodes and answer both questions in two studies.

\textbf{Study 1} (\S\ref{sec:study1}, completed) is one round of attribution-driven repair of a DeepSeek-V4-Flash agent on the retail and airline domains of $\tau^2$-bench \citep{barres2025tau2}, with patches that rewrite policy sections, tool descriptions and the step limit. We record intervention banks of targeted replays, null replays, judge checks and regression runs for 108 patches per domain, let eleven verification policies choose patch sets from them, and deploy the sets on held-out tasks. Four findings stand out. Applying the attributed patches without verification raised held-out success by 11.7 (retail) and 6.7 (airline) points, and no verified set did better. Replay evidence ranked the deployed sets poorly, but the differences it predicted between sets were below the noise of our deployment measurements. No evidence was cheap: judge checks cost 22--72\% of a replay and were at best about as informative per token, and a fifth of unpatched replays succeed anyway. Finally, verification cost at most ten episodes, whereas over a thousand deployed episodes, with a success worth ten episodes of compute, the uncertainty in its net worth spans hundreds of episodes, so its worth turns on its quality, not its price.

\textbf{Study 2} (\S\ref{sec:study2}, registered) tests evidence quality with the power and the modification space Study 1 lacked. We build on RRSI's open-source loop \citep{rrsi2026}, whose proposer edits harness code over many rounds of natural failures, port it to DeepSeek models and $\tau^2$-bench, make targeted replays work for code-level changes, and replace RRSI's selection step with each kind of evidence at equal budget. Every candidate and incumbent is deployed on held-out tasks, and the study's endpoints, decision rule, analysis code and power analysis were fixed before any held-out episode was run. \todo{Summarise the Study 2 results.}

Our contributions are a deployment-grounded evaluation of verification evidence with an explicit cost account; the measurements of Study 1; and the RRSI-based harness, replay mechanism and registered design of Study 2, which we release together with the environments and banks. The multi-fidelity allocation method we started from, which assumed that judge checks are cheap, is reported as a design study in Appendix~\ref{app:carve}.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Self-improving agents and how they verify.}
Reflexion, Self-Refine and ExpeL turn feedback into verbal lessons \citep{shinn2023reflexion,madaan2023selfrefine,zhao2024expel}; ProTeGi, OPRO, TextGrad and DSPy rewrite prompts \citep{pryzant2023protegi,yang2024opro,yuksekgonul2025textgrad,khattab2024dspy}; ADAS, the Darwin G\"odel Machine and GEPA search over designs \citep{hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa}; ACE evolves a context playbook through incremental updates \citep{zhang2026ace}. HarnessFix repairs harness code and promotes a bundle of repairs only if it solves more validation tasks while breaking at most two \citep{chen2026harnessfix}; Bayesian-Agent applies threshold rules to posteriors over skills \citep{wu2026bayesianagent}. RRSI regularises harness self-improvement: it bounds and anneals the number of edits per candidate, screens benchmark-specific edits with a critic, prunes stale edits, and selects candidates by full re-evaluation against a calibrated noise floor \citep{rrsi2026}. These systems report the success of the final agent; we evaluate the evidence behind each decision, and Study 2 uses RRSI's code as its loop.

\paragraph{Failure attribution.}
Who\&When and its extension show that locating the responsible agent and step is far from solved \citep{zhang2025whichagent,liu2026whowhenpro}. AgenTracer trains an attribution model on counterfactual replays and injected faults \citep{zhang2026agentracer}; AgentDebug and ErrorProbe pair taxonomies or dependency tracing with corrective feedback \citep{zhu2025agentdebug,li2026errorprobe}; MAST and AdaMAST give attribution a vocabulary \citep{cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast}.

\paragraph{Verification by intervention.}
DoVer validates hypotheses by editing a message or plan and re-running \citep{ma2025dover}; REFLECT tests patches by targeted replay \citep{lin2026reflect}; CausalFlow and Causal Agent Replay score step interventions causally \citep{bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}; AgentDebugX gates reruns by policy \citep{zhu2026agentdebugx}. None of them measures whether its evidence predicts deployment or what the evidence costs. Program repair has long warned that a patch passing the failing test may not fix the fault \citep{smith2015overfitting}.

\section{Verification Evidence and Deployment Gain}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Changes and their deployment gain.} A loop holds an incumbent harness $h$ and proposes a change $c$: a rewritten prompt section or tool description, a code edit, or a set of these. Its \emph{deployment gain} $\Delta p(c)$ is the difference in success rate between $h$ with $c$ and $h$ alone, measured from the first step on tasks independent of everything the loop saw. A verification rule spends $C_{\mathrm{verify}}$ episodes on evidence, forms an estimate $\widehat{\Delta}$ of the change's effect and accepts the change if the estimate clears a threshold.

\paragraph{Evidence.} We study the evidence that self-improvement loops use:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \emph{None}: accept the proposed change.
\item \emph{LLM judge}: a model reads the change and the failures it targets and predicts its effect, at a cost in tokens but no episodes.
\item \emph{Single-step check}: regenerate the failing step with the change and ask a judge whether the error is gone.
\item \emph{Targeted replay}: resume a failed trajectory from the failing step with the change; it succeeds with probability $b+(1-b)q$, where $q$ is the share of such failures the change repairs.
\item \emph{Null replay}: the same without the change, which measures the \emph{spurious recovery} rate $b$; without it, replay success cannot be interpreted.
\item \emph{Regression run}: a previously solved task, from the first step, with the change.
\item \emph{Sample} or \emph{full evaluation}: candidate and incumbent on a random sample of, or all, training tasks.
\end{itemize}
Replays and checks see only the failures a change targets; only regression runs and evaluations see harm to tasks that already succeed.

\paragraph{Is the evidence worth its cost?} Compare a rule $E$ with a reference rule, here applying changes without verification. Suppose $E$'s choices change the deployed agent's success by $\Delta p$ and its running cost by $\Delta c_{\mathrm{run}}$ per episode, that $E$ spends $C_{\mathrm{verify}}$ episodes, and that it changes the one-off cost of reviewing and maintaining the applied changes by $\Delta C_{\mathrm{change}}$. Over the next $N$ deployed episodes $E$ is worth
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Net}(N)=N\,(v\,\Delta p-\Delta c_{\mathrm{run}})-C_{\mathrm{verify}}-\Delta C_{\mathrm{change}},
\label{eq:net}
\end{equation}
with every cost in episodes of the unmodified agent's running cost and $v$ the value of one success in the same unit. The one-off costs do not grow with $N$, so once $Nv$ is large compared with $C_{\mathrm{verify}}$ the sign of $\mathrm{Net}(N)$ is the sign of $v\Delta p-\Delta c_{\mathrm{run}}$: evidence pays if the changes it selects are better, or cheaper to run. Its worth then rests on its \emph{quality}, which we measure in two ways: how well its estimates correlate with, and are calibrated against, measured deployment gains, and the \emph{decision value} of a rule that uses it, the mean deployment gain of the changes it accepts.

\paragraph{An accounting decomposition.} For single-round repair, Appendix~\ref{app:framework} splits a patch's net effect into repair, spurious recovery and regressions, and bounds the value of perfect verification by three terms: avoiding harm, choosing among candidate patches and discovering missed fixes. It shows where verification could add value; it is not a decision rule, because its decisive input, the harm of each patch to solved tasks, is what our regression runs could not measure.

\section{Study 1: One Round of Attribution-Driven Repair}
\label{sec:study1}

\paragraph{Agent and environments.} The agent is DeepSeek-V4-Flash, with the provider's default sampling, on the retail and airline domains of $\tau^2$-bench \citep{yao2024taubench,barres2025tau2}, which have an LLM user simulator and database-state rewards. Attribution, taxonomy induction \citep{wan2024tntllm}, patch generation and judging use DeepSeek-V4-Pro. The patchable components are the sections of the domain policy, every tool description and the step limit: 24 components on retail and 21 on airline. Each training episode runs clean or with one injected fault written as a plausible authoring mistake (retail: a policy section allowing gift-card refunds, a cancellation rule without the reason check, a product-tool description that confuses item and product ids, a tight step limit; airline: a wrong bag allowance, a 7-day instead of 24-hour cancellation window, a missing basic-economy restriction, proactive compensation, a tight step limit). Clean episodes contribute natural failures, 22 of 137 on retail and 28 of 112 on airline. A third environment, ShopDesk, is described in Appendix~\ref{app:shopdesk}.

\paragraph{Patches and banks.} The analyst groups failures into categories and blames components. For 36 cells (category, component) per domain, the blamed cells in order of blame plus random unblamed ones, it writes three patches each. For every patch we record 20 targeted replays from the attributed failing step, each paired with a single-step check; for every category, 20 null replays; and for every patch, 4 regression runs on solved training tasks, extended to 20 for the 121 patches that any policy accepted. An unpatched baseline of 10 runs on each of the 111 regression tasks gives task-matched estimates of harm. Policies run offline on these \emph{intervention banks}, with every experiment priced in measured tokens relative to a full replay.

\paragraph{Policies and deployment.} Eleven policies spend a budget of episodes from the banks and output a patch set (Appendix~\ref{app:policies}). \emph{Apply attributed} uses no evidence: for each category it applies the first patch of the most-blamed component and of any component with at least 40\% of the blame. \emph{Replay-each} accepts a hypothesis on two successes in three replays, as do-then-verify methods do \citep{ma2025dover}; two HarnessFix-style gates require regression runs to break nothing, per patch or on the whole bundle \citep{chen2026harnessfix}; uncertainty sampling runs with and without cheap checks; four variants of \carve{}, the multi-fidelity design we started from, and a net-effect allocation that charges regressions complete the list. We apply each policy's budget-10 set, from five seeds and reduced to one patch per component, to unseen tasks: 40 retail tasks $\times$ 3 episodes (38 clean, 82 with an injected fault) and 20 airline tasks $\times$ 3 (25 clean), for five policies on airline. Retail was the development domain for every method.

\subsection{Deployment gains}

Table~\ref{tab:deploy} reports absolute held-out success. Applying the attributed patches without verification raised success from 75.8\% to 87.5\% on retail ($+11.7$ points, 90\% interval $[4.5,18.8]$) and from 68.3\% to 75.0\% on airline ($+6.7$, $[-3.3,16.6]$). No verified set did better: their differences from the unverified set range from $-4.3$ to $+0.3$ points on retail and from $-4.0$ to $-0.3$ on airline. This is not an equivalence result. The intervals reach $+6.0$ points on retail and $+8.5$ on airline; with a margin of 5 points, chosen after the fact, applying the attributed patches is non-inferior to 9 of the 10 verified sets on retail (all but \carve{} with replays only) and to the two HarnessFix-style sets on airline. Most of retail's gain comes from fault-injected episodes: on its 38 clean episodes the unverified set gains $+5.3$ points $[-5.1,15.6]$, and on 158 clean episodes, 120 of them new, $+4$ $[-3,10]$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ablate}). The policy seed adds no detectable variance: the variance of the seed means of the verified sets' differences from Apply attributed is $0.0007$, against $0.0019$ expected from run-to-run noise alone.

\subsection{Does the evidence predict deployment?}
\label{sec:transfer}

Replays measure repair from the failing step, whereas deployed patches act from the first step and on every task. For every distinct deployed set we predict its gain over the unpatched agent from its patches' replays as $\hat F\sum_i f_i\big[1-\prod_k(1-\hat q_k)\big]$, with $\hat F$ the held-out failure rate, $f_i$ the category shares and $\hat q_k=(p_k-b_i)/(1-b_i)$ the replay success $p_k$ net of the category's spurious recovery $b_i$. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} compares the predictions with the observed gains. They correlate weakly, at $r=0.23$ over 47 distinct retail sets and $-0.49$ over 14 airline sets; predictions from the judge checks, calibrated to replay success, do no better ($r=0.14$ and $-0.05$). In level, replays predict airline's gains (6.0 points on average against 6.1 observed) but not retail's (5.6 against 9.1).

The ranking test has little power, for a reason that matters beyond this study. Replay predictions differ between sets by a standard deviation of 1.0 point on retail and 1.7 on airline, whereas a set's held-out success measured on 120 (60) episodes has a run-to-run standard error of 2.6 (3.8) points; the observed gains spread by 2.8 (3.7), no more than that noise. A perfect predictor of differences this small would be indistinguishable from a useless one. Single patches show the same problem: leaving the step-limit patch out of the attributed set lowers retail success by 3.3 points (s.e.\ 4.2) where its replays predict 4.9, and leaving out each policy patch lowers it by 0.0 to 3.3 points where replays predict 0.0 to 0.5, each within about one standard error (Appendix~\ref{app:ablate}). The shortfall in retail's predicted level is therefore spread over the policy patches rather than located in one, and no single patch's deployed effect is resolved. Testing whether evidence predicts deployment needs candidates that differ by more than the deployment noise and more precise deployment measurements; Study 2 is designed for both.

\subsection{What the evidence costs}

Table~\ref{tab:measured} collects the measurements. \emph{Judge checks are not cheap.} A single-step check costs 22--72\% of a full replay in tokens: regenerating the failing step alone costs 12--15\%, and the cheapest judge prompts add 10--15\% more (Appendix~\ref{app:judges}, where three cheaper judges are tested). Whether a check carries more information per token than a replay depends on how judge accuracy is defined: against the single replay paired with each check, every judge's efficiency ratio is below 1 (0.16--0.62), but against a patch's replay success rate, which is the quantity a replay estimates, the bank judge's efficiency ratio is 0.88 $[0.46,1.53]$ on retail and 1.25 $[0.19,3.95]$ on airline. The checks are therefore not the cheap, informative evidence multi-fidelity designs assume, and not clearly worse than replays either. \emph{Replays need controls}: without any patch, 20--23\% of replays from the failing step succeed anyway, and the rate ranges from 0 to 0.5 across categories. \emph{Harm is the least measurable quantity}: the mean effect of a patch on previously solved tasks is $-1.0$ points on retail and $-0.6$ on airline, with 90\% intervals of about $\pm2.5$, and patches differ by a spread of 2--3 points; detecting a 3-point regression against a 15\% base rate with 90\% power takes about 1{,}200 runs per patch (Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}).

\subsection{Is verification worth its cost?}

Table~\ref{tab:netn} evaluates Eq.~\ref{eq:net} for every verified set against applying the attributed patches. The verification bill is at most 10 episodes, about two cents of $\tau^2$ compute at our measured cost, while at $N=1000$ the 90\% intervals of $\mathrm{Net}(N)$ span 45--180 episodes at $v=1$ and 460--1{,}760 at $v=10$. The bill is thus negligible unless a success is worth less than an episode of compute, and the sign of $\mathrm{Net}(N)$ is decided by $\Delta p$ and, at small $v$, by the running cost. At $v=10$ the point estimate is negative for every set except \carve{} with replays only on retail and \carve{} and the net-effect method on airline, all with intervals spanning zero; only the bundle gate on retail is negative throughout. Verified sets apply fewer patches, which saves change cost, but even at one episode per patch the saving is at most 6 episodes. In this regime the question ``is verification worth its cost'' reduces to ``does it select better changes'', which Study 1 could not resolve.

\paragraph{Policies on the banks and priors.} The banks give the same answer without new runs (Appendix~\ref{app:policies}). Under the task-level harm model, applying the attributed patches is worth 1.43 (retail) and 0.66 (airline) of the repair-only oracle, and the verifying policies 0.39--1.56 at budget 40, beating it in 19--57\% of posterior draws; scored on bank halves that share no trajectory with the policies' evidence, they beat it in 22--47\%. Policies that use the analyst's blame as an edge prior used an analyst accuracy of 0.7; with the audited 0.41 and 0.29, or half of these, every such policy is worth less than with 0.7, on both domains (Table~\ref{tab:priors}).

\section{Study 2: Verification Evidence Inside RRSI}
\label{sec:study2}

Study 1 patches only text, runs one round, draws most failures from injected faults, and compares sets whose predicted differences are below its deployment noise. Study 2 removes these limitations by building on RRSI \citep{rrsi2026}, whose proposer edits harness code over many rounds of natural failures, and whose selection step, every candidate evaluated twice on the whole training set, is the most expensive evidence on our list. We keep RRSI's loop and replace that step. The study is registered: its design, endpoints, decision rule, analysis code and power analysis were fixed before any held-out episode was run.

\paragraph{Loop.} We vendor RRSI's code (Apache 2.0) and change only its model backend, its cost accounting and one rule that now bounces an empty proposal like an aborted one (Appendix~\ref{app:rrsi}). Analyst, proposer, critic and judge are DeepSeek-V4-Pro with thinking disabled; the agent and the user simulator are DeepSeek-V4-Flash at temperature 0, $\tau^2$'s standard setting; the starting harness is $\tau^2$'s reference LLM agent. Each round, the analyst digests failed trajectories, the proposer drafts $m=2$ candidates under an edit budget that anneals from 4 to 1, a critic rejects edits that hard-code task data or touch the grader, and RRSI selects among the candidates that pass a smoke test. Changes may touch prompts, control flow, gates before state-changing calls, the rendering of tool results, context management, extra model calls, harness-side tools and skill files; only memory across episodes is forbidden, because it would make episodes dependent. Evolve tasks are $\tau^2$'s training split (74 retail, 30 airline tasks) and held-out tasks its test split (40 and 20); the loop runs $T=20$ rounds per domain, and RRSI's noise band is calibrated from three evaluations of the base harness.

\paragraph{Replays under code changes.} A targeted replay re-executes the recorded prefix of a failed trajectory through the \emph{new} harness's tool hooks, and the first step at which the new harness would block a recorded call or render a different observation becomes the live start. Prompt changes thus take effect at the failing step, as in Study 1, while control-flow changes move the start earlier, which makes their replays more expensive; how often this happens, by kind of change, is a secondary outcome. With the base harness, 12 of 12 replays of a full recorded prefix reproduced the recorded reward without a model call.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence says so far.} In one round of repair, applying the attributed patches without verification did as well on held-out tasks as any verification policy with ten episodes, replay evidence ranked deployed sets poorly, and judge checks were neither cheap nor clearly more informative than replays. These are statements about one agent, one analyst and two domains, and the deployment comparison cannot exclude gains from verification of 6 to 9 points.

\paragraph{Price is not the binding constraint; quality is.} Ten episodes of $\tau^2$ cost us about two cents. Once a successful task is worth more than a few episodes of compute, Eq.~\ref{eq:net} is dominated by $Nv\Delta p$, and the verification bill hardly matters. What matters is whether evidence selects better changes, and measuring that requires deployment measurements precise enough to separate the changes being compared. Study 1's were not; Study 2 is powered for it.

\paragraph{Implications for self-improvement loops.} Loops that report the final agent's held-out success \citep{chen2026harnessfix,zhang2026ace,rrsi2026} measure the outcome of all their decisions at once. Recording the held-out gain of accepted and rejected changes, together with the evidence behind each decision, would show which evidence earns its place. Replays need null replays, and regression evidence, the only kind that sees harm to solved tasks, is also the least measurable.

\section{Conclusion}
We asked which verification evidence predicts the deployment gain of agent repairs, and whether it is worth its cost. In one round of repair on $\tau^2$-bench, no verification beat applying the attributed patches, replay evidence ranked deployed sets poorly in an underpowered test, and verification cost little compared with what is at stake in the deployment gain. A registered study inside RRSI's code-level self-improvement loop tests evidence quality with the power this question needs.

\section*{Limitations}
Both studies use one model family, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash as agent and DeepSeek-V4-Pro as analyst, proposer and judge, and two customer-service domains of $\tau^2$-bench; Study 1 adds ShopDesk. Study 1's patches rewrite only policy sections, tool descriptions and a step limit; it runs a single round, mostly on injected faults (natural failures are 16--25\% of training failures), and deploys patch sets only at budget 10, on 120 (retail) and 60 (airline) held-out episodes, where most of retail's gain comes from fault-injected episodes. Its bank analyses rest on a harm model fitted to at most 20 regression runs per patch, and its edge priors use an analyst accuracy of 0.7 unless stated. Study 1's agent used the provider's default sampling and Study 2's uses temperature 0. Study 2's results are pending; its proposer is likely weaker than the model RRSI was developed with, so its candidates may stay closer to prompt edits; replays of code-level changes start live from the first step at which the new harness diverges, which can make them nearly as expensive as full episodes; and its held-out sets are $\tau^2$'s test splits of 40 and 20 tasks. Token prices for the models we used change; we report costs in episodes.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Self-improving agents change their own behaviour without a person reviewing each change. That no gain from verification was detected on our held-out tasks, and that no average harm to other tasks was resolved, does not show that verification is unnecessary; our measurements cannot exclude small harms per patch and should not be read as licence to skip review. On ShopDesk a single patch silently broke a quarter of tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:shopdesk}). Our environments use synthetic customer data only.

\section{An Accounting Framework for Single-Round Repair}
\label{app:framework}

\paragraph{Setting.} An agent is assembled from \emph{components} $j$ (prompt sections, tool descriptions, configuration). On a task distribution it fails with rate $F$; an LLM groups its failures into categories $i$ with shares $f_i$. For each \emph{cell} $(i,j)$ that the analyst blames, it proposes $K$ candidate patches $k$ that rewrite component $j$. Let $e_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether changing $j$ can repair category $i$ at all, and $q_k$ the fraction of category-$i$ failures that patch $k$ repairs if so. Separating $e$ from $q$ matters because a patch that fails to help may be aimed at the wrong component or merely be a bad patch. We call a cell with $e_{ij}=1$ an \emph{effective edge}. This is an interventional notion: some change to $j$ repairs failures of category $i$. It need not mean that $j$ was the original cause of those failures.

\paragraph{Net effect.} Applying patch $k$ of cell $(i,j)$ changes the success rate by
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k = F f_i e_{ij} q_k - (1-F)\rho_k - c_{\mathrm{a}} - c_{\mathrm{w}}(1-e_{ij}),
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_k$ is the patch's absolute excess failure rate on tasks that used to succeed (regressions, which may hit any category): such a task fails a re-run with probability $r_0$ unpatched and $r_0+\rho_k$ patched, so $\rho_k<0$ is possible; $c_{\mathrm a}\ge0$ is a cost charged for every applied change (review, maintenance) and $c_{\mathrm w}\ge0$ a penalty charged only for a change aimed at a wrong component (risk not captured by $\rho_k$). Costs are fractions of the failure mass $F$. The goal is to choose a set of patches, at most one per cell, that maximises $\sum_k\Delta_k$ on held-out tasks; effects of patches to different components are treated as additive, and held-out success of whole patch sets (Table~\ref{tab:deploy}) and a leave-one-out ablation (Appendix~\ref{app:ablate}) are the only checks. A targeted replay of a category-$i$ failure with patch $k$ succeeds with probability $b_i+(1-b_i)e_{ij}q_k$, a null replay with probability $b_i$, and a regression run fails with probability $r_0+\rho_k$.

\paragraph{Value of verification.} Before any evidence, the net effects $\Delta=(\Delta_k)$ of the candidate patches are uncertain, with a prior that reflects the analyst's accuracy and what is known about patch quality and regressions. The \emph{default} applies, in each cell, the patch with the largest $\mathbb E[\Delta_k]$ if that is positive. Verification observes outcomes $Y$ and then chooses; its value is $V(Y)=\mathbb E\big[\max_S\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k\mid Y]\big]-\max_S\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k]$, before the cost of the experiments. Write $x^+=\max(x,0)$ and $x^-=\max(-x,0)$.

\begin{proposition}[Value of verification]
\label{prop:vpi}
For any experiments $Y$, $V(Y)$ is at most the value of learning $\Delta$ exactly, which is the sum over cells of
\begin{equation}
V_{ij}=\mathbb E\big[\max_k\Delta_k^+\big]-\max_k\big(\mathbb E\Delta_k\big)^+ .
\end{equation}
With $k^\star$ the default patch of the cell,
\begin{equation}
V_{ij}\;\le\;\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[\max_k\Delta_k^+-\Delta_{k^\star}^+\big]}_{\text{choosing among patches}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[\Delta_{k^\star}^-\big]}_{\text{avoiding harm}},
\label{eq:vpi}
\end{equation}
with equality when the default applies $k^\star$, and $\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^-]\le(1-F)\,\mathbb E[\rho_{k^\star}^+]+c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}P(e_{ij}{=}0)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Edge-only verification]
\label{cor:edge}
Suppose a cell has a single candidate patch whose net effect depends only on $e_{ij}$: $\Delta=G$ if $e_{ij}=1$ and $\Delta=-H$ if $e_{ij}=0$, with $G,H\ge0$ known. With $\pi=P(e_{ij}{=}1)$, $V_{ij}=\min\big(\pi G,(1-\pi)H\big)$.
\end{corollary}

\emph{What survives deployment constraints.} Deployment may use a heuristic default $D$, applies at most one patch per component, which couples cells, combines repairs of one category as $1-\prod_k(1-q_k)$ (overstated by at most $f_i\sum_{k<l}q_kq_l$ when added), and cannot push the failure rate on solved tasks below zero ($-\sum_k\rho_k\le r_0$). Three statements survive. (i) The first sentence of the proposition holds for any set of admissible actions, so $V(Y)$ is at most the value of perfect information over deployable sets. (ii) Over a heuristic default that value is $V+G_D$, where $G_D=\max_S\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k]-\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in D}\Delta_k]\ge0$ is the \emph{decision-rule gap} that better use of the prior alone would close. (iii) The value of dropping harmful default patches is at most their expected harm, $\sum_{k\in D}\mathbb E[(1-F)\rho_k^++c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}(1-e_{ij})]$, because dropping a patch from an overlapping set loses at most its marginal repair. What does not survive is the separation over cells: with one patch per component, the choice and discovery terms have no closed form, and we compute them in sequence (harm, then choice on the default's components, then any component) with a greedy optimiser, which gives a lower bound on the constrained value of perfect information under a given harm model. The three sources of value are \emph{avoiding harm}, which needs regressions or change costs because replay success alone cannot create it; \emph{choosing among patches} of different quality; and \emph{discovery} of edges the analyst missed, the whole term $\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+]$ in cells the default leaves alone. The corollary fails when quality or regressions are uncertain given the edge ($\pi=1$ with $\Delta=+0.10$ or $-0.05$ equally likely gives $V=0.025$).

\begin{proposition}[Cost of evidence]
\label{prop:cost}
Under Wald's approximations, a sequential test that decides $e_{ij}$ from targeted replays with error $\delta$ needs on average about $\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\,/\,\mathrm{KL}\big(b+(1-b)q\,\Vert\, b\big)$ replays when the edge is real. Detecting a regression of size $\rho$ against a base rate $r_0$ with a one-sided level-$\alpha$ test and power $1-\beta$ needs about $(z_\alpha+z_\beta)^2 r_0(1-r_0)/\rho^2$ regression runs.
\end{proposition}

Two consequences follow. First, targeted replays are uninformative about the harm term, because they only see the target category: a patch can pass every replay and still break other tasks. Second, small regressions are expensive to see. With $r_0=0.1$, a regression of $\rho=0.25$ is detected with 90\% power by 20 runs, whereas $\rho=0.03$ needs about 850. Proposition~\ref{prop:vpi} is the standard value-of-information argument \citep{chaloner1995bayesian}; what is new is its decomposition for patches and its evaluation on measured banks.

Proposition~\ref{prop:vpi} names the quantities that could make verification pay, but it cannot decide whether to verify a new diagnosis: its most influential input, each patch's harm, takes hundreds of regression runs to measure (Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}), and its values move several-fold with the harm model.


\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:vpi}.} For any signal $Y$ and any action set, $\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)\mid Y]\le\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)\mid Y]$, so taking expectations gives $V(Y)\le\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)]-\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]$, the value of perfect information; this is the standard argument and needs no assumption on how $Y$ relates to $\Delta$. With $U(S)=\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k$ and at most one patch per cell, both maxima separate over cells: $\max_S U(S)=\sum_{ij}\max_k\Delta_k^+$ and $\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]=\sum_{ij}\max_k(\mathbb E\Delta_k)^+$, which gives $V_{ij}$; no independence between cells is needed. For Eq.~\ref{eq:vpi}, let $k^\star$ maximise $\mathbb E\Delta_k$. If $\mathbb E\Delta_{k^\star}\ge0$, then $V_{ij}=\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+]-\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^+]+\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^-]$, which is Eq.~\ref{eq:vpi} with equality. Otherwise $V_{ij}=\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+]=\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+-\Delta_{k^\star}^+]+\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^+]$, and $\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^+]<\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^-]$ because $\mathbb E\Delta_{k^\star}<0$. Finally, the repair term $Ff_ie_{ij}q_k$ is non-negative, so $\Delta_k^-\le(1-F)\rho_k^++c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}(1-e_{ij})$; take expectations.

\paragraph{Heuristic defaults and constraints.} For a default $D$, $\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)]-\mathbb E[U(D)]=\big(\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)]-\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]\big)+\big(\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]-\mathbb E[U(D)]\big)$, and the second bracket is $G_D\ge0$ by definition of the maximum. Restricting $S$ to deployable sets leaves the argument unchanged. For repairs in one category, the Bonferroni inequality gives $1-\prod_k(1-q_k)\ge\sum_kq_k-\sum_{k<l}q_kq_l$, which bounds the overstatement of the additive form. The bound on $-\sum_k\rho_k$ holds because the patched failure rate $r_0+\sum_k\rho_k$ on solved tasks is non-negative under additivity. For (iii), removing patch $k$ from a set $S$ changes its value by $-\big[\mathrm{rep}(S)-\mathrm{rep}(S\setminus k)\big]+(1-F)\big[\bar\rho(S)-\bar\rho(S\setminus k)\big]+c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}(1-e_{ij})$, where $\mathrm{rep}$ is the product-form repair and $\bar\rho$ the capped sum of harms. Repair is monotone in $S$, so the first bracket is non-negative, and capping $\sum\rho$ from below changes it by at most $\rho_k^+$ when $k$ is removed; summing over the dropped patches and taking expectations gives the bound. The greedy optimiser keeps, per component, the cell and patch with the largest positive net effect, which is a feasible deployable set, so the value it attains is a lower bound on the constrained optimum.

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:edge}.} With one patch, $V=\mathbb E[\Delta^+]-(\mathbb E\Delta)^+=\pi G-(\pi G-(1-\pi)H)^+=\min(\pi G,(1-\pi)H)$. The assumption that $\Delta$ is a function of $e_{ij}$ is what makes the edge state sufficient: if $q_k$ or $\rho_k$ is uncertain given $e_{ij}$, learning only $e_{ij}$ leaves value on the table and the general expression applies.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}.} The first part is Wald's approximation for a sequential probability ratio test between $p_1=b+(1-b)q$ and $p_0=b$: with thresholds $\pm\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}$, neglecting overshoot, the expected sample size under $p_1$ is the threshold divided by the per-observation drift $\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)$. The second is the normal-approximation sample size of a one-sided test of a binomial proportion, evaluated with the variance at $r_0$.

\section{Verification Policies on the Intervention Banks}
\label{app:policies}

\paragraph{Policies.} \emph{Net-effect allocation.} A spike-and-slab model per cell takes its edge prior from the attribution counts (Eq.~\ref{eq:prior}, Appendix~\ref{app:carve}), with grid posteriors for $q_k$ and Beta posteriors for each $b_i$ and each patch's regression failure rate $r_k$. It estimates Eq.~\ref{eq:delta} as $P(e_{ij}{=}1\mid\mathcal D)\,Ff_i\,\mathbb E[q_k\mid\mathcal D]-(1-F)\max(0,\mathbb E[r_k\mid\mathcal D]-r_0)/(1-r_0)-c$, a stricter objective than Eq.~\ref{eq:delta}: it charges $c$ on every applied change, never credits a patch for lowering failures on solved tasks, and scales harm by $1/(1-r_0)$; Table~\ref{tab:netabl} ablates the last two. It repeatedly runs the targeted replay, null replay or regression run with the largest $n$-step knowledge gradient per unit cost, $n\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ \citep{frazier2008kg}, and applies every patch with positive posterior net effect. \emph{Apply attributed} uses no evidence: for each category it applies the first patch of the most-blamed component and of any component with at least 40\% of the blame. \emph{Replay-each} tests hypotheses in order of blame with three replays and accepts on two successes, as in do-then-verify methods \citep{ma2025dover}. \emph{HarnessFix-style, per patch} adds two regression runs that must break nothing; these counts are ours. \emph{HarnessFix-style, bundle gate} is closer to the original, which validates a whole bundle of repairs on held-out tasks and promotes it if it solves at least one more task and breaks at most two \citep{chen2026harnessfix}; we apply the \emph{Apply attributed} bundle, draw validation episodes from the bank, and allow three attempts. \emph{Uncertainty sampling} replays the cell with the largest frequency-weighted posterior uncertainty, with or without three cheap checks per patch first. \carve{} and its variants are described in Appendix~\ref{app:carve}.

\paragraph{Scoring.} Policies run offline by drawing recorded outcomes from the bank, with replacement (without, as a check), with costs in measured tokens and a full replay as the unit; resampling seeds measure policy variability given the bank, not new evidence. \emph{Adjudication}: a cell is an effective edge if its best patch's replay success exceeds the category's null rate by at least 0.2 (one-sided Fisher $p<0.05$), giving 4 effective edges on retail and 7 on airline; choosing the best of three patches on the same replays makes this label optimistic, so we also score policies on bank halves they did not see. \emph{Harm}: every patch's $\rho_k$ is drawn from the task-level binomial model of Appendix~\ref{app:harm}, and edges and repair rates from a bootstrap of the replays. \emph{Deployable sets}: every accepted set is reduced to one patch per component, keeping the cell of the most frequent category, as on held-out tasks. Values are fractions of the \emph{repair-only oracle}, the failure mass repaired by the effective edges' best patches with no cost, a fixed yardstick worth 0.070 (retail) and 0.114 (airline) in absolute success. The unpatched baseline (10 runs on each of 111 regression tasks) was collected about 12 hours after the regression runs; unpatched held-out success did not change over that time (0.816 vs.\ 0.817 on clean retail episodes). \emph{Protocol}: retail was the development domain for every method, including the variants in Appendix~\ref{app:dev}; airline was evaluated once for the original policies and once, later, for the net-effect method, which was frozen on retail beforehand.

\section{Harm Model, Checks and Sensitivity}
\label{app:harm}

\paragraph{Model.} Regression outcomes are modelled per patch and task, together with the unpatched baseline runs on the same tasks. With $n_{0t}$ unpatched runs of regression task $t$ and $f_{0t}$ failures, and $n_{kt}$ runs of patch $k$ on task $t$ with $f_{kt}$ failures,
\begin{align*}
f_{0t}&\sim\mathrm{Bin}\big(n_{0t},\sigma(\alpha_t)\big),\\
f_{kt}&\sim\mathrm{Bin}\big(n_{kt},\sigma(\alpha_t+\beta(\alpha_t-\bar\alpha)+\delta_k)\big),
\end{align*}
with $\alpha_t\sim N(a,s_a^2)$, $\delta_k\sim N(\mu,s_d^2)$, $a,\mu\sim N(0,3^2)$, $s_a\sim\mathrm{HalfNormal}(2)$, $s_d\sim\mathrm{HalfNormal}(1)$, $\beta\sim N(0,1)$, and $\bar\alpha$ the logit of the mean unpatched failure rate. Task effects are shared by the baseline and every patch, so tasks that often fail unpatched do not make patches look harmful, and the common slope $\beta$ lets patches move every task's failure rate towards or away from the middle. A patch's harm is its excess failure probability averaged over the regression tasks, weighted by how often each task was solved among clean training episodes,
\[
\rho_k=\textstyle\sum_t w_t\big[\sigma(\alpha_t+\beta(\alpha_t-\bar\alpha)+\delta_k)-\sigma(\alpha_t)\big],
\]
which respects the support $\rho_k\ge-r_0$ by construction; patches without runs draw $\delta_k$ from the population. We sample by Metropolis within Gibbs, four chains, 2{,}400 retained draws; $\hat R\le1.006$ for $\mu$, $s_d$, $\beta$ and the pooled harm.

\paragraph{Estimates.} The unpatched failure rate on solved tasks is 0.146 (retail) and 0.124 (airline). The pooled excess failure rate of the patches is $-1.0$ points, 90\% interval $[-3.4,1.5]$, on retail and $-0.6$ $[-3.3,1.9]$ on airline, and the spread of $\rho_k$ between patches is 2.6 $[0.2,5.3]$ and 2.1 $[0.2,4.5]$ points. On retail the slope is negative, $\beta=-0.36$ $[-0.51,-0.17]$: patches tend to raise failures on tasks that rarely fail and lower them on tasks that often fail; on airline it is $0.03$ $[-0.21,0.31]$.

\paragraph{Posterior predictive check.} On the patches with 20 runs, the observed standard deviation of failure rates is 0.076 (retail) and 0.066 (airline); data replicated from the fitted model give 0.079 and 0.072, with $P(\text{replicated}\ge\text{observed})=0.62$ and 0.79. The number of patches without a failure (4 and 7 observed, 4.2 and 6.2 replicated) and the largest failure rate (0.30 and 0.30 observed, 0.35 and 0.31 replicated) are also reproduced. An earlier analysis used a normal approximation, $y_k=m_k/n_k-r_{0,k}\sim N(\rho_k,v_k)$ with $\rho_k\sim N(\mu,\tau^2)$, which predicted more spread between patches than observed (0.092 and 0.081, $P=0.95$ and 0.97); its posterior columns in Table~\ref{tab:robust} and its variants in Tables~\ref{tab:vpi} and~\ref{tab:harmmodels} are kept as sensitivity analyses.

\section{Study 2: RRSI Port and Registered Analysis}
\label{app:rrsi}

\paragraph{Code.} RRSI's loop is vendored from its public repository under the Apache 2.0 licence, with the upstream copyright headers kept. We changed the model backend (calls go to DeepSeek's Anthropic-compatible endpoint, with thinking disabled and token usage recorded per role), and one rule: a proposal that ends with no file change and no declared edit is bounced back to the proposer like an aborted one, at most three times, instead of ending the round with no candidate. RRSI's own domains (coding, workspace, engineering design) are not used. Our $\tau^2$ adapter implements RRSI's domain interface: a frozen episode driver that uses $\tau^2$'s databases, tools, policies, tasks and user-simulation guidelines, a starting harness with four hooks (choose the next action, check a tool call before it runs, render a tool result, rebuild per-episode state from a recorded prefix), and optional mechanisms the proposer may use: bounded extra model calls, harness-side tools and skill files. Memory across episodes is not allowed.

\paragraph{Leakage screening.} Besides RRSI's LLM critic, a pattern check rejects any added line that contains a task's user id, order or reservation id, item or flight number or customer e-mail, refers to the grader (gold actions, database hashes, evaluation criteria) or to $\tau^2$'s task data, imports the benchmark or our experiment code, or writes files.

\paragraph{Evidence collection per round.} \emph{References}: for every evolve task the incumbent failed in RRSI's full evaluation, its worst trial, at most 20 tasks (a seeded random subset beyond that). The start step is the earliest step the round's failure digest cites; without a digest, the first state-changing call that is not a gold action; otherwise the first step. Each reference is replayed 4 times with each measured candidate and 4 times with the incumbent (null replays). \emph{Judge}: DeepSeek-V4-Pro reads the candidate's diff and declared edits, up to 14 of the incumbent's failed trials of the round with their grading block, and one line per solved task, and returns a probability of repair for each failed task and an expected number of broken trials; its estimate is (expected repairs $-$ expected breaks) / evolve trials. \emph{Deployment}: every incumbent and measured candidate on the held-out tasks (retail 4, airline 8 trials per task), the base and final harnesses on 10 trials per task.

\paragraph{Estimators.} With $f$ the incumbent's evolve failure share, \emph{sample@b} is the mean paired difference on $b$ random (task, trial) pairs of the full evaluations; \emph{replay@b} is $f\cdot\widehat{\mathrm{fix}}_c$ from $b$ candidate replays spread evenly over the references; \emph{replay$-$null@b} is $f(\widehat{\mathrm{fix}}_c-\widehat{\mathrm{fix}}_{\mathrm{null}})$ from $b/2$ replays of each kind; \emph{net@b} uses $b/4$ candidate and $b/4$ null replays and $b/2$ regression pairs, on tasks the incumbent solved, and adds $(1-f)$ times their mean paired difference; \emph{net@b with prior} shrinks the candidate's repair rate to a Beta prior with mean 0.41 or 0.29, 0.7 or 0.2 and two pseudo-trials; \emph{full} is RRSI's own decision. Every estimate goes through RRSI's selection rule with the evidence's own noise band, twice its standard error, and the evidence's measured token cost; the judge has no noise band. Each rule's per-round score is the deployment gain of its choice, averaged over 400 random subsamples of the evidence.

\paragraph{Registration.} The pre-registration, the analysis code and the run scripts were committed before the first held-out episode; the analysis code may change after that only to fix a bug, and any such fix will be reported. Domains, splits, models, temperatures, $T$, $m$, $k$, budgets, evidence definitions, the decision rule, the held-out trial counts (except the pre-specified increase for power), the margin and the endpoints are fixed.

\paragraph{Planned cost.} Per domain, the RRSI trajectory (three base evaluations and up to 40 candidate evaluations on the evolve set), about 2{,}000--2{,}700 targeted replays and about 48 harnesses deployed on 160 held-out episodes each; about 17{,}000 episodes on retail and 12{,}400--13{,}100 on airline, plus the search and judge models. A replay from the failing step used about 0.85 of a full episode's tokens in a smoke run and is counted as one episode.

\section{The \carve{} Design Study}
\label{app:carve}

\paragraph{Why the simulator was optimistic.} Our first design study, \carve{}, used a simulator in which it needed half the budget of the strongest baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:carve}). Calibrated to the measured judge accuracy, spurious recovery, analyst accuracy and cost ratio, the simulator still predicts that \carve{} leads uncertainty sampling by 14 points on retail at budget 50; on the bank it trails by 15 points at budget 40 (significant harm, $c_{\mathrm w}=0.02$). The simulator shared the method's structure: one $b$ for all categories, value spread over many edges, and a fixed cost $c$ that made every wrong change harmful, an assumption that was never measured and on which the value of verification largely depends.


Our first approach treated judge checks as cheap and replays as expensive, and allocated a budget over both by Bayesian experimental design. We keep it here as the multi-fidelity method compared in \S\ref{sec:study1}, together with the simulator in which it was developed. The simulator shares the method's structure. Its results show the method working under its own assumptions, and they are not evidence about real agents.

\paragraph{Calibrated simulation.} With the measured judge accuracy, spurious recovery, analyst accuracy and cost ratio, the simulator predicts that \carve{} runs no judge checks on ShopDesk and retail and only a few on airline, which matches the break-even analysis in Table~\ref{tab:measured}. For $\tau^2$ retail it predicts net gains at budget 50 of 0.51 for \carve{} and 0.38 for uncertainty sampling; for airline, 0.33 and 0.31; for ShopDesk, 0.81 and 0.79.

An agent is built from \emph{components} $j\in\{1,\dots,J\}$: sections of its system prompt, tool descriptions, tool implementations, orchestration settings. Its failures have been grouped into \emph{categories} $i\in\{1,\dots,I\}$ by an LLM, with $f_i$ the share of failures in category $i$. For every cell $(i,j)$ an analyst LLM can propose $K$ candidate patches $a_{ijk}$ that rewrite component $j$ to address category $i$.

\paragraph{Interventions.} An intervention $(i,j,k,\phi)$ samples a failed trajectory labelled $i$, applies patch $a_{ijk}$, and observes a binary outcome at fidelity $\phi$. A \emph{full replay} ($\phi=F$) resumes the episode from the attributed failing step and records whether the task now succeeds. A \emph{single-step check} ($\phi=S$) regenerates only the failing step and asks a judge whether the diagnosed error is gone. They cost $c_F$ and $c_S\ll c_F$.

\paragraph{Edges and patch quality.} We write $e_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ for whether component $j$ is a cause of category $i$, meaning some change to $j$ repairs a non-trivial fraction of its failures, and $q_{ijk}\in[0,1]$ for the repair probability of patch $k$ when $e_{ij}=1$. This separation matters because a negative replay outcome is ambiguous: either $j$ is the wrong component or $a_{ijk}$ is a bad patch. Testing several patches per cell resolves the ambiguity.

\paragraph{Objective.} After spending at most budget $B$, the method accepts a set of cells $\mathcal{A}$ and, for each, a patch $k^\star_{ij}$. Accepting a true edge repairs a fraction $q_{ijk^\star}$ of category $i$'s failures; accepting a false edge costs a regression $c_{\mathrm{fp}}$, reflecting that unnecessary changes perturb behaviour on tasks that used to succeed \citep{vasudev2026intervention}. We measure
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G(\mathcal{A}) = {}&\sum_i f_i\Big(1-\!\!\prod_{j\in\mathcal{A}_i^+}\!\!(1-q_{ijk^\star_{ij}})\Big)\\
&- c_{\mathrm{fp}}\,\big|\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}:e_{ij}=0\}\big|,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_i^+=\{j:(i,j)\in\mathcal{A},\,e_{ij}=1\}$. This is the net failure mass repaired; we report it as a fraction of the oracle value obtained by accepting exactly the true edges with their best patches.


\subsection{\carve{}}
\label{app:cmethod}

\paragraph{Observation model}
For a trajectory labelled $i$ and patch $a_{ijk}$, the probability that a full replay succeeds is
\begin{equation}
p_{ijk} = \begin{cases} \lambda\, q_{ijk} + (1-\lambda)\, b & e_{ij}=1,\\ b & e_{ij}=0,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the spurious recovery rate (stochastic re-generation sometimes succeeds regardless of the patch) and $\lambda$ is the accuracy of the category labels used to sample trajectories; a mislabelled trajectory is treated as unaffected by the patch. A single-step check returns $z=1$ with probability $s\,p+\epsilon\,(1-p)$, where $s$ and $\epsilon$ are the sensitivity and false-positive rate of the check relative to the full outcome. All four nuisance parameters can be estimated from a small calibration set on which both fidelities and a human audit are run.

\paragraph{Attribution-informed prior}
Let $n_{ij}$ be the number of failures in category $i$ that the analyst attributed to component $j$, and $\hat r$ the analyst's attribution accuracy estimated on a small audited sample. With $d$ the expected number of causal components per category and $\rho=d/J$ the base edge density, we set
\begin{equation}
\pi_{ij} = (1-\hat r)\,\rho + \hat r\,\min\!\big(1,\ d\, n_{ij}/n_i\big),
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
so that an unreliable analyst ($\hat r\to0$) yields the flat prior $\rho$ and a reliable one concentrates mass on the components it names. Eq.~\ref{eq:prior} is a heuristic interpolation, not a calibrated model; with a larger audit, $\pi$ could instead be fitted by logistic regression on audited attributions. Patch quality has prior $q_{ijk}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_q,\beta_q)$.

\paragraph{Posterior}
Cells are independent given the data. For each cell we keep the posterior log-odds of $e_{ij}$ and, for each patch, a posterior over $q_{ijk}$ on a fixed grid of $G=101$ points. With $\mathcal{D}_{ijk}$ the outcomes observed for patch $k$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{logit}\,P(e_{ij}{=}1\mid\mathcal{D}) ={}& \mathrm{logit}\,\pi_{ij}\\
&+ \textstyle\sum_k \log\big(\bar L^{1}_{ijk}/L^{0}_{ijk}\big),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\bar L^{1}_{ijk}=\int P(\mathcal{D}_{ijk}\mid q,e_{ij}{=}1)\,dP(q)$ is the marginal likelihood of patch $k$'s outcomes under a true edge and $L^{0}_{ijk}=P(\mathcal{D}_{ijk}\mid e_{ij}{=}0)$.
Every update multiplies one grid vector by a likelihood vector, so updates and look-aheads are vectorised dot products.

\paragraph{Selecting interventions}
The value of the current decision for cell $(i,j)$, with $P_{ij}$ its edge posterior and $\hat Q_{ij}=\max_k\mathbb{E}[q_{ijk}\mid\mathcal{D}]$, is
\begin{equation}
V_{ij} = \max\big(P_{ij}\,f_i\,\hat Q_{ij} - (1-P_{ij})\,c_{\mathrm{fp}},\ 0\big),
\end{equation}
the expected contribution of the cell to Eq.~\ref{eq:gain} under the Bayes decision (accept iff the first term is positive), treating cells as additive. Eq.~\ref{eq:gain} combines components of one category multiplicatively, so $V_{ij}$ slightly overstates the value of a second cause for an already repaired category; we accept this approximation because it keeps cells independent. For an action $a=(i,j,k,\phi)$ repeated $n$ times, let $V'_{ij}(s)$ be the value after observing $s$ successes. The $n$-step knowledge gradient is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KG}_n(a) = \sum_{s=0}^{n} P(s\mid\mathcal{D},a)\,V'_{ij}(s) - V_{ij},
\end{equation}
and \carve{} runs the action maximising
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(a) = \max_{n\in\{1,2,4,8\}} \frac{\mathrm{KG}_n(a)}{n\,c_\phi}.
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Looking ahead several observations matters because a single binary outcome often cannot flip a decision, in which case $\mathrm{KG}_1=0$ even for a valuable cell; maximising over $n$ is the standard remedy \citep{frazier2008kg}. Because cells are independent, only the observed cell's scores change after each outcome, so each step rescores one cell in $O(KG)$ time after an initial pass over all cells.

\paragraph{Decisions and stopping.} At any point the method accepts the cells whose current value is positive and applies, for each, the patch with the highest posterior mean quality. It stops early when no action has a score above a small threshold, i.e.\ when no remaining experiment is expected to be worth its cost.


\subsection{When Do Priors and Cheap Checks Help?}
\label{app:canalysis}

These results analyse an idealised per-cell sequential test, not \carve{}'s full policy; they explain which quantities govern the method's behaviour. We consider a single cell and a single patch with known success probabilities $p_1$ (true edge) and $p_0=b$, under a rule that stops when the posterior log-odds leaves $[-A,A]$, $A=\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}$. Let $D_1=\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)$ and $D_0=\mathrm{KL}(p_0\Vert p_1)$ be the expected log-likelihood-ratio drift per observation under each hypothesis. Proofs, which follow Wald's approximations \citep{wald1945sequential}, are in Appendix~\ref{app:cproofs}.

\begin{proposition}[Value of the LLM prior]
\label{prop:cprior}
Let $|\ell|<A$ and neglect overshoot and stopping at the wrong boundary (whose probability is approximately $\delta e^{-\ell}$ for a true edge and $\delta e^{\ell}$ for a false one). A cell with initial log-odds $\ell$ then needs $N_1(\ell)=(A-\ell)/D_1$ observations in expectation if it is a true edge and $N_0(\ell)=(A+\ell)/D_0$ if not. Replacing a flat prior $\ell_\rho$ by LLM-derived log-odds $\ell_{ij}$ saves, in expectation over cells,
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \frac{\rho\,\mathbb{E}[\delta\ell\mid e{=}1]}{D_1} - \frac{(1-\rho)\,\mathbb{E}[\delta\ell\mid e{=}0]}{D_0},
\end{equation}
with $\delta\ell=\ell_{ij}-\ell_\rho$,
observations per cell, which is positive exactly when the prior raises true edges more, relative to their base rate and drift, than it raises false ones.
\end{proposition}

Under Eq.~\ref{eq:prior}, $\ell_{ij}\to\ell_\rho$ as $\hat r\to0$, so $\Delta\to0$: a useless analyst costs nothing beyond the flat prior, whereas trusting its labels outright (LLM-only) makes permanent errors. A miscalibrated analyst can make $\Delta$ negative, but only by an amount linear in the log-odds shift, after which the evidence takes over. Appendix~\ref{app:prop1} compares these predictions with simulated sequential tests.

\begin{proposition}[Cheap checks]
\label{prop:cfid}
Let $D^S_e$ denote the drifts of single-step outcomes, $z\sim\mathrm{Bern}(s\,p_e+\epsilon(1-p_e))$. Under the same approximations, a test using only single-step checks reaches the stopping boundary at lower expected cost than one using only full replays if and only if
\begin{equation}
\frac{c_S}{c_F} < \frac{D^S_e}{D_e},
\end{equation}
where $e$ is the true state of the cell.
\end{proposition}

Since $e$ is unknown, the proposition gives a clear rule only when the cost ratio lies outside the interval spanned by the two critical ratios; inside it, which fidelity is better depends on the posterior, which is what \carve{}'s look-ahead weighs.

With our default parameters ($p_1=0.54$, $b=0.05$, $s=0.85$, $\epsilon=0.15$) the critical ratio is $0.31$ for true edges and $0.44$ for false ones; with a weak check ($s=0.65$, $\epsilon=0.35$) it falls to $0.05$--$0.08$. Section~\ref{app:cfid} tests both predictions.


\subsection{Controlled Experiments}
\label{app:csim}

Real agents do not reveal which component truly caused a failure, so we first evaluate in a testbed where the causal graph is known and the method's model is deliberately misspecified.

\paragraph{Setup}
\paragraph{Testbed.} Each world has $I=12$ categories with Zipfian frequencies, $J=20$ components and $K=3$ patches per cell. Every category has one or two true components and one \emph{decoy} component that wrong attributions prefer, mimicking symptom-level mislabelling. Patch quality is bimodal: with probability 0.3 a patch is bad ($q\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,9)$), otherwise $q\sim\mathrm{Beta}(6,3)$. A trajectory sampled for category $i$ is mislabelled with probability $1-\lambda=0.2$, in which case it comes from another category (weighted by frequency) and is repaired only if \emph{its own} edge to $j$ exists, unlike the model's flat treatment of mislabelled traces. The analyst attributes 20 failures per category with accuracy $r=0.5$; wrong attributions go to the decoy with probability 0.6. The analyst's accuracy is estimated from an audit of 20 attributions. Costs are $c_F=1$, $c_S=0.1$, $c_{\mathrm{fp}}=0.02$, and $s=0.85$, $\epsilon=0.15$, $b=0.05$. Section~\ref{app:crobust} relaxes the assumptions the generator shares with the model. Full details are in Appendix~\ref{app:sim}.

\paragraph{Baselines.} \emph{LLM-only} accepts, for every category, the most-attributed component and any component holding at least 40\% of the attributions, with the first patch, i.e.\ it acts on an induced taxonomy without verification. \emph{Replay-each} tests attributed hypotheses in order of attribution count with three full replays of the first patch and accepts a hypothesis if at least two succeed, mirroring do-then-verify methods such as DoVer; \emph{Replay-each, 3 patches} gives each hypothesis two replays of each of three patches and accepts if any patch succeeds twice; \emph{Sequential-each} replays each hypothesis, cycling through patches, until \carve{}'s own posterior leaves $[0.05,0.95]$. Untested hypotheses fall back to LLM-only, except for Sequential-each, which uses the Bayes decision. \emph{Thompson sampling} samples $(e,q)$ from the posterior and replays the cell and patch with the largest sampled repair. \emph{Uncertainty sampling} replays the cell with the largest frequency-weighted uncertainty $f_i\min(P_{ij},1-P_{ij})$, cycling through patches, at full fidelity; \emph{Uncertainty + cheap checks} first runs three single-step checks per (cell, patch) and then confirms with full replays. All baselines except LLM-only and the Replay-each variants share \carve{}'s posterior and decision rule, so comparisons among them isolate the selection rule.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Net gain (Eq.~\ref{eq:gain}) as a fraction of the oracle, and edge F1. We report means and standard errors over 50 worlds for the main experiment and 30 for the others; all methods see the same worlds, so differences are paired.

\subsection{Proofs for \carve{}'s propositions}
\label{app:cproofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:cprior}.} Let $L_t=\ell+\sum_{u\le t}\log\frac{P(o_u\mid e=1)}{P(o_u\mid e=0)}$ be the posterior log-odds after $t$ observations. Under $e=1$, the increments are i.i.d.\ with mean $D_1=\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)>0$. The rule stops at $N=\inf\{t: L_t\notin(-A,A)\}$. By Wald's identity, $\mathbb{E}_1[L_N-\ell]=D_1\,\mathbb{E}_1[N]$. Wald's approximations give $P_1(L_N\le -A)\approx e^{-(A+\ell)}\approx\delta e^{-\ell}$ and $P_0(L_N\ge A)\approx\delta e^{\ell}$. Neglecting overshoot and this probability (which requires $\delta e^{|\ell|}\ll1$), $L_N=A$, hence $\mathbb{E}_1[N]=(A-\ell)/D_1$; if $\ell\ge A$ no observation is taken, which is why we assume $|\ell|<A$. Symmetrically, under $e=0$ the drift is $-D_0$ and $\mathbb{E}_0[N]=(A+\ell)/D_0$. Taking the difference between $\ell_\rho$ and $\ell_{ij}$ and averaging over cells with $P(e{=}1)=\rho$ gives $\Delta$. When the patch quality $q$ is unknown, outcomes are exchangeable rather than i.i.d.\ under the marginal likelihood, and Wald's identity does not apply directly; conditioning on $q$ recovers the result with $D_1=D_1(q)$, which approaches zero for a bad patch. This is the formal reason a single bad patch can stall verification and why \carve{} keeps several patches per cell.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:cfid}.} By the same argument a single-step test needs $(A\mp\ell)/D^S_e$ observations at cost $c_S$ each, and a full-replay test needs $(A\mp\ell)/D_e$ at cost $c_F$. The single-step test is cheaper iff $c_S/D^S_e<c_F/D_e$. Since the single-step outcome is a garbling of the full outcome, the data-processing inequality gives $D^S_e\le D_e$, so the critical ratio is at most one.

\subsection{Simulation Details}
\label{app:sim}

Category frequencies are $f_i\propto 1/i$. For each category the number of true components is uniform on $\{1,2\}$ and the decoy is a further distinct component. The audit estimate $\hat r$ is a binomial proportion from 20 audited attributions. The prior uses $d=1.5$. The model's grid has 101 points; look-ahead horizons are $\{1,2,4,8\}$; the stopping threshold is $10^{-6}$. Replay-each uses three replays and accepts with at least two successes. Uncertainty sampling switches to an untried patch once the current patch has three full observations. In the loop experiment, 240 attributions per round are allocated to categories by a multinomial draw with the current failure distribution. All randomness is seeded; the code reproduces every number in this paper with \texttt{python -m sim.experiments all}.
