\title{Is a Diagnosis Worth Verifying? Measuring the Cost, Noise and Payoff\\of Patch Verification in Self-Improving Agents}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Self-improving LLM agents let an analyst model read failed trajectories, blame a component and patch it, and recent systems verify each patch before applying it. We measure what this verification costs and what it buys, for one agent model and one analyst model on three customer-service tool-use settings: a fault-injected environment we built and the retail and airline domains of $\tau^2$-bench. Four findings organise the paper. First, none of the evidence we tested was cheap. A single-step check that regenerates the failing step and asks an LLM judge costs 22--72\% of a full replay in tokens, because regenerating the step alone costs 12--15\%; with four judge configurations, its information per token is at best comparable to a replay's, with intervals that do not separate the two. Second, replays need controls: 20--23\% of unpatched replays from the failing step succeed anyway. Third, a patch's effect on previously solved tasks decides which verification policy is best, and it is the least measurable quantity: a task-matched unpatched baseline places its mean at $-0.003$ (retail) and $-0.006$ (airline), with 90\% intervals of about $\pm0.03$, but per-patch effects are not identified, and no policy is reliably best on our intervention banks, whether policies and outcomes use the same bank or separate halves of it. Fourth, on held-out tasks, applying the attributed patches without verification raised success from 0.76 to 0.88 on retail and from 0.68 to 0.75 on airline, and no verified patch set did better in this small-budget experiment; under the measured uncertainty, the probability that ten episodes of verification ever pay back is at most 0.56. To organise these measurements we decompose what verification can be worth into avoiding harm, choosing among candidate patches and discovering missed fixes. The decomposition is an accounting framework whose values depend on the harm model, not a decision rule. We release the environments, fault suites and intervention banks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A growing family of systems improves LLM agents from their own failures: the agent runs, an analyst model reads the failed trajectories, and its diagnosis drives a change to a prompt, a tool description or the orchestration \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa,chen2026harnessfix}. The diagnosis step, \emph{failure attribution}, is unreliable \citep{zhang2025whichagent,liu2026whowhenpro}: models label failures by their visible symptom rather than their cause, so a loop that trusts them will often change the wrong component. The common remedy is to verify before acting: patch the blamed component and replay the failure \citep{ma2025dover,lin2026reflect,shah2026car}, ask a judge whether the failing step is fixed, or re-run tasks that used to pass and keep the patch only if nothing breaks \citep{chen2026harnessfix}.

Verification costs agent episodes. A tempting design, and the one we started from, treats LLM judgements as cheap evidence and full replays as expensive and allocates a budget across both; in a simulator built on these assumptions it needs half the budget of the strongest baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:carve}). On real agents the premise failed for every judge we tried. Across three tool-use settings, a judge check that reads the trajectory costs 23--72\% of a full replay; giving the judge only the failing step, or using the smaller agent model as judge, makes it cheaper, but regenerating the step is itself a sizeable part of a replay, and per token the checks are at best about as informative as replays. None of the evidence we tested was cheap.

We therefore price every piece of evidence in episodes and ask a prior question: \emph{when is a diagnosis worth verifying at all?} Our answer is empirical and specific to the agent, analyst and environments we study. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Measurements of verification evidence} on ShopDesk and on offline intervention banks for $\tau^2$-bench retail and airline: paired targeted replays and judge checks for every patch, four judge configurations, null replays that expose spurious recovery, regression runs, and a task-matched unpatched baseline (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{An evaluation of eleven verification policies} in three ways: on the full banks, with policy evidence and outcome assessment taken from separate halves of each bank, and by deploying their patch sets on held-out tasks. A patch's effect on previously solved tasks decides the ranking; its mean is within $\pm0.03$ of zero, but per-patch effects leave the ranking open, and on held-out tasks no gain from verification over applying the attributed patches was detected at budget 10 (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{An accounting framework.} A patch's net effect splits into repair, spurious recovery and regressions, each estimated by one kind of experiment (\S\ref{sec:problem}), and what verification can add splits into avoiding harm, choosing among candidate patches and discovering missed edges (\S\ref{sec:when}). We state which guarantees survive deployment constraints. The framework says where verification could pay; it is not an operational decision rule, because its decisive input, the harm of each individual patch, is what our experiments could not measure, and its values change several-fold with the harm model.
\end{itemize}
A net-effect allocation method built from these measurements serves as a reference policy (\S\ref{sec:method}); it is not best everywhere, and \carve{}, the multi-fidelity design we started from, is reported as a design study in Appendix~\ref{app:carve}.


\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Failure attribution for LLM agents.}
Who\&When and its extension show that locating the responsible agent and step remains far from solved \citep{zhang2025whichagent,liu2026whowhenpro}. AgenTracer trains a small attribution model on counterfactual replays and injected faults \citep{zhang2026agentracer}; AgentDebug and ErrorProbe pair taxonomies or dependency tracing with corrective feedback \citep{zhu2025agentdebug,li2026errorprobe}. MAST gives attribution a fixed vocabulary \citep{cemri2025mast}, and AdaMAST induces a system-specific taxonomy validated by agreement between LLM annotators \citep{cemri2026adamast}; we instead check the mapping from categories to components by intervention.

\paragraph{Verification by intervention.}
DoVer validates hypotheses by editing a message or plan and re-running \citep{ma2025dover}; REFLECT tests patches by targeted replay \citep{lin2026reflect}; CausalFlow and Causal Agent Replay score or formalise step interventions causally \citep{bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}; AgentDebugX gates reruns by policy \citep{zhu2026agentdebugx}. These operate per trajectory or hypothesis and do not measure what the evidence costs or allocate a shared budget, which is our focus; any of them can serve as the replay primitive.

\paragraph{Self-improving agents.}
Reflexion, Self-Refine and ExpeL turn feedback into verbal lessons \citep{shinn2023reflexion,madaan2023selfrefine,zhao2024expel}; ProTeGi, OPRO, TextGrad and DSPy rewrite prompts from feedback \citep{pryzant2023protegi,yang2024opro,yuksekgonul2025textgrad,khattab2024dspy}; ADAS, the Darwin G\"odel Machine and GEPA search over designs \citep{hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa}. HarnessFix promotes a repaired harness only if it solves more validation tasks while breaking at most two \citep{chen2026harnessfix}, a regression-aware gate on whole bundles; Bayesian-Agent applies threshold rules to Beta posteriors over skills \citep{wu2026bayesianagent}. We compare against HarnessFix-style gates and ask whether any of this verification pays.

\paragraph{Experimental design.}
Our reference policy uses the knowledge gradient \citep{frazier2008kg,frazier2010paradoxes} within Bayesian experimental design \citep{chaloner1995bayesian}; related problems include multi-fidelity bandits \citep{kandasamy2016multifidelity,poloczek2017miso}, best-arm identification \citep{locatelli2016thresholding,audibert2010bai,russo2016toptwo}, sequential testing \citep{wald1945sequential} and causal bandits \citep{tong2001active,hauser2014active,lattimore2016causal}. Delta debugging isolates failure-inducing changes by experiment \citep{zeller2002dd}, and program repair warns that a patch passing a failing test may not fix the fault \citep{smith2015overfitting}.

\section{Problem: Episode-Priced Evidence}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Setting.} An agent is assembled from \emph{components} $j$ (prompt sections, tool descriptions, configuration). On a task distribution it fails with rate $F$; an LLM groups its failures into categories $i$ with shares $f_i$. For each \emph{cell} $(i,j)$ that the analyst blames, it proposes $K$ candidate patches $k$ that rewrite component $j$. Let $e_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether changing $j$ can repair category $i$ at all, and $q_k$ the fraction of category-$i$ failures that patch $k$ repairs if so. Separating $e$ from $q$ matters because a patch that fails to help may be aimed at the wrong component or merely be a bad patch. We call a cell with $e_{ij}=1$ an \emph{effective edge}. This is an interventional notion: some change to $j$ repairs failures of category $i$. It need not mean that $j$ was the original cause of those failures, and our ground truth (\S\ref{sec:setup}) establishes only that a tested patch helps.

\paragraph{Net effect.} Applying patch $k$ of cell $(i,j)$ changes the success rate by
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k = F f_i e_{ij} q_k - (1-F)\rho_k - c_{\mathrm{a}} - c_{\mathrm{w}}(1-e_{ij}),
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_k$ is the patch's absolute excess failure rate on tasks that used to succeed (regressions, which may hit any category): such a task fails a re-run with probability $r_0$ unpatched and $r_0+\rho_k$ patched, so $\rho_k<0$ is possible, $c_{\mathrm a}\ge0$ is a cost charged for every applied change (review, maintenance) and $c_{\mathrm w}\ge0$ a penalty charged only for a change aimed at a wrong component (risk not captured by $\rho_k$). We report costs as fractions of the failure mass $F$ and vary both. The goal is to choose a set of patches, at most one per cell, that maximises $\sum_k\Delta_k$ on held-out tasks.\footnote{Effects of patches to different components are treated as additive. Held-out success of whole patch sets and a leave-one-out ablation (\S\ref{sec:results}, Appendix~\ref{app:ablate}) are the only checks.}

\paragraph{Evidence.} Three kinds of experiment, each costing a fraction of an episode, inform Eq.~\ref{eq:delta}:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item A \emph{targeted replay} resumes a category-$i$ failure from the attributed step with the patch applied; it succeeds with probability $b_i+(1-b_i)e_{ij}q_k$.
\item A \emph{null replay} does the same without a patch and succeeds with probability $b_i$, the category's \emph{spurious recovery} rate. Without it, replay success cannot be interpreted.
\item A \emph{regression run} executes a previously successful task from scratch with the patch and fails with probability $r_0+\rho_k$, where $r_0$ is the natural re-run failure rate.
\end{itemize}
A fourth, a \emph{single-step check} that regenerates only the failing step and asks an LLM judge whether the error is gone, is often proposed as the cheap option. We measure its cost and accuracy in \S\ref{sec:results}. Unlike classic multi-fidelity settings, all four cost a substantial fraction of an episode, within a factor of four of each other.

\section{When Can Verification Pay?}
\label{sec:when}

Before any evidence, the net effects $\Delta=(\Delta_k)$ of the candidate patches are uncertain, with a prior that reflects the analyst's accuracy and what is known about patch quality and regressions. The \emph{default} applies, in each cell, the patch with the largest $\mathbb E[\Delta_k]$ if that is positive. Verification observes outcomes $Y$ and then chooses; its value is $V(Y)=\mathbb E\big[\max_S\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k\mid Y]\big]-\max_S\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k]$, before the cost of the experiments. Write $x^+=\max(x,0)$ and $x^-=\max(-x,0)$.

\begin{proposition}[Value of verification]
\label{prop:vpi}
For any experiments $Y$, $V(Y)$ is at most the value of learning $\Delta$ exactly, which is the sum over cells of
\begin{equation}
V_{ij}=\mathbb E\big[\max_k\Delta_k^+\big]-\max_k\big(\mathbb E\Delta_k\big)^+ .
\end{equation}
With $k^\star$ the default patch of the cell,
\begin{equation}
V_{ij}\;\le\;\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[\max_k\Delta_k^+-\Delta_{k^\star}^+\big]}_{\text{choosing among patches}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[\Delta_{k^\star}^-\big]}_{\text{avoiding harm}},
\label{eq:vpi}
\end{equation}
with equality when the default applies $k^\star$, and $\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^-]\le(1-F)\,\mathbb E[\rho_{k^\star}^+]+c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}P(e_{ij}{=}0)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Edge-only verification]
\label{cor:edge}
Suppose a cell has a single candidate patch whose net effect depends only on $e_{ij}$: $\Delta=G$ if $e_{ij}=1$ and $\Delta=-H$ if $e_{ij}=0$, with $G,H\ge0$ known (so $q_k$ and $\rho_k$ are known given the edge). With $\pi=P(e_{ij}{=}1)$, $V_{ij}=\min\big(\pi G,(1-\pi)H\big)$.
\end{corollary}

Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. \emph{What survives deployment constraints.} Deployment may use a heuristic default $D$, applies at most one patch per component, which couples cells, combines repairs of one category as $1-\prod_k(1-q_k)$ (overstated by at most $f_i\sum_{k<l}q_kq_l$ when added), and cannot push the failure rate on solved tasks below zero ($-\sum_k\rho_k\le r_0$). Three statements survive. (i) The first sentence of the proposition holds for any set of admissible actions, so $V(Y)$ is at most the value of perfect information over deployable sets. (ii) Over a heuristic default that value is $V+G_D$, where $G_D=\max_S\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k]-\mathbb E[\sum_{k\in D}\Delta_k]\ge0$ is the \emph{decision-rule gap} that better use of the prior alone would close. (iii) The value of dropping harmful default patches is at most their expected harm, $\sum_{k\in D}\mathbb E[(1-F)\rho_k^++c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}(1-e_{ij})]$, because dropping a patch from an overlapping set loses at most its marginal repair. What does not survive is the separation over cells: with one patch per component, the choice and discovery terms have no closed form, and we compute them in sequence (harm, then choice on the default's components, then any component) with a greedy optimiser, which gives a lower bound on the constrained value of perfect information under a given harm model (\S\ref{sec:results}). The three sources of value are \emph{avoiding harm}, which needs regressions or change costs because replay success alone cannot create it; \emph{choosing among patches} of different quality; and \emph{discovery} of edges the analyst missed, the whole term $\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+]$ in cells the default leaves alone. The corollary fails when quality or regressions are uncertain given the edge ($\pi=1$ with $\Delta=+0.10$ or $-0.05$ equally likely gives $V=0.025$).

\begin{proposition}[Cost of evidence]
\label{prop:cost}
Under Wald's approximations, a sequential test that decides $e_{ij}$ from targeted replays with error $\delta$ needs on average about $\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\,/\,\mathrm{KL}\big(b+(1-b)q\,\Vert\, b\big)$ replays when the edge is real. Detecting a regression of size $\rho$ against a base rate $r_0$ with a one-sided level-$\alpha$ test and power $1-\beta$ needs about $(z_\alpha+z_\beta)^2 r_0(1-r_0)/\rho^2$ regression runs.
\end{proposition}

Two consequences follow. First, targeted replays are uninformative about the harm term, because they only see the target category: a patch can pass every replay and still break other tasks. Second, small regressions are expensive to see. With $r_0=0.1$, a regression of $\rho=0.25$ is detected with 90\% power by 20 runs, whereas $\rho=0.03$ needs about 850. Since many small regressions add up, failing to detect them is not evidence that they are absent; \S\ref{sec:results} therefore reports interval estimates rather than significance counts.

\paragraph{Three questions.} Proposition~\ref{prop:vpi} is the standard value-of-information argument \citep{chaloner1995bayesian}; what is new is its decomposition for patches and its evaluation on measured banks. \emph{Allocation} asks which experiments to run within a budget (\S\ref{sec:method}); \emph{whether to verify at all} depends on the horizon: experiments costing $C$ episodes pay back over $N$ future episodes when $N\,V(Y)>C$ in success units.

\section{Allocation Policies}
\label{sec:method}

When verification is warranted, the budget must still be allocated across cells, patches and experiment types. We compare eleven policies.

\paragraph{Net-effect allocation (reference).} A spike-and-slab model per cell takes its edge prior from the attribution counts (Eq.~\ref{eq:prior}, Appendix~\ref{app:carve}), with grid posteriors for $q_k$ and Beta posteriors for each $b_i$ and each patch's regression failure rate $r_k$. It estimates Eq.~\ref{eq:delta} as $P(e_{ij}{=}1\mid\mathcal D)\,Ff_i\,\mathbb E[q_k\mid\mathcal D]-(1-F)\max(0,\mathbb E[r_k\mid\mathcal D]-r_0)/(1-r_0)-c$, a stricter objective than Eq.~\ref{eq:delta}: it charges $c$ on every applied change ($c_{\mathrm a}=c$, $c_{\mathrm w}=0$, whereas we score with $c_{\mathrm w}$), never credits a patch for lowering failures on solved tasks, and scales harm by $1/(1-r_0)$; Appendix~\ref{app:harm} ablates the last two. It repeatedly runs the targeted replay, null replay or regression run with the largest $n$-step knowledge gradient per unit cost, $n\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ \citep{frazier2008kg}, and applies every patch with positive posterior net effect.

\paragraph{Baselines.} \emph{Apply attributed} uses no evidence: for each category it applies the first patch of the most-blamed component and of any component with at least 40\% of the blame. \emph{Replay-each} tests hypotheses in order of blame with three replays and accepts on two successes, as in do-then-verify methods \citep{ma2025dover}. \emph{HarnessFix-style, per patch} adds two regression runs that must break nothing; these counts are ours. \emph{HarnessFix-style, bundle gate} is closer to the original, which validates a whole bundle of repairs on held-out tasks and promotes it if it solves at least one more task and breaks at most two \citep{chen2026harnessfix}. We apply the \emph{Apply attributed} bundle, draw validation episodes from the bank, and allow three attempts. \emph{Uncertainty sampling} replays the cell with the largest frequency-weighted posterior uncertainty, with or without three cheap checks per patch first. \carve{}, the multi-fidelity design we started from, and its variants are described in Appendix~\ref{app:carve}.

\section{Environments and Intervention Banks}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Environments.} \emph{ShopDesk} is a customer-service environment we built, with a seeded database, six deterministic tools, exactly checkable answers and 11 named agent components. \emph{$\tau^2$-bench retail and airline} \citep{yao2024taubench,barres2025tau2} are customer-service domains with an LLM user simulator and database-state rewards; unlike $\tau^2$'s telecom domain, they are not dual-control, since only the agent acts through tools. We split each domain policy into sections and add every tool description and the step limit as components, giving 24 components for retail and 21 for airline. The agent is DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Attribution, taxonomy induction \citep{wan2024tntllm}, patch generation and judging use DeepSeek-V4-Pro.

\paragraph{Faults that look like defects.} Each training episode runs clean or with one injected fault, written as a plausible authoring mistake rather than an obvious artefact. In retail: a policy section allowing gift-card refunds, a cancellation rule without the reason check, a product-tool description that confuses item and product ids, and a tight step limit. In airline: a wrong bag allowance, a 7-day instead of 24-hour cancellation window, a missing basic-economy restriction, proactive compensation, and a tight step limit. Clean episodes contribute natural failures: 22 of 137 on retail and 28 of 112 on airline.

\paragraph{Banks.} For each $\tau^2$ domain we take 36 cells, the attributed cells in order of blame plus random unattributed cells of populated categories, and generate three patches per cell. For every patch we record 20 paired targeted replays and single-step checks. We also record 20 null replays per category and 4 regression runs per patch, extended to 20 for the 121 patches that any of the original policies accepted. Policies run offline by drawing recorded outcomes from the bank, with replacement (without, as a check), with costs in measured tokens and full replay $=1$; resampling seeds measure policy variability given the bank, not new evidence. \emph{Adjudication}: a cell is an effective edge if its best patch's replay success exceeds the category's null rate by at least 0.2 (one-sided Fisher $p<0.05$), giving 4 effective edges on retail and 7 on airline. Choosing the best of three patches on the same replays makes this label optimistic, so we also score policies on bank halves they did not see. \emph{Baseline}: we ran the unpatched agent 10 times on each of the 111 regression tasks (1,110 runs), about 12 hours after the regression runs; unpatched held-out success did not change over that time (0.816 vs.\ 0.817 on clean retail episodes). \emph{Harm}: we score the posterior expected value of each patch set, drawing every patch's $\rho_k$, its excess failure rate over its task-matched baseline, from a hierarchical normal posterior with estimated mean $\mu$ and spread $\tau$, and edges and repair rates from a bootstrap of the replays. Draws respect the support: $\rho_k\ge-r_{0,k}$ and $\sum_k\rho_k\ge-\bar r_0$. Appendix~\ref{app:harm} states and checks the model. \emph{Deployable sets}: every accepted set is reduced to one patch per component, keeping the cell of the most frequent category, as on held-out tasks; all primary results score these sets. Charging only significant regressions (one-sided binomial $p<0.05$) is a sensitivity analysis. Values are fractions of the \emph{repair-only oracle}, the failure mass repaired by the effective edges' best patches with no cost, a fixed yardstick worth 0.070 (retail) and 0.114 (airline) in absolute success. Independently of the bank, we apply the accepted patch sets to 40 unseen retail tasks and 20 unseen airline tasks.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Retail was the development domain for every method, including the variants in Appendix~\ref{app:dev}. Airline was evaluated once for the original policies and once, later, for the net-effect method, which was frozen on retail beforehand.

\paragraph{What policies know before the budget.} Attributions, taxonomy and candidate patches are shared and uncharged; every edge prior uses analyst accuracy $\hat r=0.7$ rather than the audited values, and experiment costs, mean spurious recovery and judge calibration come uncharged from the bank. Regression tasks are drawn from solved clean training episodes, and 98--100\% of the patches any policy deploys have 20 regression runs. Appendix~\ref{app:inputs} gives details.

\paragraph{Judge checks are not cheap, and not clearly more informative.} A single-step check costs 22--72\% of a full replay in tokens: regenerating the failing step alone costs 0.12--0.15, and the cheapest judge prompts add 0.10--0.15 (Table~\ref{tab:judges}, Appendix~\ref{app:judges}, with three cheaper judges tested on 360 bank pairs per domain). It is faster: two sequential model calls against a median of 6--7 for a replay. Whether it carries more information per token than a replay depends on how judge accuracy is defined. Against the single replay paired with each check, the bank judge recognises 37--57\% of repairs with false-positive rates of 0.11--0.21, and every judge's ratio of break-even to actual cost (Proposition~\ref{prop:cfid}) is 0.16--0.62, with 90\% intervals below 1 except for the bank judge on airline. But a single replay is itself a noisy outcome, and the model behind the break-even ratio relates a check to the patch's replay success rate $p$. Estimated that way from all 2{,}160 bank pairs per domain, the bank judge has sensitivity 0.49--0.55 and false-positive rate 0.06--0.07, and its ratio is 0.88 $[0.46,1.53]$ on retail and 1.25 $[0.19,3.95]$ on airline; for the cheaper judges, estimated from 360 pairs, the intervals are wider still. Ratios also differ across categories (0 to 2.4). What the measurements support is therefore that judge checks are not the cheap, informative evidence multi-fidelity designs assume, not that they are less informative than replays. Their false-positive rate is no higher for bad patches than for good ones. Caching, batching and deterministic checks were not tested.

\paragraph{Replays need controls.} Without any patch, 20--23\% of replays from the failing step succeed anyway, and the rate ranges from 0 to 0.5 across categories. Deciding one edge takes 5--7 replays even with the right $b_i$.

\paragraph{No average harm is resolved, and per-patch harm is not measured.} Against the task-matched unpatched baseline, 5 of 121 patches are significantly harmful and 9 helpful on previously solved tasks (one-sided 95\%), about what chance would produce. Pooled over all patches, the excess failure rate is $-0.003$ on retail and $-0.006$ on airline, with 90\% intervals of $\pm0.03$--$0.04$. With $r_0$ from paired training re-runs instead, the intervals were twice as wide, and on retail that $r_0$ was biased low (0.09 vs.\ 0.14). Only 3--6 patches per domain are shown to lie within $\pm0.10$ (TOST), and on retail the hierarchical model estimates a spread of $\tau\approx0.04$ between patches, about 0.1 of the failure mass, comparable to what an effective edge repairs; measuring it per patch would take hundreds of runs (Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}).

\subsection{Policies on the banks}

\paragraph{Posterior expected value.} Table~\ref{tab:tau2} reports each policy's posterior expected value for deployable sets. With $\mu$ near zero, the ranking is decided by which patches each policy happens to apply. On retail, \carve{} with a robust check (1.22 at budget 40) and uncertainty sampling with checks (1.01 at 10) lead and applying everything scores 0.77; verifying policies beat it in 35--69\% of draws, and with $c_{\mathrm w}=0.02$ applying everything falls to 0.44 (Table~\ref{tab:tau2cw}). On airline, applying everything (0.73) and \carve{} with replays only (0.74--0.83) lead at $c=0$, and applying everything is the single best policy in 18--40\% of draws. Most intervals span about one oracle or more (Table~\ref{tab:robust}), so the policies are statistically indistinguishable: what remains uncertain is each patch's own $\rho_k$ and the adjudication of edges.

\paragraph{Separating evidence from assessment.} Reusing one bank for both the policies' evidence and the outcome labels makes the labels optimistic for policies that select on the same replays. When policies see one half of every list of outcomes and are scored only on the other (right blocks of Table~\ref{tab:tau2}), every value falls, because each half supports fewer effective edges (3--5 on retail, 3--9 on airline), but the picture does not change. On retail, applying everything scores 0.29 and the best verifying policies 0.84--0.93 (\carve{}, uncertainty sampling with checks), yet they beat it in only 50--60\% of draws, and most 90\% intervals span two oracles or more. On airline, applying everything scores 0.67, no verifying policy exceeds it on average, and it is the single best policy in 30--39\% of draws. Independent evidence on the banks therefore leaves the ranking as open as reused evidence does.


\paragraph{Robustness.} Table~\ref{tab:robust} (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}) rescores the budget-40 decisions. (i) \emph{Adjudication is unstable.} On held-back halves only 1--3 of retail's 4 effective edges and 3--7 of airline's 7 are confirmed, and bootstrapping the replays yields anywhere from 2 to 19 (retail) and 4 to 19 (airline) edges. (ii) \emph{Retail's value is mostly the step limit.} Without the step-limit component, every retail policy falls below zero, while airline keeps 83\% of its oracle. (iii) \emph{Deployment constraints matter}: under significant-harm scoring, one patch per component raises applying everything from 0.16 to 0.47 (retail) and 0.15 to 0.46 (airline). 

\subsection{Held-out tasks}


Table~\ref{tab:heldout} applies each policy's patch set to the agent on unseen tasks, which tests whole sets, interactions included, with runs independent of the bank. On retail, applying the attributed patches raises success from 0.76 to 0.88 ($+0.12\pm0.04$, paired, task-clustered); on airline, from 0.68 to 0.75 ($+0.07\pm0.06$). No improvement from verification was detected on either domain: every verified set differs from applying everything by $-0.04$ to $0.00$ overall. This is not an equivalence result. With a non-inferiority margin of 0.05 in success, chosen after the fact, applying everything is non-inferior to 8 of the 10 verified sets on retail and to the two HarnessFix-style sets on airline, but not to \carve{} or the net-effect method there, whose intervals reach $+0.08$. Retail ablations are in Appendix~\ref{app:ablate}: the step-limit patch helps only on step-limit-fault episodes, and on 158 clean episodes the sets are indistinguishable. This evidence is narrow (one budget, 40 and 20 tasks, five airline policies, mostly injected faults): it shows that no benefit was detected here, and its intervals still allow gains of up to $+0.06$ (retail) and $+0.08$ (airline).

\paragraph{From replays to deployment.} Replays measure repair from the failing step; deployed patches act from the first step. The bank's repaired failure mass times the held-out failure rate predicts airline's held-out gains closely (Apply $+0.064$ vs.\ $+0.067$ observed, \carve{} and net-effect within 0.001) but half of retail's ($+0.054$ vs.\ $+0.117$), mostly because the step-limit patch adds $+0.07$ from the start against $+0.01$ predicted.


\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The operating regime we can characterise.} With analyst precision 0.18--0.33, spurious recovery about 0.2, a repairable gain of 0.07--0.11, and patch effects on solved tasks of mean $0\pm0.03$ and spread $\le0.04$, perfect information about every patch is worth 0.06--0.33 (retail) and 0.08--0.13 (airline) in absolute success over applying the attributed patches, depending on the harm model. Whether verification captures any of it was tested only at budget 10. Drawing each verified set's held-out gain from its task-clustered uncertainty, adding the change cost it saves by applying fewer patches ($c_{\mathrm a}\sim U[0,0.02]$), and charging its episodes once, the probability that it has paid back after 1{,}000 future episodes is 0.02--0.45 (retail) and 0.18--0.44 (airline), and that it ever pays back 0.05--0.56 and 0.23--0.51 (Table~\ref{tab:payback}). In this regime ten episodes of verification are at best a coin flip. With a less precise analyst or larger harms, such as ShopDesk's formatting patch (Appendix~\ref{app:shopdesk}), the harm term grows, and only regression runs and bundle tests measure it.

\paragraph{A framework, not a decision rule.} Proposition~\ref{prop:vpi} names the quantities that could make verification pay, but it cannot decide whether to verify a new diagnosis: its most influential input, each patch's harm, takes hundreds of regression runs to measure (Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}), and its values move several-fold with the harm model.

\section{Conclusion}
With our agent and the four judges we tried, no verification evidence was cheap, no average patch effect on solved tasks was resolved, policy rankings remain unidentified whether or not evidence and assessment share a bank, and on held-out tasks no gain from verification was detected at budget 10.

\section*{Limitations}
We study one agent model and one analyst model on three customer-service settings, and four judge configurations built from those two models; caching, batching, fine-tuned or deterministic checks were not tested. The banks have 36 cells per domain, and their 4 and 7 effective edges are only partly confirmed on held-back replays; our edges are effective interventions, not established causes. Regression estimates rest on 20 runs per patch; the unpatched baseline (10 runs per task) was collected about 12 hours after them, and only held-out success checks for drift between the two; the hierarchical posterior assumes normally distributed patch effects and predicts more spread between patches than observed (Appendix~\ref{app:harm}). Effects are treated as additive within a deployable set, apart from repairs in one category; whole sets are tested on held-out tasks at budget 10 only, with 60 episodes per set on airline, and the leave-one-out runs are too noisy to measure interactions. Most of retail's held-out gain on fault-injected episodes comes from the step-limit fix. Most failures come from injected faults written to resemble real defects (natural failures are 16--25\%), and reweighting to natural failures is not a bank built from them. We evaluate a single round of improvement under fixed budgets, not a multi-round loop or a deployment horizon. The net-effect method was developed on retail after we had seen airline results for the other policies, although not its own. All edge priors use an analyst accuracy of 0.7 rather than the audited values, and some inputs (experiment costs, mean spurious recovery, judge calibration) come uncharged from the bank. Replay from a failing step requires deterministic tools.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Self-improving agents change their own behaviour without a person reviewing each change. That no improvement from verification was detected on our held-out tasks, and that no average harm to other tasks was resolved, does not show that verification is unnecessary, and our measurements cannot exclude small harms per patch; it should not be read as licence to skip review. On ShopDesk a single patch silently broke a quarter of tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:shopdesk}). Our environments use synthetic customer data only.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:vpi}.} For any signal $Y$ and any action set, $\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)\mid Y]\le\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)\mid Y]$, so taking expectations gives $V(Y)\le\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)]-\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]$, the value of perfect information; this is the standard argument and needs no assumption on how $Y$ relates to $\Delta$. With $U(S)=\sum_{k\in S}\Delta_k$ and at most one patch per cell, both maxima separate over cells: $\max_S U(S)=\sum_{ij}\max_k\Delta_k^+$ and $\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]=\sum_{ij}\max_k(\mathbb E\Delta_k)^+$, which gives $V_{ij}$; no independence between cells is needed. For Eq.~\ref{eq:vpi}, let $k^\star$ maximise $\mathbb E\Delta_k$. If $\mathbb E\Delta_{k^\star}\ge0$, then $V_{ij}=\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+]-\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^+]+\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^-]$, which is Eq.~\ref{eq:vpi} with equality. Otherwise $V_{ij}=\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+]=\mathbb E[\max_k\Delta_k^+-\Delta_{k^\star}^+]+\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^+]$, and $\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^+]<\mathbb E[\Delta_{k^\star}^-]$ because $\mathbb E\Delta_{k^\star}<0$. Finally, the repair term $Ff_ie_{ij}q_k$ is non-negative, so $\Delta_k^-\le(1-F)\rho_k^++c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}(1-e_{ij})$; take expectations.

\paragraph{Heuristic defaults and constraints.} For a default $D$, $\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)]-\mathbb E[U(D)]=\big(\mathbb E[\max_S U(S)]-\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]\big)+\big(\max_S\mathbb E[U(S)]-\mathbb E[U(D)]\big)$, and the second bracket is $G_D\ge0$ by definition of the maximum. Restricting $S$ to deployable sets leaves the argument unchanged. For repairs in one category, the Bonferroni inequality gives $1-\prod_k(1-q_k)\ge\sum_kq_k-\sum_{k<l}q_kq_l$, which bounds the overstatement of the additive form. The bound on $-\sum_k\rho_k$ holds because the patched failure rate $r_0+\sum_k\rho_k$ on solved tasks is non-negative under additivity. For (iii), removing patch $k$ from a set $S$ changes its value by $-\big[\mathrm{rep}(S)-\mathrm{rep}(S\setminus k)\big]+(1-F)\big[\bar\rho(S)-\bar\rho(S\setminus k)\big]+c_{\mathrm a}+c_{\mathrm w}(1-e_{ij})$, where $\mathrm{rep}$ is the product-form repair and $\bar\rho$ the capped sum of harms. Repair is monotone in $S$, so the first bracket is non-negative, and capping $\sum\rho$ from below changes it by at most $\rho_k^+$ when $k$ is removed; summing over the dropped patches and taking expectations gives the bound. The greedy optimiser keeps, per component, the cell and patch with the largest positive net effect, which is a feasible deployable set, so the value it attains is a lower bound on the constrained optimum.

\paragraph{Corollary~\ref{cor:edge}.} With one patch, $V=\mathbb E[\Delta^+]-(\mathbb E\Delta)^+=\pi G-(\pi G-(1-\pi)H)^+=\min(\pi G,(1-\pi)H)$. The assumption that $\Delta$ is a function of $e_{ij}$ is what makes the edge state sufficient: if $q_k$ or $\rho_k$ is uncertain given $e_{ij}$, learning only $e_{ij}$ leaves value on the table and the general expression applies.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}.} The first part is Wald's approximation for a sequential probability ratio test between $p_1=b+(1-b)q$ and $p_0=b$: with thresholds $\pm\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}$, neglecting overshoot, the expected sample size under $p_1$ is the threshold divided by the per-observation drift $\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)$. The second is the normal-approximation sample size of a one-sided test of a binomial proportion, evaluated with the variance at $r_0$.

\section{Policy Inputs and Regression Sampling}
\label{app:inputs}

Attribution, taxonomy induction, labelling and candidate patches are shared by all policies and not charged to any budget. Every policy that uses an edge prior uses Eq.~\ref{eq:prior} with analyst accuracy $\hat r=0.7$, not the audited 0.41 and 0.29 of Table~\ref{tab:measured}; with the audited values the Bayes default is unchanged at $c=0$, and at $c_{\mathrm w}=0.02$ it applies 2 cells instead of 4--6 and scores 0.33 (retail) and 0.30 (airline) of the oracle instead of 0.27 and 0.63. Three further inputs come from the bank itself and are also uncharged: the cost of each kind of experiment (mean tokens), the mean spurious recovery $\hat b$, which \carve{} and uncertainty sampling plug in and the net-effect method uses only as a prior mean for its per-category $b_i$, and, for the two \carve{} variants that use them, the judge's measured sensitivity and false-positive rate. The re-run failure rate $r_0$ comes from paired re-runs of training tasks. \emph{Regression tasks} are drawn uniformly, with replacement, from the clean training episodes the agent solved, so they follow the training-task distribution of the same domain, not the held-out tasks, which also include fault-injected episodes. The extension to 20 runs covered every patch an original policy accepted; 98--100\% of the patches that any policy, including the later net-effect method, deploys at budget 40 have 20 runs, and a policy asking for more runs of a 4-run patch resamples those 4.

\section{Harm Model, Checks and Sensitivity}
\label{app:harm}
\paragraph{Model.} For patch $k$ with $n_k$ regression runs and $m_k$ failures, let $r_{0,k}$ be the unpatched failure rate of the tasks its runs used, averaged with the same task weights (10 unpatched runs per task). We set $y_k=m_k/n_k-r_{0,k}$ and use a normal approximation $y_k\sim N(\rho_k,v_k)$ with $v_k=\bar p(1-\bar p)/n_k$, $\bar p$ the pooled patch failure rate, and $\rho_k\sim N(\mu,\tau^2)$. $\tau^2$ is estimated by the method of moments and truncated at zero; given $\tau^2$, $\mu$ has a flat prior and a normal posterior, and each $\rho_k$ the conjugate normal posterior; patches without runs take $\rho_k=\mu$. Each of 300 draws resamples the unpatched runs within each task (a per-task Jeffreys posterior would add about $0.5/11$ to every task without failures and bias the baseline upwards), draws $\mu$ and $\rho$, clips $\rho_k$ at $-r_{0,k}$, and caps the set's total at $-\bar r_0$. Tasks are shared across patches; the \emph{tasks resampled} variant resamples tasks before fitting, which propagates that dependence.

\paragraph{Posterior predictive check.} On the patches with 20 runs, the observed standard deviation of failure rates is 0.076 (retail) and 0.066 (airline); replicated data from the fitted model give 0.092 and 0.081, with $P(\text{replicated}\ge\text{observed})=0.95$ and $0.97$, and the same for new patches drawn from $N(\mu,\tau^2)$. The model therefore predicts more spread between patches than the data show, which makes harm uncertainty, and the harm and choice terms of Table~\ref{tab:vpi}, conservative (too large). The number of patches with no failure and the largest failure rate are reproduced (posterior predictive $p$ of 0.91 and 0.99 on retail, 0.73 and 0.93 on airline, so the largest rates are slightly overpredicted as well).

\label{app:cproblem}

An agent is built from \emph{components} $j\in\{1,\dots,J\}$: sections of its system prompt, tool descriptions, tool implementations, orchestration settings. Its failures have been grouped into \emph{categories} $i\in\{1,\dots,I\}$ by an LLM, with $f_i$ the share of failures in category $i$. For every cell $(i,j)$ an analyst LLM can propose $K$ candidate patches $a_{ijk}$ that rewrite component $j$ to address category $i$.

\paragraph{Interventions.} An intervention $(i,j,k,\phi)$ samples a failed trajectory labelled $i$, applies patch $a_{ijk}$, and observes a binary outcome at fidelity $\phi$. A \emph{full replay} ($\phi=F$) resumes the episode from the attributed failing step and records whether the task now succeeds. A \emph{single-step check} ($\phi=S$) regenerates only the failing step and asks a judge whether the diagnosed error is gone. They cost $c_F$ and $c_S\ll c_F$.

\paragraph{Edges and patch quality.} We write $e_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ for whether component $j$ is a cause of category $i$, meaning some change to $j$ repairs a non-trivial fraction of its failures, and $q_{ijk}\in[0,1]$ for the repair probability of patch $k$ when $e_{ij}=1$. This separation matters because a negative replay outcome is ambiguous: either $j$ is the wrong component or $a_{ijk}$ is a bad patch. Testing several patches per cell resolves the ambiguity.

\paragraph{Objective.} After spending at most budget $B$, the method accepts a set of cells $\mathcal{A}$ and, for each, a patch $k^\star_{ij}$. Accepting a true edge repairs a fraction $q_{ijk^\star}$ of category $i$'s failures; accepting a false edge costs a regression $c_{\mathrm{fp}}$, reflecting that unnecessary changes perturb behaviour on tasks that used to succeed \citep{vasudev2026intervention}. We measure
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
G(\mathcal{A}) = {}&\sum_i f_i\Big(1-\!\!\prod_{j\in\mathcal{A}_i^+}\!\!(1-q_{ijk^\star_{ij}})\Big)\\
&- c_{\mathrm{fp}}\,\big|\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}:e_{ij}=0\}\big|,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_i^+=\{j:(i,j)\in\mathcal{A},\,e_{ij}=1\}$. This is the net failure mass repaired; we report it as a fraction of the oracle value obtained by accepting exactly the true edges with their best patches.


\subsection{\carve{}}
\label{app:cmethod}

\paragraph{Observation model}
For a trajectory labelled $i$ and patch $a_{ijk}$, the probability that a full replay succeeds is
\begin{equation}
p_{ijk} = \begin{cases} \lambda\, q_{ijk} + (1-\lambda)\, b & e_{ij}=1,\\ b & e_{ij}=0,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the spurious recovery rate (stochastic re-generation sometimes succeeds regardless of the patch) and $\lambda$ is the accuracy of the category labels used to sample trajectories; a mislabelled trajectory is treated as unaffected by the patch. A single-step check returns $z=1$ with probability $s\,p+\epsilon\,(1-p)$, where $s$ and $\epsilon$ are the sensitivity and false-positive rate of the check relative to the full outcome. All four nuisance parameters can be estimated from a small calibration set on which both fidelities and a human audit are run.

\paragraph{Attribution-informed prior}
Let $n_{ij}$ be the number of failures in category $i$ that the analyst attributed to component $j$, and $\hat r$ the analyst's attribution accuracy estimated on a small audited sample. With $d$ the expected number of causal components per category and $\rho=d/J$ the base edge density, we set
\begin{equation}
\pi_{ij} = (1-\hat r)\,\rho + \hat r\,\min\!\big(1,\ d\, n_{ij}/n_i\big),
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
so that an unreliable analyst ($\hat r\to0$) yields the flat prior $\rho$ and a reliable one concentrates mass on the components it names. Eq.~\ref{eq:prior} is a heuristic interpolation, not a calibrated model; with a larger audit, $\pi$ could instead be fitted by logistic regression on audited attributions. Patch quality has prior $q_{ijk}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_q,\beta_q)$.

\paragraph{Posterior}
Cells are independent given the data. For each cell we keep the posterior log-odds of $e_{ij}$ and, for each patch, a posterior over $q_{ijk}$ on a fixed grid of $G=101$ points. With $\mathcal{D}_{ijk}$ the outcomes observed for patch $k$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{logit}\,P(e_{ij}{=}1\mid\mathcal{D}) ={}& \mathrm{logit}\,\pi_{ij}\\
&+ \textstyle\sum_k \log\big(\bar L^{1}_{ijk}/L^{0}_{ijk}\big),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\bar L^{1}_{ijk}=\int P(\mathcal{D}_{ijk}\mid q,e_{ij}{=}1)\,dP(q)$ is the marginal likelihood of patch $k$'s outcomes under a true edge and $L^{0}_{ijk}=P(\mathcal{D}_{ijk}\mid e_{ij}{=}0)$.
Every update multiplies one grid vector by a likelihood vector, so updates and look-aheads are vectorised dot products.

\paragraph{Selecting interventions}
The value of the current decision for cell $(i,j)$, with $P_{ij}$ its edge posterior and $\hat Q_{ij}=\max_k\mathbb{E}[q_{ijk}\mid\mathcal{D}]$, is
\begin{equation}
V_{ij} = \max\big(P_{ij}\,f_i\,\hat Q_{ij} - (1-P_{ij})\,c_{\mathrm{fp}},\ 0\big),
\end{equation}
the expected contribution of the cell to Eq.~\ref{eq:gain} under the Bayes decision (accept iff the first term is positive), treating cells as additive. Eq.~\ref{eq:gain} combines components of one category multiplicatively, so $V_{ij}$ slightly overstates the value of a second cause for an already repaired category; we accept this approximation because it keeps cells independent. For an action $a=(i,j,k,\phi)$ repeated $n$ times, let $V'_{ij}(s)$ be the value after observing $s$ successes. The $n$-step knowledge gradient is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KG}_n(a) = \sum_{s=0}^{n} P(s\mid\mathcal{D},a)\,V'_{ij}(s) - V_{ij},
\end{equation}
and \carve{} runs the action maximising
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(a) = \max_{n\in\{1,2,4,8\}} \frac{\mathrm{KG}_n(a)}{n\,c_\phi}.
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Looking ahead several observations matters because a single binary outcome often cannot flip a decision, in which case $\mathrm{KG}_1=0$ even for a valuable cell; maximising over $n$ is the standard remedy \citep{frazier2008kg}. Because cells are independent, only the observed cell's scores change after each outcome, so each step rescores one cell in $O(KG)$ time after an initial pass over all cells.

\paragraph{Decisions and stopping.} At any point the method accepts the cells whose current value is positive and applies, for each, the patch with the highest posterior mean quality. It stops early when no action has a score above a small threshold, i.e.\ when no remaining experiment is expected to be worth its cost.


\subsection{When Do Priors and Cheap Checks Help?}
\label{app:canalysis}

These results analyse an idealised per-cell sequential test, not \carve{}'s full policy; they explain which quantities govern the method's behaviour. We consider a single cell and a single patch with known success probabilities $p_1$ (true edge) and $p_0=b$, under a rule that stops when the posterior log-odds leaves $[-A,A]$, $A=\log\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}$. Let $D_1=\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)$ and $D_0=\mathrm{KL}(p_0\Vert p_1)$ be the expected log-likelihood-ratio drift per observation under each hypothesis. Proofs, which follow Wald's approximations \citep{wald1945sequential}, are in Appendix~\ref{app:cproofs}.

\begin{proposition}[Value of the LLM prior]
\label{prop:cprior}
Let $|\ell|<A$ and neglect overshoot and stopping at the wrong boundary (whose probability is approximately $\delta e^{-\ell}$ for a true edge and $\delta e^{\ell}$ for a false one). A cell with initial log-odds $\ell$ then needs $N_1(\ell)=(A-\ell)/D_1$ observations in expectation if it is a true edge and $N_0(\ell)=(A+\ell)/D_0$ if not. Replacing a flat prior $\ell_\rho$ by LLM-derived log-odds $\ell_{ij}$ saves, in expectation over cells,
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \frac{\rho\,\mathbb{E}[\delta\ell\mid e{=}1]}{D_1} - \frac{(1-\rho)\,\mathbb{E}[\delta\ell\mid e{=}0]}{D_0},
\end{equation}
with $\delta\ell=\ell_{ij}-\ell_\rho$,
observations per cell, which is positive exactly when the prior raises true edges more, relative to their base rate and drift, than it raises false ones.
\end{proposition}

Under Eq.~\ref{eq:prior}, $\ell_{ij}\to\ell_\rho$ as $\hat r\to0$, so $\Delta\to0$: a useless analyst costs nothing beyond the flat prior, whereas trusting its labels outright (LLM-only) makes permanent errors. A miscalibrated analyst can make $\Delta$ negative, but only by an amount linear in the log-odds shift, after which the evidence takes over. Appendix~\ref{app:prop1} compares these predictions with simulated sequential tests.

\begin{proposition}[Cheap checks]
\label{prop:cfid}
Let $D^S_e$ denote the drifts of single-step outcomes, $z\sim\mathrm{Bern}(s\,p_e+\epsilon(1-p_e))$. Under the same approximations, a test using only single-step checks reaches the stopping boundary at lower expected cost than one using only full replays if and only if
\begin{equation}
\frac{c_S}{c_F} < \frac{D^S_e}{D_e},
\end{equation}
where $e$ is the true state of the cell.
\end{proposition}

Since $e$ is unknown, the proposition gives a clear rule only when the cost ratio lies outside the interval spanned by the two critical ratios; inside it, which fidelity is better depends on the posterior, which is what \carve{}'s look-ahead weighs.

With our default parameters ($p_1=0.54$, $b=0.05$, $s=0.85$, $\epsilon=0.15$) the critical ratio is $0.31$ for true edges and $0.44$ for false ones; with a weak check ($s=0.65$, $\epsilon=0.35$) it falls to $0.05$--$0.08$. Section~\ref{app:cfid} tests both predictions.


\subsection{Controlled Experiments}
\label{app:csim}

Real agents do not reveal which component truly caused a failure, so we first evaluate in a testbed where the causal graph is known and the method's model is deliberately misspecified.

\paragraph{Setup}
\paragraph{Testbed.} Each world has $I=12$ categories with Zipfian frequencies, $J=20$ components and $K=3$ patches per cell. Every category has one or two true components and one \emph{decoy} component that wrong attributions prefer, mimicking symptom-level mislabelling. Patch quality is bimodal: with probability 0.3 a patch is bad ($q\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,9)$), otherwise $q\sim\mathrm{Beta}(6,3)$. A trajectory sampled for category $i$ is mislabelled with probability $1-\lambda=0.2$, in which case it comes from another category (weighted by frequency) and is repaired only if \emph{its own} edge to $j$ exists, unlike the model's flat treatment of mislabelled traces. The analyst attributes 20 failures per category with accuracy $r=0.5$; wrong attributions go to the decoy with probability 0.6. The analyst's accuracy is estimated from an audit of 20 attributions. Costs are $c_F=1$, $c_S=0.1$, $c_{\mathrm{fp}}=0.02$, and $s=0.85$, $\epsilon=0.15$, $b=0.05$. Section~\ref{app:crobust} relaxes the assumptions the generator shares with the model. Full details are in Appendix~\ref{app:sim}.

\paragraph{Baselines.} \emph{LLM-only} accepts, for every category, the most-attributed component and any component holding at least 40\% of the attributions, with the first patch, i.e.\ it acts on an induced taxonomy without verification. \emph{Replay-each} tests attributed hypotheses in order of attribution count with three full replays of the first patch and accepts a hypothesis if at least two succeed, mirroring do-then-verify methods such as DoVer; \emph{Replay-each, 3 patches} gives each hypothesis two replays of each of three patches and accepts if any patch succeeds twice; \emph{Sequential-each} replays each hypothesis, cycling through patches, until \carve{}'s own posterior leaves $[0.05,0.95]$. Untested hypotheses fall back to LLM-only, except for Sequential-each, which uses the Bayes decision. \emph{Thompson sampling} samples $(e,q)$ from the posterior and replays the cell and patch with the largest sampled repair. \emph{Uncertainty sampling} replays the cell with the largest frequency-weighted uncertainty $f_i\min(P_{ij},1-P_{ij})$, cycling through patches, at full fidelity; \emph{Uncertainty + cheap checks} first runs three single-step checks per (cell, patch) and then confirms with full replays. All baselines except LLM-only and the Replay-each variants share \carve{}'s posterior and decision rule, so comparisons among them isolate the selection rule.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Net gain (Eq.~\ref{eq:gain}) as a fraction of the oracle, and edge F1. We report means and standard errors over 50 worlds for the main experiment and 30 for the others; all methods see the same worlds, so differences are paired.

Figure~\ref{fig:budget} and Table~\ref{tab:main} show that \carve{} is best at every budget. With 50 units it recovers 79\% of the oracle gain; the strongest baseline, uncertainty sampling with the same cheap checks, recovers 66\% and needs about twice the budget (100 units, 78\%) to match it. The gap narrows at large budgets (90\% vs.\ 88\% at 200), as every reasonable method eventually resolves the frequent categories.

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.} Two comparisons separate the selection rule from the cheap checks. With full replays only, \carve{} beats uncertainty sampling by 1--6 points (e.g.\ 0.609 vs.\ 0.581 at budget 50): the knowledge-gradient rule alone helps only modestly. Given cheap checks, its advantage grows to 7--13 points (0.787 vs.\ 0.662 at 50), because it decides per action whether a check is informative enough for its price and stops checking a cell once checks can no longer change the decision, whereas a fixed check-then-confirm schedule wastes checks on resolved cells. Most of \carve{}'s advantage is therefore in \emph{how it uses} cheap checks, which also makes their calibration critical (\S\ref{app:crobust}).

\paragraph{Verification baselines.} LLM-only is stuck at 30\% because it accepts decoys and bad first patches. Replay-each is initially \emph{worse} than not verifying: with $p_1\approx0.54$, requiring two successes in three replays rejects about 44\% of true edges even when the patch is good, and its fixed per-hypothesis cost exhausts small budgets on frequent but already-certain hypotheses. Testing three patches (61\% at 200) or stopping adaptively (81\% at 200) helps, but both remain well below \carve{} at small budgets (32\% and 52\% at 50). Thompson sampling spends most replays on cells it already believes in and reaches only 63\% at 200.

\paragraph{Ablations.} Removing single-step checks costs 15--18 points at small budgets. Restricting the look-ahead to one observation is below the full method at every budget and plateaus at 73\%, because many cells cannot be flipped by a single outcome and are never revisited. A flat prior costs 32 points at budget 20 and still 10 points at 200. With one patch per cell the method plateaus at 69\%: it cannot tell a wrong component from a bad patch and rejects true edges for good. Dropping the label-noise term makes no difference at the default label accuracy $\lambda=0.8$ (it is even slightly better at large budgets, 0.922 vs.\ 0.902 at 200), but it matters when labels are worse (\S\ref{app:crobust}).

\paragraph{When does the LLM prior help?}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/prior_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Net gain at budget 50 as the analyst's attribution accuracy $r$ varies (30 worlds, $\pm1$ s.e.).}
\label{fig:prior}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:prior} varies the analyst's accuracy $r$. As Proposition~\ref{prop:cprior} suggests, \carve{} improves monotonically with $r$ and is never below the flat-prior variant: at $r=0.1$ it scores $0.51\pm0.02$ against $0.48\pm0.03$. The prior is not exactly flat there (the decoy still receives about twice the base rate), but its shifts are small, so evidence quickly dominates. Acting on attributions directly is harmful below $r\approx0.35$, where LLM-only has a \emph{negative} net gain (regressions outweigh repairs), and even at $r=0.95$ it reaches only 71\% of the oracle against 96\% for \carve{}, since it cannot detect bad patches. Replay-each improves with $r$ and only matches the flat-prior variant of \carve{} at $r=0.95$ ($0.48$ vs.\ $0.48$).

\paragraph{When do cheap checks help?}
\label{app:cfid}

Figure~\ref{fig:fid} (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}) is qualitatively consistent with Proposition~\ref{prop:cfid}. With the default check, the benefit of cheap checks shrinks as their cost grows and vanishes between 0.35 and 0.5 ($0.64\pm0.02$ vs.\ $0.61\pm0.03$ at 0.35, overlapping; $0.59$ vs.\ $0.61$ at 0.5), in line with the predicted critical ratios of 0.31 and 0.44. At cost 0.1, a check of quality $s-\epsilon=0.5$ (critical ratio 0.14--0.22) still helps, while one of quality 0.3 (critical ratio 0.05--0.08) brings no gain. At cost 0.5 \carve{} is slightly below its full-replay-only variant although its action set contains every full-replay action, which shows that the greedy rule is not exactly cost-optimal when the two fidelities are nearly equally efficient.

The generator shares several assumptions with the model: check errors depend only on the full outcome, cells are independent, the analyst's accuracy is estimated without bias, and the regression cost is small. Table~\ref{tab:stress} and Appendix~\ref{app:extra} relax each in turn.

\paragraph{Worse labels.} With label accuracy $\lambda=0.5$ both methods lose ground, and the label-noise term now matters: \carve{} scores 0.688, against 0.588 without the term and 0.513 for the baseline.

\paragraph{Biased analyst audit.} If the audit overstates the analyst's accuracy by 0.3, \carve{} actually improves (0.857), because a stronger prior concentrates the budget and the evidence corrects the decoys. Understating it by 0.3 costs 12 points (0.653), still above the baseline. Under-trusting a decent analyst is thus the more costly error in this testbed.

\paragraph{Regression cost.} Raising $c_{\mathrm{fp}}$ from 0.005 to 0.1 leaves \carve{} between 0.78 and 0.82, while LLM-only falls from 0.39 to $-0.13$: the more costly a wrong change, the more verification is worth.

\paragraph{Joint causes.} When 30\% of categories need two components changed together (a single patch then repairs only a fifth as often), violating cell independence, \carve{} drops from 0.787 to 0.661 at budget 50 but stays ahead of uncertainty sampling with checks (0.558; Table~\ref{tab:joint}).

\paragraph{Correlated check errors.} The one setting where \carve{} loses is when the single-step judge is fooled by plausible-looking bad patches (false-positive rate 0.6 for patches with $q<0.3$). \carve{} then trusts checks that systematically pass bad patches and falls to 0.377, below every method that relies on full replays (0.58--0.61). Modelling the dependence (a quality-dependent false-positive rate in the likelihood) restores it to 0.584, on par with full replays, at a cost of 9 points when the judge is not fooled (0.684 vs.\ 0.776). Whether real judges behave this way is therefore the most important quantity to measure before relying on cheap checks.

\paragraph{Nuisance parameters and scale.} Misspecifying $b$, $\lambda$ or the patch-quality prior changes the result by at most 5 points (Table~\ref{tab:misspec}, Appendix~\ref{app:extra}); misstating the check's sensitivity and false-positive rate costs 6--14 points. On a larger graph ($I=20$, $J=50$; Table~\ref{tab:scale}) the gap widens: at budget 50 \carve{} reaches 78\% of the oracle against 60\% for uncertainty sampling with checks.

\paragraph{Inside a self-improvement loop}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/rsi.pdf}
\caption{Agent success over six rounds of attribute--verify--patch, with a budget of 40 units per round (30 worlds, $\pm1$ s.e.).}
\label{fig:rsi}
\end{figure}

Finally we embed each method in a loop. The agent starts at 40\% success. In each round the analyst attributes 240 new failures, sampled in proportion to the current failure distribution; the method spends 40 units; accepted patches are applied, which repairs failures (true edges) or adds a regression of $c_{\mathrm{fp}}$ times the current failure mass (false edges); and the failure distribution is recomputed, so priorities shift towards categories that remain. Posteriors carry over between rounds.

Figure~\ref{fig:rsi} shows that \carve{} reaches 80\% success after one round and 85\% after two, close to its plateau of 86\% (the oracle ceiling is 91.5\%). Uncertainty sampling with checks needs four rounds to reach 84\% and ends at 85\%; without checks it needs six. LLM-only and Replay-each plateau at 61\% and 66\%: once their accepted false edges and rejected true edges are fixed in place, new attributions do not correct them.


\subsection{Proofs for \carve{}'s propositions}
\label{app:cproofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:cprior}.} Let $L_t=\ell+\sum_{u\le t}\log\frac{P(o_u\mid e=1)}{P(o_u\mid e=0)}$ be the posterior log-odds after $t$ observations. Under $e=1$, the increments are i.i.d.\ with mean $D_1=\mathrm{KL}(p_1\Vert p_0)>0$. The rule stops at $N=\inf\{t: L_t\notin(-A,A)\}$. By Wald's identity, $\mathbb{E}_1[L_N-\ell]=D_1\,\mathbb{E}_1[N]$. Wald's approximations give $P_1(L_N\le -A)\approx e^{-(A+\ell)}\approx\delta e^{-\ell}$ and $P_0(L_N\ge A)\approx\delta e^{\ell}$. Neglecting overshoot and this probability (which requires $\delta e^{|\ell|}\ll1$), $L_N=A$, hence $\mathbb{E}_1[N]=(A-\ell)/D_1$; if $\ell\ge A$ no observation is taken, which is why we assume $|\ell|<A$. Symmetrically, under $e=0$ the drift is $-D_0$ and $\mathbb{E}_0[N]=(A+\ell)/D_0$. Taking the difference between $\ell_\rho$ and $\ell_{ij}$ and averaging over cells with $P(e{=}1)=\rho$ gives $\Delta$. When the patch quality $q$ is unknown, outcomes are exchangeable rather than i.i.d.\ under the marginal likelihood, and Wald's identity does not apply directly; conditioning on $q$ recovers the result with $D_1=D_1(q)$, which approaches zero for a bad patch. This is the formal reason a single bad patch can stall verification and why \carve{} keeps several patches per cell.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:cfid}.} By the same argument a single-step test needs $(A\mp\ell)/D^S_e$ observations at cost $c_S$ each, and a full-replay test needs $(A\mp\ell)/D_e$ at cost $c_F$. The single-step test is cheaper iff $c_S/D^S_e<c_F/D_e$. Since the single-step outcome is a garbling of the full outcome, the data-processing inequality gives $D^S_e\le D_e$, so the critical ratio is at most one.

\subsection{Simulation Details}
\label{app:sim}

Category frequencies are $f_i\propto 1/i$. For each category the number of true components is uniform on $\{1,2\}$ and the decoy is a further distinct component. The audit estimate $\hat r$ is a binomial proportion from 20 audited attributions. The prior uses $d=1.5$. The model's grid has 101 points; look-ahead horizons are $\{1,2,4,8\}$; the stopping threshold is $10^{-6}$. Replay-each uses three replays and accepts with at least two successes. Uncertainty sampling switches to an untried patch once the current patch has three full observations. In the loop experiment, 240 attributions per round are allocated to categories by a multinomial draw with the current failure distribution. All randomness is seeded; the code reproduces every number in this paper with \texttt{python -m sim.experiments all}.
