\title{Auditing Verification Decisions in Agent Self-Improvement}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Self-improving agent loops change an agent's harness (its prompts, tools and code) and keep a change only when verification evidence says it helps. We audit these decisions one candidate at a time: inside RRSI, a self-improvement loop that edits harness code, run with DeepSeek models on $\tau^2$-bench and AppWorld, we deploy every measured candidate, accepted or rejected, on held-out tasks and account for what its evidence cost. Most candidate-level effects remained unresolved: unadjusted 95\% intervals excluded zero positively for 8 of the 183 measured candidates and negatively for 12. Evidence that never ran the changed agent on solved tasks would have accepted changes that crashed the harness or blocked legitimate actions, and no rule's verification was shown to pay for itself against keeping the incumbent. A cheaper rule that stops evaluating a candidate once RRSI's rule is predicted to reject it used 57\% of full re-evaluation's episodes but dropped a quarter of its acceptances; a post hoc diagnosis traces this to stops that treated an estimated cost change as known. It still passed a registered non-inferiority test on recorded decisions, as keeping the incumbent would. The independent-loop comparison did not establish non-inferiority at its preregistered five-point margin ($-4.4$ points, 95\% interval $-9.6$ to $+0.7$). Under shared calibration, the estimated difference was $-0.8$ points (95\% interval $-3.4$ to $+1.7$); this experiment did not preregister the same non-inferiority test. Its loops cost 10--12\% less (not statistically established). These findings concern one loop, one acceptance rule and one stopping rule. Cheaper verification should be reported with its recall of the reference's acceptances, the held-out value of those it drops and the calibration of its stops.
\end{abstract}

A growing family of systems improves LLM agents from their own failures. The agent runs, an analyst model reads the failed trajectories, a proposer changes the agent's \emph{harness}, the prompts, tool descriptions and code around a frozen model, and the loop keeps the change or discards it \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa,chen2026harnessfix,zhang2026ace,rrsi2026}. The check that decides is where these systems differ most: some replay the failure with the change applied \citep{ma2025dover,lin2026reflect,shah2026car}, some ask an LLM judge, some re-run tasks that used to pass \citep{chen2026harnessfix}, and RRSI re-evaluates every candidate twice on its whole training set \citep{rrsi2026}. What every check is meant to estimate is a change's \emph{deployment gain}, how much it raises success on tasks the loop has not seen, and that gain can differ from what the loop observes on its own tasks: RRSI reports in-distribution gains that shrink out of distribution \citep{rrsi2026}.

This paper asks two questions about verification evidence. \emph{Which evidence predicts a change's deployment gain?} And \emph{is the evidence worth what it costs?} Answering the first needs ground truth that verification does not provide: deploying the changes, from the first step, on independent tasks. Answering the second needs an account of what verification costs and what the change it selects is worth over a deployment horizon, which we write as $\mathrm{Net}(N)$ (\S\ref{sec:problem}). We price every piece of evidence in agent episodes and answer both questions in two studies, for one loop at a time: rather than rank kinds of evidence by how well they predict single changes, which test sets of this size cannot resolve (\S\ref{sec:e1res}), we audit individual verification decisions against deployment. RRSI already treats generalization and constrained selection as central \citep{rrsi2026}; we add an audit of each decision.

A preliminary study of one round of attribution-driven text patches on $\tau^2$-bench \citep{barres2025tau2} could not resolve differences between verification policies (Study 1, Appendix~\ref*{S-sec:study1}). \textbf{Study 2} (\S\ref{sec:study2}, registered) tests evidence with the power and the modification space Study 1 lacked. We run RRSI's open-source loop \citep{rrsi2026}, which edits harness code over many rounds, score each kind of evidence in its selection step, and deploy every candidate and incumbent on held-out tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Few candidates measurably helped held out; evidence that does not run the changed agent on solved tasks would have accepted a change that crashed the harness and one that blocked legitimate actions; and no rule was shown to outperform keeping the incumbent or to pay for its verification. Sequential evaluation, which stops a candidate's evaluation once RRSI's rule is predicted to reject it, used 57\% of full re-evaluation's episodes on the loop's recorded decisions but dropped a quarter of its acceptances, because its stops were overconfident where RRSI admits a change for being cheaper or new; it passed a registered non-inferiority test, as keeping the incumbent does. Six independent airline loops per arm driven by it did not establish non-inferiority to full evaluation's at the registered five-point margin: they gained 4.4 points less held out (95\% [$-9.6,+0.7$]), and their separately calibrated noise bands differed by chance. With one calibration shared by five loops in each of four blocks, sequential loops gained 0.8 points less (95\% [$-3.4,+1.7$]; this experiment registered no non-inferiority test), for 37\% fewer candidate-evaluation episodes but only 12\% lower loop cost, which the proposer dominates. An ablation that separates admission from the proposer's history resolved neither effect: the admission effect, $-1.6$ points, does not survive adjustment for multiple contrasts.

Our contributions are an audit of individual verification decisions, a methodological case study rather than a ranking of evidence or a new loop: a candidate-level evaluation protocol that deploys every candidate, accepted or rejected, with an explicit cost account; a diagnosis of why decision values against a reference that gains little cannot reveal lost acceptances, and of the miscalibrated stops that lose them; registered loop comparisons that examine admission, the proposer's history, calibration and total cost; and the environments, banks and evidence of both studies. The loop, its regularised edits and its selection rule are RRSI's \citep{rrsi2026}; we change only the evidence its selection step uses.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Self-improving agents and how they verify.}
Reflexion, Self-Refine and ExpeL turn feedback into verbal lessons \citep{shinn2023reflexion,madaan2023selfrefine,zhao2024expel}; ProTeGi, OPRO, TextGrad and DSPy rewrite prompts \citep{pryzant2023protegi,yang2024opro,yuksekgonul2025textgrad,khattab2024dspy}; ADAS, the Darwin G\"odel Machine and GEPA search over designs \citep{hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm,agrawal2025gepa}; ACE evolves a context playbook \citep{zhang2026ace}. HarnessFix repairs harness code and promotes a bundle of repairs only if it solves more validation tasks while breaking at most two \citep{chen2026harnessfix}; Bayesian-Agent applies threshold rules to posteriors over skills \citep{wu2026bayesianagent}. RRSI regularises harness self-improvement and selects by full re-evaluation against a calibrated noise floor \citep{rrsi2026}. These systems report the success of the final agent; we evaluate the evidence behind each decision.

\paragraph{Attribution and verification by intervention.}
Locating the responsible agent and step is far from solved \citep{zhang2025whichagent,liu2026whowhenpro}; attribution models, taxonomies and dependency tracing address it \citep{zhang2026agentracer,zhu2025agentdebug,li2026errorprobe,cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast}. DoVer validates hypotheses by editing a message or plan and re-running \citep{ma2025dover}; REFLECT tests patches by targeted replay \citep{lin2026reflect}; CausalFlow and Causal Agent Replay score step interventions causally \citep{bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}; AgentDebugX gates reruns by policy \citep{zhu2026agentdebugx}. Closest to our question, a failure critic with an AUROC of 0.94 lowered one agent's success by 26 points when its predictions triggered interventions within runs, because interventions also derail runs that would have succeeded \citep{vasudev2026intervention}. We study the analogous gap for harness changes selected over many rounds, between a rule's recorded decisions and the loops it drives. Program repair has long warned that a patch passing the failing test may not fix the fault \citep{smith2015overfitting}.

\paragraph{Sequential evaluation and racing.}
Hoeffding races and F-race discard configurations once a bound or a Friedman test separates them from the best \citep{maron1993hoeffding,birattari2002racing}; successive halving and Hyperband spread a budget over rounds of elimination \citep{jamieson2016successive,li2018hyperband}; fast cross-validation drops parameter settings by sequential tests across growing subsamples \citep{krueger2015fastcv}; clinical trials stop for futility when the predictive probability of a conclusive result is small \citep{spiegelhalter1986predictive}; and online experiments use always-valid inference so that continuously monitored A/B tests keep their error rates \citep{johari2017peeking}. Our sequential rule is a futility stop whose target is a self-improvement loop's admission rule, with its noise band, cost budget and novelty branch.

\section{Verification Evidence and Deployment Gain}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Changes and their deployment gain.} A loop holds an incumbent harness $h$ and proposes a change $c$: a rewritten prompt section or tool description, a code edit, or a set of these. Its \emph{deployment gain} $\Delta p(c)$ is the difference in success rate between $h$ with $c$ and $h$ alone, measured from the first step on tasks independent of everything the loop saw. A verification rule spends $C_{\mathrm{verify}}$ episodes on evidence, forms an estimate $\widehat{\Delta}$ of the change's effect and accepts the change if the estimate clears a threshold.

\paragraph{Evidence.} \emph{None} accepts the proposed change. An \emph{LLM judge} reads the change and the failures it targets and predicts its effect, at a cost in tokens but no episodes. A \emph{targeted replay} resumes a failed trajectory from the failing step with the change and succeeds with probability $b+(1-b)q$, where $q$ is the share of such failures the change repairs; a \emph{null replay} does the same without the change and measures the \emph{spurious recovery} rate $b$, without which replay success cannot be interpreted. A \emph{regression run} is a previously solved task, from the first step, with the change; a \emph{sample} or \emph{full evaluation} runs candidate and incumbent on a random sample of, or all, training tasks. Replays and judges see only the failures a change targets; only regression runs and evaluations see harm to tasks that already succeed.

\paragraph{Cost and value.} Against a reference rule, a rule $E$ whose choices change deployed success by $\Delta p$ and running cost by $\Delta c_{\mathrm{run}}$ per episode, which spends $C_{\mathrm{verify}}$ on evidence and changes the one-off cost of reviewing the applied changes by $\Delta C_{\mathrm{change}}$, is worth, over the next $N$ deployed episodes,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Net}(N)=N\,(v\,\Delta p-\Delta c_{\mathrm{run}})-C_{\mathrm{verify}}-\Delta C_{\mathrm{change}},
\label{eq:net}
\end{equation}
with costs in episodes of the unmodified agent and $v$ the value of one success (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:framework}). We count \emph{episodes}, runs of the agent on a task (a replay from a failing step counts as one); \emph{episode equivalents}, which price replays and checks by their tokens and are the unit of $C_{\mathrm{verify}}$; and \emph{dollars} at list prices, for a whole loop's cost, including the analyst's and proposer's model calls. For large $N$ evidence pays only if the changes it selects are better or cheaper to run, so we measure the \emph{decision value} of a rule that uses it, the deployment gain of its choice averaged over rounds, zero when it keeps the incumbent; how well its estimates track single changes' gains is bounded by the test sets' resolution (\S\ref{sec:e1res}).

\section{Study 2: Verification Evidence Inside RRSI}
\label{sec:study2}

Study 2 builds on RRSI \citep{rrsi2026}, whose proposer edits harness code over many rounds of natural failures. We run RRSI's loop, collect every kind of evidence for every candidate it measures, deploy every candidate, accepted or rejected, and ask what each kind of evidence would have decided. Table~\ref{tab:overview} lists the experiments, and Appendix~\ref{app:rrsi} describes the port, the registrations and every change made after them.

\paragraph{Loop.} We vendor RRSI's code (Apache 2.0) and change only its model backend, its cost accounting and one rule for empty proposals. Analyst, proposer, critic and judge are DeepSeek-V4-Pro with thinking disabled; the agent and user simulator are DeepSeek-V4-Flash at temperature 0; the starting harness is $\tau^2$'s reference agent. Each round the proposer drafts $m=2$ candidates under an edit budget that anneals from 4 to 1, a critic rejects edits that hard-code task data or touch the grader, and RRSI evaluates the candidates that pass a smoke test twice on every evolve task. With $S'$ a candidate's evolve success, $S^*$ the best so far, $\Delta S$ and $\Delta C$ its changes in success and relative running cost against the incumbent and $\nu$ its novelty, RRSI admits it if
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&S'\ge S^*-\delta\ \text{ and}\\
&\begin{cases}\Delta C\le\beta_0+\beta_1\Delta S & \text{if }\Delta S>\delta,\\ w_s\Delta S-w_c\Delta C+w_n\nu>0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:admit}
\end{equation}
where the noise band $\delta$ is calibrated from three evaluations of the base harness and, with RRSI's weights ($w_s=0$), only a cheaper or untried change passes inside the band; it rejects a harness that raises errors in more than 2\% of trials, and the admissible candidate with the highest success wins (Appendix~\ref{app:rrsi}). Evolve tasks are $\tau^2$'s training split (74 retail, 30 airline) and held-out tasks its test split (40 and 20). The loop ran $T=20$ rounds per domain on three independent trajectories, r1--r3.

\paragraph{Evidence and decisions.} For each measured candidate we collect the evidence of \S\ref{sec:problem} as estimates of its change $\Delta S$ in evolve success (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}, Appendix~\ref{app:rrsi}): an LLM judge's prediction from the diff and the failures it targets (no episodes); \emph{sample@$b$}, the paired difference on $b$ evolve episodes; \emph{replay@$b$}, the failure share times the success rate of $b$ targeted replays of failed evolve trials; \emph{replay$-$null@$b$}, which subtracts the success rate of null replays with the incumbent; and \emph{net@$b$}, which splits $b$ between both kinds of replay and regression runs on solved tasks, with or without a Beta prior on the repair rate. Budgets of 10, 40 and 80 episodes per candidate are subsamples of one collection (Appendix~\ref{app:rrsi}); a replay re-executes the recorded prefix through the new harness and goes live where it would first act differently. Every rule decides with RRSI's rule, with $\Delta S$ replaced by its estimate and $\delta$ by twice the estimate's standard error; full evaluation keeps RRSI's calibrated $\delta$. Appendix~\ref*{S-app:review} decides every rule with one criterion.

\paragraph{Sequential full evaluation.} RRSI evaluates a candidate on $N$ paired episodes, two per evolve task. The sequential rule runs them in batches of 10 in random order. After $n$ episodes with mean paired difference $m$ and running cost change $\hat c$, it predicts the final $\Delta S\sim\mathcal N(m,v_n)$ with $v_n=\big(\frac{N-n}{N}\big)^2 s^2\big(\frac1n+\frac1{N-n}\big)$, where $s^2$ is the larger of the running variance of the paired differences and $S_t(1-S_t)$, and computes the probability $p_n$ that Eq.~\ref{eq:admit} will hold with $\Delta C=\hat c$ and the known novelty. It stops the candidate if $p_n<\gamma=0.05$ or its harness errors already exceed 2\% of $N$. The \emph{cost-aware rule} (post hoc, Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r9}) instead draws the final $\Delta C\sim\mathcal N(\hat c,v^c_n)$, with $v^c_n$ of the same form from the per-pair token differences, independently of $\Delta S$, and integrates Eq.~\ref{eq:admit} over both. A candidate that is not stopped completes its evaluation and is decided as under full evaluation. Stopping thus changes only admission: it can lose an acceptance but never add one. A stopped candidate enters RRSI's history with its early-stopped estimate. \emph{Sequential sampling} caps $N$ at 80 sampled episodes and decides with the sample's own band.

\paragraph{Ground truth and estimand.} Every incumbent and measured candidate is deployed from the first step on the held-out tasks, 240 episodes each (retail 40 tasks $\times$ 6, airline 20 $\times$ 12), and the base and final harnesses on 400 and 240. A candidate's deployment gain $\Delta p(c\mid H_t)$ is its held-out success minus that of the incumbent $H_t$ it was proposed against, paired by task. A rule $E$'s \emph{decision value} on a set $\mathcal{T}$ of rounds of both domains is $D(E)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_c P_E(c\mid t)\,\Delta p(c\mid H_t)$, the mean expected deployment gain of its choice per round (zero when it keeps $H_t$), with $P_E$ taken over 400 subsamples of the evidence. For r1 the registered endpoints are (P1) \emph{transfer}, the held-out gain of the final harness over the base, and (P2) $D$ at $b=40$ with three Holm-adjusted contrasts (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r1}).

\paragraph{Further trajectories.} Because r1's values rested on one trajectory per domain and its registered analysis gave RRSI's error check to full evaluation only, we registered r2 and r3 before any of their episodes ran, with this study's primary analysis: $D$ over their 80 rounds with the error check applied to every rule's own episodes (the \emph{common error check}), and one-sided non-inferiority tests of sample@40, net@40 and replay$-$null@40 against full evaluation at a margin of 0.75 points per round, Holm-adjusted at $\alpha=0.05$; the two sequential rules form a second family. A bootstrap resamples blocks of rounds that share an incumbent and, within each trajectory and domain, one draw of held-out tasks shared by all its candidates, incumbents and rules, so its intervals generalise to new held-out tasks and to rounds like the observed ones, not to new improvement runs, which only the loop comparisons below resample; brackets give 90\% intervals, the registered level, unless marked 95\%. E3 runs the same loop and analysis on AppWorld \citep{trivedi2024appworld} (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:e3}).

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:e1res}

\paragraph{The loop.} Over r1--r3 RRSI drafted 240 candidates and measured 154, 106 of which changed harness code. It accepted 5 candidates on r1, all in the first five rounds, and 21 on r2 and r3, as late as round 19; 19 of the 26 were within the noise band. Candidates' mean deployment gain was $-1.5$, $-1.25$ and $+0.14$ points on r1--r3. 63 of 154 had positive point estimates, but held-out tasks resolve a single candidate's gain only beyond about four points: unadjusted 95\% intervals lay above zero for 8 and below for 10 (on AppWorld for none and 2 of 29; post hoc, Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r1}), against 4--5 per side expected by chance.

\paragraph{Transfer (P1).} Airline's evolve gains transferred on r1 and r2, by $+8.75$ [$+2.5,+15.8$] and $+7.9$ [$+2.5,+14.2$] points against $+13.3$ on evolve tasks. On r3 its six acceptances, all within the band, left evolve success unchanged ($+1.7$ [$-2.5,+5.8$] held out). Retail's evolve gains of 4.1, 4.7 and 7.4 points never transferred: $-0.25$ [$-3.0,+2.75$], $0.0$ [$-2.0,+1.75$] and $-4.25$ [$-7.5,-1.0$].

\paragraph{First trajectory.} On r1, one candidate (retail, round 6) added a check that reads an attribute the harness never sets: the harness raised errors in half of its evolve episodes and held-out success fell by 51.7 points, yet the critic passed it and the judge predicted a gain. It causes most of the losses of no verification, the judge and replay without null replays. Full evaluation's decision value on r1 was $+0.22$ points per round [$-0.06,+0.62$] (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r1}).

\paragraph{Primary analysis (r2 and r3).} On their 80 rounds full evaluation's decision value was only $+0.04$ points per round [$-0.35,+0.43$] at 141 episodes (Table~\ref{tab:primary}; every rule in Table~\ref{tab:e1r}), and no rule was shown to outperform keeping the incumbent. Replay$-$null@40 lost value, $-0.39$ [$-0.79,-0.03$], 40\% of it from one candidate (r3 retail, round 11) that held every write whose arguments it could not trace to earlier tool results: it raised no harness errors but blocked legitimate writes, lowering held-out success by 12.5 points; full evaluation, sample and net, which run solved tasks, rejected it, while replay$-$null and the judge accepted it. The registered non-inferiority tests against full evaluation, at a margin of 0.75 points per round, say little here, as keeping the incumbent passes too ($p=0.004$; Table~\ref*{S-tab:margins}). Sample@40 and net@40 passed narrowly (Holm $p=0.045$ each) and replay$-$null@40 did not ($p=0.14$); sharing one task draw between r2 and r3, which use the same test tasks, gives the @40 rules 0.056 (post hoc), so their pass is borderline, sensitive to the dependence structure and to Monte Carlo error (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:review}). A rule can pass them while dropping many of the reference's acceptances.

\paragraph{Sequential evaluation on the recorded rounds.} Sequential full evaluation, at 81 episodes per round (57\%), made full evaluation's choice in 93\% of rounds but only 74\% [67, 81] of its 21 acceptances (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). It passed the registered test, with a difference from full evaluation of $-0.02$ [$-0.12,+0.07$] points per round ($p<0.001$; sequential sampling $+0.03$ [$-0.26,+0.35$] at 53 episodes), and post hoc a margin of 0.25 points, which keeping the incumbent fails, with any one trajectory and domain left out (Table~\ref*{S-tab:loo}); but the acceptances it would drop gained no less held out ($+0.30$ against $+0.11$ points), almost all in success rather than running cost; pooled, it lost little because full evaluation's acceptances were worth little (Table~\ref{tab:accept}, Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r15}). Their value was concentrated: full evaluation gained $+0.58$ points per round on airline rounds 0--9 and $-0.19$ to $-0.10$ elsewhere; there the rule's difference was $-0.01$ [$-0.17,+0.19$] (the 2.4 acceptances it dropped were worth $+0.01$ points), and $-0.10$ [$-0.36,+0.09$] with r1, point estimates of 0.1 and 1.0 points over ten rounds (Table~\ref*{S-tab:cells}). Simpler halvings with RRSI's own rule are less faithful: one trial per task keeps 63\% of the acceptances, both trials on half the tasks 53\% (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r9}).

\paragraph{Is the stopping rule calibrated?} No (post hoc; Table~\ref{tab:calib}, Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r9}). Candidates stopped at a mean predicted probability of 0.02 would have been admitted by full evaluation in 8\% of cases on r2+r3 and 13\% in the six live loops below, 5.6 [2.5, 11.1] and 11.3 [3.0, 25.8] points more than predicted (95\%, resampling incumbent blocks); AppWorld shows no detectable miscalibration (3\%). Holding the cost change at its running estimate causes this: with the complete evaluation's cost change in the same formula, looks below $\gamma$ were followed by admission in at most 2\% of cases. The rule stopped about a third of the candidates RRSI admits inside its band for being cheaper or new, in recorded and live rounds alike, against 4\% of those admitted above the band. The cost-aware rule, designed post hoc on these rounds, halves the stops inside the band and keeps 88\% of full evaluation's acceptances on r2+r3 at 64\% of the episodes (Table~\ref*{S-tab:costaware}). Offline, the registered rule's recall on airline falls from 95\% at a band of 2.5 points to 51\% at 17.5, while the cost-aware rule keeps 87--96\% (Table~\ref*{S-tab:bands}).

\paragraph{Independent loops.} The analyses above score decisions in full evaluation's states. To compare loops, we registered two airline loops driven by sequential full evaluation (CL: cl1, cl2), then eight concurrent loops, four per selection step (IL; Appendix~\ref*{S-app:cl}). With r2 and r3, six loops per arm ran rounds 0--9. Transfer after ten rounds averaged $+4.2$ points with sequential and $+8.7$ with full evaluation, a difference of $-4.4$ [$-9.4,-0.1$] (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}d); non-inferiority at the registered margin of 5 points was not established ($p=0.38$), and the 95\% interval, [$-9.6,+0.7$], includes zero (Appendix~\ref{app:claims}). The recorded decisions of airline rounds 0--9 of r1--r3 predict $-1.0$ [$-3.7,+0.8$] (Table~\ref*{S-tab:loops}). But each loop calibrated its own noise band, and by chance the sequential loops' bands were wider (9.8 against 6.7 points); a wider band leaves more candidates to RRSI's in-band branch, where the rule's stops are least reliable, and adjusting for it post hoc halves the pooled difference, to $-2.1$ [$-6.2,+1.9$], though the concurrent loops keep $-5.8$ [$-9.4,-2.2$].

\paragraph{Blocked loops (BK).} To separate the rule from its calibration and locate the loss, we registered a third comparison after IL's results (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:bk}). Four calibration blocks each gave one noise band to five airline loops that differ only in selection: full evaluation (f), sequential full evaluation (s), the cost-aware rule (c) and two ablations. Loop h admits as f does but enters stopped candidates into the proposer's history with their early-stopped estimates, as s does; loop a admits as s does but shows the proposer every complete evaluation. Loops of a block share computation only while their states are identical, so each has the distribution of an independent loop of its rule; the four blocks, not the twenty loops, are the independent units. Sequential loops gained 0.8 points less than full evaluation's [$-2.9,+1.2$] (Table~\ref{tab:bk}), close to the recorded decisions' prediction: IL's loss did not replicate under shared calibration. A loss of about three points is not excluded, but one of five, IL's margin, is, by the 95\% interval [$-3.4,+1.7$] and the Bonferroni interval [$-4.1,+2.4$] (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:bk}); BK registered no non-inferiority test, so this reading is post hoc (pooled with IL and adjusted for the band, $-1.7$ [$-4.1,+0.8$]). The admission main effect, $-1.6$ [$-3.0,-0.2$], met its registered reading, which uses unadjusted 90\% intervals; at 95\% its interval includes zero and Holm's adjustment over the five registered contrasts gives $p=0.19$, so it is consistent with, but does not establish, a cost of the dropped admissions; without any one block it lies between $-1.0$ and $-2.2$ (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:bk}). Four blocks had 80\% power only for admission effects of 2.0 points, and a one-point loop difference needs 28 (post hoc). In 7 of the 16 rounds in which full evaluation would have accepted in s's states, the rule had stopped its choice. The history effect, $+0.7$ [$-2.2,+3.7$] on 1.4 df, is unresolved. The cost-aware rule made full evaluation's choice in all 40 of its rounds at 80\% of the candidate-evaluation episodes; its gains over the registered rule, $+1.8$ [$-0.2,+3.8$], and over full evaluation, $+0.9$ [$-1.1,+3.0$], are not resolved.

\paragraph{Cost.} What sequential evaluation saves is candidate-evaluation episodes, not the loop's cost: its IL loops ran 39\% fewer such episodes at 10\% lower cost ($-\$0.35$ [$-0.57,-0.10$]; at 95\% the interval includes zero), and its BK loops 37\% fewer at 12\% lower cost (descriptive; Table~\ref{tab:bk}), because the proposal side's model calls dominate; the cost-aware rule saved 20\% of the episodes and 2.5\% of the cost. Spent on 1.4 more rounds, the registered rule's saving would move its offline ten-round loss on airline only from $-1.0$ to $-0.9$ [$-3.2,+0.9$] (post hoc; Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r9}), a comparison that keeps full evaluation's states; no loops ran at matched total cost. Against keeping the incumbent, no rule's verification was shown to pay on r2 and r3: $\mathrm{Net}(10^4)$ at $v=10$ was $-108$ episodes [$-500,+274$] for full evaluation and $+10$ [$-315,+350$] at best, for sequential sampling.

\paragraph{Other trajectories.} Pooled over r1--r3, full evaluation gained $+0.10$ [$-0.18,+0.41$] per round, and trajectories differed more than rules. On AppWorld (E3) the evolve gain of 4.2 points did not transfer ($0.0$ [$-2.1,+2.1$]); sequential full evaluation passed non-inferiority at 62\% of the episodes and the @40 rules did not (Table~\ref*{S-tab:e3}).

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence shows.} Our results identify failure modes of verification, not a ranking of evidence by predictive quality. Few changes measurably helped held out, so verification earned its place by rejecting harmful ones, and only evidence that runs the changed agent on solved tasks rejected both kinds we saw. Sorted by evidence (Appendix~\ref{app:claims}), only three conclusions rest on registered analyses with adjusted inference: no rule was shown to outperform keeping the incumbent; the sequential rules were non-inferior to full evaluation on recorded decisions, as keeping the incumbent was; and sequential loops ran fewer candidate-evaluation episodes. The harmful and the dropped acceptances are registered but descriptive, the stops' miscalibration is a post hoc diagnosis whose fix BK checked descriptively, and the loop losses, their mechanism and the dollar saving are not established. The most secure contributions are thus the audit method and the localisation of one stopping rule's decision distortion; how much such distortion costs a loop's final agent, and which verification is best at a fixed budget, remain open.

\paragraph{Recorded decisions and loops.} Scoring each decision once, in the states full evaluation chose, ignores that an accepted change becomes the base of later rounds and that every evaluated candidate enters the proposer's history; BK's estimate of an adverse effect of the first did not survive adjustment, and the second is unresolved. A calibration imbalance may have contributed to IL's loss, but the experiments do not isolate it; loops compared with each other should share or balance their calibration.

\paragraph{What recall adds.} Recall detected this rule's problem, which its decision value could not: it dropped a quarter of full evaluation's acceptances, in recorded rounds as in the loops, but they were worth little in the primary analysis. Recall measures fidelity to the reference, not deployment quality (the evolve cost savings for which RRSI admitted many changes did not carry to held-out tasks; Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r15}), but it adds what a decision value averaged over rounds in which the reference gains little averages away: whether a rule keeps the changes a loop then builds on. It should be reported with the held-out value of the dropped acceptances and the calibration of the stops on each branch of the acceptance rule; here they flagged the rule's mechanism before any loop ran, but whether higher recall predicts a better final agent has been checked on only two rules in one domain.

\paragraph{Implications for self-improvement loops.} Loops that report only the final agent's held-out success \citep{chen2026harnessfix,zhang2026ace,rrsi2026} measure all their decisions at once; on retail and AppWorld, evolve gains of 4--7 points never transferred. Replays need null replays and regression runs. A stopping rule must model the uncertainty of every condition in the acceptance rule it predicts; without RRSI's cost and novelty branch the registered rule makes 90--100\% of the far fewer acceptances (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r9}). Because proposal calls dominate a loop's cost, cheaper proposals, which we did not vary, are likely a larger lever than cheaper verification.

\section{Conclusion}
Auditing RRSI's decisions one candidate at a time, we found that few changes measurably helped held out, that evidence that never runs the changed agent on solved tasks would have accepted harmful ones, and that no rule's verification was shown to pay against keeping the incumbent. A sequential rule stayed close to full re-evaluation's decision value on recorded rounds but dropped a quarter of its acceptances; the cost of this to a loop's final agent, and the best verification at a fixed budget, remain open. Cheaper verification should report its recall of the reference's acceptances and its calibration.

\section*{Limitations}
\paragraph{Models and domains.} Both studies use one model family, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash as agent and DeepSeek-V4-Pro as analyst, proposer and judge, and two customer-service domains of $\tau^2$-bench; Study 1 adds ShopDesk and Study 2 one AppWorld trajectory. Study 1's agent used the provider's default sampling and Study 2's uses temperature 0. Study 2's proposer is likely weaker than the model RRSI was developed with; a fourth trajectory with a thinking proposer was cut short because its drafts took over an hour and mostly ended without a proposal; on airline all six of its measured candidates helped, which suggests that a stronger proposer leaves less for verification to decide, but rests on one trajectory (Appendix~\ref{app:rrsi}). Token prices for the models we used change; we report costs in episodes, and weighting tokens by list prices changes no ranking.

\paragraph{Study 1.} Study 1's patches rewrite only policy sections, tool descriptions and a step limit; it runs a single round, mostly on injected faults (natural failures are 16--25\% of training failures), and deploys patch sets only at budget 10, on 120 (retail) and 60 (airline) held-out episodes, where most of retail's gain comes from fault-injected episodes. Its bank analyses rest on a harm model fitted to at most 20 regression runs per patch, and its edge priors use an analyst accuracy of 0.7 unless stated.

\paragraph{Test tasks and resolution.} Study 2's held-out sets are $\tau^2$'s test splits of 40 and 20 tasks, on which most candidates' gains were not resolved: of the 183 measured candidates, unadjusted per-candidate 95\% intervals lay above zero for 8, about what chance alone would give, and below zero for 12. Every airline loop (r1--r4, CL, IL and BK) and most post hoc analyses use the same 20 held-out tasks, and several analyses (the noise-band adjustment, the cost-aware rule, the noise-band replays) were designed after held-out results of earlier experiments on those tasks were known; task bootstraps capture sampling error on these tasks, not the effect of analysing them repeatedly, and $\tau^2$'s airline domain has no further tasks to hold out. Registering an experiment before its episodes run does not make these tasks a fresh test set. Resolution limited these studies more than price: a round of full evaluation costs about 25 cents of $\tau^2$ compute, but comparing evidence needs per-round differences of about 0.7 points (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r1}), and more trajectories and held-out tasks would help more than more trials (Table~\ref*{S-tab:effective}). Three trajectories per domain show that between-trajectory variation is as large as the differences between rules, and the pooled analysis treats trajectories as fixed. The non-inferiority margin of 0.75 points per round was set before any result, but it is large relative to the decision values we observed.

\paragraph{Recorded decisions and loops.} Study 2's decision values are conditional on RRSI's trajectories: they score each rule's decisions in the states RRSI visited. The loop comparisons use one domain and ten rounds, at equal rounds rather than equal cost; the equal-cost comparison of Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r9} is offline and assumes extra rounds are worth what full evaluation's later rounds were. IL's arms mix loops run at different times (cl1, cl2, r2 and r3, whose outcomes were known when it was registered, and eight concurrent loops), with arm labels fixed rather than randomised and noise bands that came out wider in the sequential arm. Its registered 90\% intervals were not adjusted for its three primary outcomes; with 95\% intervals and Holm's adjustment, neither its transfer difference nor its cost difference is established (Appendix~\ref{app:claims}). BK has four blocks, its independent units, which give 80\% power only for admission effects of about two points; its bands (5.4 to 8.2 points) were narrower than those of IL's sequential loops, and a wider band may enlarge the rule's loss. Its registration read each of five contrasts by an unadjusted 90\% interval; the admission effect does not survive a 95\% interval or Holm's adjustment, its history effect is imprecise (1.4 Satterthwaite df), its loops share computation while their states are identical, and its cost-aware loops' gain over the registered rule was not resolved. Whether calibration explains the difference between IL and BK is not identified: the two experiments also differ in their trajectories and band ranges.

\paragraph{Scope of the diagnosis.} The stops' miscalibration appeared on $\tau^2$'s recorded rounds and in the live loops but not detectably on AppWorld, and only airline's loops improved held out, so what it costs a loop has been measured in one domain. Our diagnosis concerns RRSI's acceptance rule, under which 19 of the 26 acceptances of r1--r3 fell inside the noise band, where cost and novelty decide, and one stopping rule; it does not establish general properties of replay, judging or sequential evaluation, and a loop with a different acceptance criterion may behave differently. The cost-aware rule's fidelity in loops rests on 11 acceptances in bands of 5.4 to 8.2 points; offline it held for bands of 2.5 to 17.5 points, on recorded trajectories only. $\mathrm{Net}(N)$ sets the change cost to zero; charging each accepted change a fixed review cost favours rules that accept less and leaves the order of the sequential rules and full evaluation unchanged (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:review}). No rule's verification was shown to pay against keeping the incumbent on r2 and r3, so our cost comparisons rank rules that may all cost more than they return in this loop.

\paragraph{Registration and post hoc analyses.} The analysis of other noise bands is post hoc. BK was registered after IL's results, and the cost-aware rule was designed post hoc on the recorded rounds before it was registered for BK. Post hoc are, on recorded decisions, the smaller margins, the agreement rates and their split by full evaluation's decision, the decision-rule ablation, the coverage, calibration, threshold, matched-cost and leave-one-out analyses, the simpler halvings, the noise-band replays, the re-decision without the cost and novelty branch, the split by domain and stage, the utility of the dropped acceptances, the clustered calibration intervals, the per-candidate resolution, the task draw shared across trajectories and the offline evaluation of the cost-aware rule; and, on loops, the matched offline comparison, the noise-band adjustment, the accounting of IL's loops, BK's sensitivity, leave-one-block-out and power analyses and the equal-cost comparison (Appendix~\ref{app:claims} sorts the conclusions by the evidence behind them). On r1, the registered analysis gave RRSI's error check to full evaluation only, so the comparison with a common check is post hoc there; its held-out count was raised after six rounds, when some held-out episodes had run and a few results had been seen (Appendix~\ref{app:rrsi}), and the registered count gives the same conclusions. While r2 and r3 ran, a few of their held-out success rates appeared in progress logs before analysis; the analysis code had been fixed and tested on r1 only. The sequential rules were designed on r1's rounds and confirmed on r2 and r3. r1's component labels did not recognise our harness's files, which may have changed what its proposer was told to explore; r2 and r3 use corrected labels, so the trajectories differ in that respect as well as in sampling.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Loops of the kind we study modify software that is then deployed. In Study 2 few proposed changes measurably raised held-out success, and two harmful ones, one that crashed the harness in half of its episodes and one that blocked legitimate writes, would have been accepted by checks that did not run previously solved tasks. That Study 1 detected no gain from verification does not show that verification is unnecessary; our measurements cannot exclude small harms per change and should not be read as licence to deploy changes without review. On ShopDesk a single patch silently broke a quarter of tasks (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:shopdesk}). Our environments use synthetic customer data only.

\subsection*{Terms and Abbreviations}
\phantomsection\label{app:terms}

\begin{description}\itemsep0pt
\item[Harness, change, candidate.] The prompts, tool descriptions and code around the frozen agent model; a proposed edit to it; a change the loop measures.
\item[Incumbent.] The harness a round's candidates are proposed against.
\item[Deployment gain, transfer.] A candidate's held-out success minus its incumbent's; a loop's held-out success after round 9 minus that of the base harness.
\item[Evolve and held-out tasks.] $\tau^2$'s training split, on which the loop evaluates candidates, and its test split, on which we deploy them.
\item[Full evaluation.] RRSI's selection: every candidate is run twice on every evolve task.
\item[Noise band $\delta$.] RRSI's threshold, calibrated from three evaluations of the base harness. A candidate above it is admitted if its cost change is within budget; inside it (the \emph{in-band branch}), if a score of its success change, cost change and novelty is positive.
\item[Sequential full evaluation (registered rule).] Full evaluation run in batches of 10 that stops a candidate once the predicted probability that RRSI's rule admits it falls below $\gamma=0.05$, with the cost change held at its running estimate.
\item[Cost-aware rule.] The same, with the uncertainty of the final cost change modelled (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:r9}).
\item[Admission, history.] Which candidates a rule can accept; the scores of evaluated candidates that RRSI shows the proposer in later rounds.
\item[Decision value $D$.] The held-out gain of a rule's choice, averaged over rounds, zero when it keeps the incumbent.
\item[Agreement, recall.] The share of rounds in which a rule makes full evaluation's choice; the share of full evaluation's acceptances it makes.
\item[r1--r4, E3.] Recorded trajectories of RRSI driven by full evaluation on $\tau^2$ (r4 with a thinking proposer) and on AppWorld.
\item[CL, IL, BK.] Loops driven by sequential evaluation (two, descriptive); the registered comparison of six independent loops per rule; the registered blocked comparison of five rules in four blocks that share one calibration (Appendix~\ref*{S-app:bk}).
\item[Episode, episode equivalent.] One run of the agent on a task; the token cost of a replay or check relative to an episode.
\end{description}
