\title{Where Did the Agent Go Wrong? Evaluating Failure Attribution\\ Against Counterfactual Rescue}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Self-improving agent loops start by attributing each failure to a step, usually by asking an LLM to read the trace. We evaluate attribution against counterfactual rescue: for every step we measure how much one oracle correction raises the success of continued replays above uncorrected replays from that step, and score a method by this rescue gain at the step it names. On 300 natural failures from a self-improvement loop in $\tau^2$-bench retail and airline and in AppWorld, with registered analyses, the evaluation's design moved the comparison as much as the methods did. Under the registered prompts a zero-cost rule naming the first state-changing action scored highest (0.42, against 0.20--0.34), not significantly above the best LLM method; judges asked post hoc for the measured quantity, and counterfactual search given their information and the metric's selection rule, came within 0.02. The rule's lead rests on an oracle that reads the grading: two independent auditors judged only 3 and 6 of 17 audited $\tau^2$ corrections that change state to be steps a careful agent could take, without such corrections the rule ranked seventh of ten, and an oracle shown only the failed episode rarely rescued writes. Without null replays the ranking reverses. In a repair experiment, neither these attributions nor the step of largest rescue gain, even with a diagnosis and correction that kept most of their gain on fresh replays, brought a detectable gain on held-out tasks over the loop's own analysis; the experiment had no positive control and cannot exclude gains the size of one accepted round.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Agents that improve themselves from their own failures share a first step. An analyst model reads a failed episode, decides where it went wrong, and hands that diagnosis to a proposer that edits the agent's prompts, tools or code \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,zhang2026ace,chen2026harnessfix,rrsi2026}. If the diagnosis points at the wrong step, the proposer may repair the wrong thing. Failure attribution has therefore become a task of its own, with benchmarks of human-labelled multi-agent logs \citep{zhang2025whichagent,liu2026whowhenpro}, taxonomies of failure modes \citep{cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast,zhu2025agentdebug}, and methods that replay a failure with a step changed \citep{zhang2026agentracer,ma2025dover,lin2026reflect,bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}.

Three questions are rarely asked together: is an attribution correct in the sense a repair loop needs, is it worth its cost, and does a more accurate attribution make the loop's repairs better? A human label says which step an annotator blames; a repair loop needs a step whose correction would have made the episode succeed. We evaluate replay-based methods and LLM judges against a reference defined by rescue, report cost next to the score, and hand each attribution to a working loop to measure the repairs it produces.

Defining that reference was the hard part: the \emph{earliest} step whose single correction beats uncorrected replays was unstable, because a failure usually has several rescuing steps. We therefore measure a \emph{rescue profile}: for every step, the mean gain in success over null replays across four independent corrections. A method is scored by the rescue gain at the step it names, and a failure is \emph{rescuable} when some step reaches a gain of 0.5. The null replays, which continue the unchanged episode from the same step, matter most: without them an LLM judge would rank first and the rule that wins with them sixth.

We apply the profile to 300 natural failures of agents built by a self-improvement loop, RRSI \citep{rrsi2026}, in $\tau^2$-bench's retail and airline domains \citep{barres2025tau2} and in AppWorld with a code agent \citep{trivedi2024appworld}, and compare ten attribution methods, from LLM judges of different cost and design \citep{zhang2025whichagent} to counterfactual search with up to 40 replays and two structural rules. Methods, metrics and analyses were registered before any rescue profile was computed. A second registered experiment re-runs one round of RRSI at eight of its own states in $\tau^2$ with each attribution, or none, given to the proposer, and deploys every resulting repair on 240 held-out episodes (\S\ref{sec:repairs}); post hoc, we repeat it with a proposer that cannot read the failing traces. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A reference by rescue profile} with null controls, and what it depends on. Post hoc, the method ranking held across oracle samples, thresholds, a second oracle, the observed action as control and a screen of the corrections, but two choices change it: without null replays it reverses, because uncorrected replays from early steps often succeed by chance, and without the grading the oracle rarely rescues writes, whose corrections two auditors mostly judged invalid (\S\ref{sec:robust}).
\item \textbf{A cost-aware comparison of ten attribution methods} on 300 natural failures, which shows that the evaluation's design decides the comparison. Under the registered reference and prompts a free structural rule was competitive: the first write scored highest, not significantly above binary search ($+0.08$ $[-0.02,+0.18]$). Post hoc, judges asked for the quantity the metric measures came within 0.02 of it, and so did counterfactual search given the judges' information and a selection rule matched to the metric, at ten times a judge's cost. The rule's lead came from writes the oracle repairs with the grading (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{A test on repairs.} Repairs drafted from first-write or binary-search steps, from the step of largest rescue gain, or from its diagnosis and correction, showed no detectable incremental benefit on held-out tasks over those drafted from RRSI's own analysis. The intervals exclude differences beyond about $\pm2.5$ points, not the gain of one accepted round; the drafts took up the evidence but did not fix even the cases it named, and without a positive control the experiment cannot show whether attribution limits this loop (\S\ref{sec:repairs}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Failure attribution.} Who\&When introduced automated attribution of the responsible agent and step in multi-agent logs, with all-at-once, step-by-step and binary-search judges, and found step-level accuracy far below agent-level accuracy \citep{zhang2025whichagent}; Who\&When Pro revisits these labels \citep{liu2026whowhenpro}. Taxonomies organise failures into modes \citep{cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast,zhu2025agentdebug,zhu2026agentdebugx}; ErrorProbe learns diagnosis from its history \citep{li2026errorprobe}. MP-Bench argues that a failure often admits several plausible attributions and finds that earlier conclusions about LLMs' weakness at attribution owe much to benchmark design \citep{in2026rethinking}. These evaluations score agreement with human or injected labels; we score the effect of correcting the named step, and likewise find that how judges are asked changes their score.

\paragraph{Counterfactual attribution.} AgenTracer labels failures by replaying corrected steps of injected faults and trains an attributor on them \citep{zhang2026agentracer}; DoVer and REFLECT intervene on a trace to validate hypotheses \citep{ma2025dover,lin2026reflect}; CausalFlow and Causal Agent Replay attribute failures by counterfactual replay \citep{bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}. Our reference uses the same intervention with null replays from the same step and a full profile over steps instead of the first step that flips. The profile is a graded, sampled version of but-for causation \citep{halpern2016actual,pearl2009causality}.

\paragraph{Self-improving agents.} Reflexion, ExpEL and ACE turn failures into lessons or playbooks \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,zhang2026ace}; HarnessFix diagnoses and repairs harness flaws \citep{chen2026harnessfix}; RRSI digests failed traces, proposes harness edits and keeps them by re-evaluation \citep{rrsi2026}. Prediction of failure does not imply that intervening helps \citep{vasudev2026intervention}. We evaluate the diagnostic step these loops share, on failures one of them produced.

\section{A Reference: Rescue Profiles}
\label{sec:gt}

\paragraph{Replays.} A failed episode is a sequence of agent steps $0,\dots,n-1$. A \emph{replay from step $k$} keeps steps $0,\dots,k-1$ and the environment state they produced, and lets the same agent and user simulator continue; a \emph{corrected} replay forces a given action at step $k$ first. The agent runs at temperature 0, but replays still vary, so each outcome is a sample.

\paragraph{Oracle corrections.} For each step an oracle (DeepSeek-V4-Pro, thinking off) sees the domain policy, the tools, the episode up to the step and the task's grading criteria: $\tau^2$'s gold actions and required information, or AppWorld's unit tests with their expected values. It either judges the step correct or writes the action the agent should have taken, using only what the agent knew at that point: the criteria tell it the right outcome, not identifiers or facts the agent had not yet seen. The reference is thus repairability by an oracle that knows the intended outcome, privileged information an offline evaluation may use; it does not certify that each correction was justified by what the agent had seen.

\paragraph{Protocol 3.} It replaced an earliest-step protocol whose independent runs agreed on 42\% of pilot failures (Appendix~\ref{app:gt}). At every step $k$ we draw $K=4$ oracle samples. Each sample that corrects the step gets 2 corrected replays, and the step gets 4 null replays. The rescue gain of step $k$ is
\begin{equation}
R_k=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}c_{k,i}\bigl(\hat{p}^{\,\mathrm{corr}}_{k,i}-\hat{p}^{\,\mathrm{null}}_{k}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $c_{k,i}=1$ if sample $i$ gives an action the replay can apply, and $c_{k,i}=0$ if it judges the step correct or its action cannot be applied. $R_k$ is thus the share of samples that propose a correction times the mean gain of those corrections. A null replay lets the agent act again at step $k$. It reproduced the observed tool calls in 91--95\% of $\tau^2$ null replays and the observed code in 58\% of AppWorld ones (post hoc), so $R_k$ compares forcing the oracle's action with letting the agent act again, which in $\tau^2$ is close to keeping the observed action. A failure is rescuable if $\max_k R_k\ge 0.5$, and then its best rescue step is the step with the largest gain (earliest on ties). We also record the earliest step with $R_k\ge 0.5$. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows one profile.

\paragraph{Scoring a method.} A method names one step $\hat{k}$. Its score on a failure is $\Rhat$, the rescue gain at that step, and 0 for an invalid step. The primary metric is the mean of $\Rhat$ over rescuable failures; it rewards naming any step whose correction helps, in proportion to how much it helps. Secondary metrics include exact agreement with the best rescue step (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:secondary}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Failures.} All failures come from RRSI \citep{rrsi2026} run as a self-improvement loop on a DeepSeek-V4-Flash agent: from the harnesses it accepted over 20 rounds, in their evaluations on training tasks and in their deployment on held-out tasks. In $\tau^2$-bench \citep{barres2025tau2} the agent serves a simulated customer through tool calls; in AppWorld \citep{trivedi2024appworld} it writes Python against the APIs of nine apps and submits with a final call. We drew 100 failed episodes per domain with a registered rule: no harness error, at most two per task and harness, tasks taken round-robin in a seeded order. They cover 36 (retail), 24 (airline) and 47 (AppWorld) tasks and average 11.7, 10.3 and 19.6 agent steps.

\paragraph{Methods.} Every LLM method except counterfactual search reads the failure as RRSI's analyst reads it: the task (in $\tau^2$ with the simulated customer's hidden instructions), the episode with numbered steps, and the grading report the agent never sees, which lists $\tau^2$'s gold actions and missing information or AppWorld's failed unit tests with their assertions. The judges therefore have the information the oracle has. Each method names one step.
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Rules}: the \emph{first write}, the first step that calls a state-changing tool or API (else the last step), and the \emph{last step}.
\item \textbf{All-at-once} judge \citep{zhang2025whichagent}: one call over the whole episode, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash, V4-Pro, and V4-Pro with thinking.
\item \textbf{Step-by-step} judge: one call per step, in order, until a step is judged decisive.
\item \textbf{Binary search} judge: halves the episode $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ times, asking which half holds the decisive mistake.
\item \textbf{Root-cause attributor}: one call asking for a root cause, the earliest wrong step and a component.
\item \textbf{RRSI trace digester} \citep{rrsi2026}: RRSI's own multi-turn failure digest; we take the earliest step it cites as evidence.
\item \textbf{Counterfactual search} (CF search), replay-based like AgenTracer's labelling \citep{zhang2026agentracer} but without grading criteria: one call names up to five suspect steps with corrected actions; suspects are tested in order with 4 corrected and 4 null replays, and the search stops at the first correction that beats null by 2 successes or when its budget of 40 replays runs out; otherwise it answers with the first suspect.
\end{itemize}
Judges are prompted for the decisive mistake, defined as the earliest step whose correction would most likely have rescued the episode (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}); post hoc reruns change this definition or withhold the grading information. Pro runs with thinking off unless stated.

\paragraph{Registration and analysis.} Methods, prompts, metrics and analyses were registered, and all methods had been run, before the main profiles were computed; no method sees a profile. The registered primary comparison is counterfactual search at budget 40 against the all-at-once judge with Pro: the paired difference in $\Rhat$ over rescuable failures, with a 95\% bootstrap interval over task clusters (10{,}000 draws) and a paired permutation test flipping signs by task, on retail and airline pooled and, for AppWorld, separately. Everything else is estimated with cluster-bootstrap intervals and no further tests; unregistered analyses are marked post hoc or exploratory. A seeded 10\% of failures in each domain got a second, independent profile. Costs are list-price dollars for tokens and replays. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} lists every change made after registration.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Profiles.} Of 300 failures, 157 were rescuable: 65 of 100 in retail, 37 in airline and 55 in AppWorld. Null replays from the same step succeeded 27\%, 18\% and 11\% of the time; without them, many rescues would be chance recoveries. A third of the rescuable failures (18, 11 and 26) had two or more steps with a gain of at least 0.5. In the retest, 27 of 30 failures got the same verdict on rescuability and 15 of the 17 that both found rescuable the same best rescue step (Appendix~\ref{app:gt}).

\paragraph{Under the registered prompts, the first-write rule scored highest.} Table~\ref{tab:main} gives every method's rescue gain. Pooled over the three domains, the first write scored 0.42 [0.33, 0.51]; the LLM methods scored between 0.20 (step-by-step) and 0.34 (binary search). The first write's lead over binary search was not significant ($+0.08$, exploratory, $[-0.02,+0.18]$); over the all-at-once judges it was $+0.11$ to $+0.12$. Exact agreement with the best rescue step ordered the methods the same way, and over all 300 failures, a cohort chosen without the reference, the first write scored 0.23 and the LLM methods 0.11 to 0.19 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:secondary}). At the steps the methods named, the oracle proposed a correction about equally often (0.52--0.64 of samples); the first write led because corrections at its steps worked better (mean gain 0.61, against 0.34--0.51; post hoc, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). Cost bought little: the all-at-once judges with Flash (\$0.001 per failure), Pro (\$0.003) and Pro with thinking (\$0.014) scored 0.30, 0.30 and 0.31 (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). On Who\&When our judges reproduce the published pattern (Appendix~\ref{sec:whowhen}).

\paragraph{Aligned with the judges and the metric, search reached the rule.} Counterfactual search at budget 40 scored 0.26 pooled, at seven times the cost of the all-at-once judge with Pro (\$0.022 against \$0.003 per failure). The registered comparison with that judge detected no difference, with intervals that admit 0.1 either way: $-0.061$ $[-0.146,+0.032]$ in $\tau^2$ (permutation $p=.20$, $n=102$) and $+0.025$ $[-0.087,+0.136]$ in AppWorld ($p=.69$, $n=55$). Unlike the judges, search did not read the grading, asked where the agent went wrong, and stopped at the first suspect passing a noisy threshold. Post hoc we removed each difference at the same budget. Grading information and replays raised the score by similar amounts (main effects $+0.07$ $[+0.01,+0.13]$ and $+0.06$ $[+0.03,+0.10]$; Table~\ref{tab:main}, lower block). Testing every suspect and answering the largest estimated gain added 0.02 to registered and 0.03 to informed search (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:maxgain}). With all three aligned, the suspect call also asking for the steps of largest gain over letting the agent act again, search scored 0.41, $+0.15$ $[+0.07,+0.22]$ above its registered form and level with the first write ($-0.01$ $[-0.10,+0.09]$) and with binary search asked for the gain (0.40), at about \$0.03 per failure. Under the registered design, search trailed the rule because of how it was configured more than because of what replay can do. These contrasts are post hoc and descriptive.

\paragraph{Asked for the measured quantity, judges came close to the rule.} Judges that missed the best rescue step usually named an earlier one. The registered prompts asked for the earliest decisive mistake, while $R_k$ measures the gain of a correction over letting the agent act again. Post hoc, we re-ran three judges asking instead for the step whose correction would most likely rescue the task, or would most increase success over letting the agent act again (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The second, which matches the metric, raised the Pro judges by 0.07 (all-at-once 0.30 to 0.36, $[+0.02,+0.12]$; binary search 0.34 to 0.40, $[-0.01,+0.13]$; Table~\ref{tab:main}) and the Flash judge by 0.01. Binary search asked this way showed no detected difference from the first write ($-0.02$ $[-0.10,+0.07]$, an interval too wide to show equivalence), so the first write's lead under the registered prompts owes much to how the judges were asked.

\paragraph{The rule's lead depends on the kind of step.} The first write led in retail (0.53, against 0.14--0.33 for LLM methods) and AppWorld (0.39); in airline the all-at-once judges led (0.41--0.42, against 0.27). Most best rescue steps were writes in retail (36 of 65), usually a wrong write replaced by the right one, and utterances in airline (25 of 37), typically a refusal or a clarifying question the agent should have made before acting. Split by the kind of best rescue step (post hoc, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:strata}), the first write scored 0.60 on the 82 failures where that step changed state (a write, or AppWorld's submission), against 0.46 for binary search asked for the largest gain ($+0.13$ $[+0.03,+0.23]$), and 0.23 on the other 75, against 0.34 to 0.39 for the best judges and informed search; 74\% of its score came from the first group. Since the oracle repairs writes more readily than utterances (\S\ref{sec:discussion}), the rule's lead is conditional on this correction mechanism; with the eight domain-by-kind cells weighted equally, binary search asked for the largest gain scored 0.43 and the first write 0.41.

\paragraph{The ranking held under most choices, but not without null replays.}
\label{sec:robust}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robust} re-scores every method under other choices (post hoc; Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). With fewer samples, thresholds from 0.25 to 0.75, only failures with a single rescuing step, failures chosen on two samples and scored on the other two, a second oracle model (V4-Flash), or without the 2.5\% of corrections a mechanical screen flagged for an unseen identifier or extra calls (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:admiss}), the first write stayed first (Kendall's $\tau$ of 0.78 to 1.00). With the observed action forced as the control, binary search asked for the largest gain tied it (0.45 against 0.44). The screen cannot see a choice guided by the grading, and individual profiles are noisier than the ranking: halves of the data with two samples each agreed on the best rescue step for 56\% of failures (Appendix~\ref{app:further}). Dropping the null replays reversed the ranking: 203 failures became rescuable, the first write fell to sixth place, and the all-at-once judge with thinking ranked first (0.42 against 0.37). Uncorrected replays from an episode's first fifth succeeded 41--44\% of the time and from its last fifth 2--6\% (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:nullpos}); LLM methods other than binary search named steps on average 53--67\% of the way through an episode, against 77\% for best rescue steps, so without the null control they collect credit for recoveries that happen anyway.

\paragraph{Without the grading, the oracle rarely rescued writes.} Post hoc, we reran the oracle with the same instructions and context but no grading, telling it only that the episode failed, at the 960 steps any method named on the 157 rescuable failures (\$4.0; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blind}). Gains at best rescue steps fell from 0.76 to 0.17. Corrections of writes lost nearly everything (retail 0.77 to 0.00, AppWorld 0.82 to 0.20), corrections of utterances half to two thirds (0.72--0.76 to 0.24--0.36): the oracle repairs writes mainly by knowing which write the task wanted. Under this oracle every method scored 0.03 to 0.11, the first write ranked seventh of ten (0.05, against 0.11 for counterfactual search), and the ordering kept little of the registered one ($\tau=.24$). Full profiles under this oracle on a random 100 of the 300 failures (\$3.6; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blindfull}) found 17 rescuable, against 51 under the registered oracle; none of the 12 in $\tau^2$ was best rescued at a write, and on the 17 the first write ranked eighth of ten (0.17, against 0.36 for counterfactual search). Removing the grading also weakens the oracle, so this is a sensitivity analysis, not an admissibility test.

\paragraph{Auditors judged most state-changing corrections invalid.} Two auditors independently labelled a stratified sample of 60 corrections with a positive gain (post hoc; Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). They judged 29 and 34 of the 60 valid given what the agent could see and the policy (Cohen's $\kappa=.63$), but only 3 and 6 of the 17 $\tau^2$ corrections that change state: most replaced the item, payment or baggage count the user had last confirmed, or broke the policy, choices with no unseen value for the mechanical screen to find. Without every $\tau^2$ correction that changes state, 105 failures stayed rescuable and the first write ranked seventh (0.27, against 0.34 for the all-at-once judge with thinking; $\tau=.38$). The registered profile measures where a correction that knows the intended outcome helps; for a critic without it, the first write's lead does not hold.

\section{Do Better Attributions Give Better Repairs?}
\label{sec:repairs}

\paragraph{Design.} A rescue score is a proxy: a loop needs attributions only to repair its agent. We tested this on RRSI itself, in a separately registered experiment. We took two other runs of the loop in each $\tau^2$ domain and the harness each run had accepted by the start of rounds 5 and 15, giving eight incumbent states. At each state we re-ran one unmodified RRSI round five times, changing only the evidence the proposer gets:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{No attribution}: a note asking the proposer to read the traces itself.
\item \textbf{RRSI analysis}: the round's own analysis report and trace digests, unchanged.
\item \textbf{First write}, \textbf{binary search} (Pro) and \textbf{counterfactual search@40}: for each failing trace, the step the method named and an excerpt of it, with no reason, component or method name (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
\item \textbf{Oracle step} (post hoc): the same digest naming the step with the largest rescue gain in the trace's own profile, for the 60 traces where some step had a positive gain.
\item \textbf{No reading} (post hoc): two groups whose proposer cannot open failing traces (a read returns a notice; passing traces stay readable), given either RRSI's analysis, or for the same 60 traces the oracle step with its excerpt, the oracle's diagnosis and the correction that rescued most often there (\emph{oracle fix}).
\end{itemize}
The proposer works from the traces RRSI builds from the incumbent's evaluation on training tasks (81 failing traces in all, 42 retail and 39 airline) and, as in RRSI, can read each of them, grading report included, except in the no-reading groups. Each round drafts two candidates and puts them through RRSI's critic, smoke test and full evaluation on training tasks, after which RRSI's own rule decides whether to accept one. A candidate is \emph{deployable} if it passes these gates with a harness error rate of at most 2\%. Every deployable candidate and every incumbent was deployed on 240 held-out episodes (retail 40 tasks $\times$ 6, airline 20 $\times$ 12).

\paragraph{Analysis.} A candidate slot's gain $G$ is its held-out success minus its incumbent's, in points, and 0 if it was not deployable, since the loop then keeps the incumbent. A contrast between two groups is the mean over the eight states of the difference in their mean gains. The six primary contrasts compare each step attribution with no attribution and with RRSI's analysis; $p$ values come from permuting group labels within states and are Holm-adjusted, and 95\% intervals from a $t$ distribution over states (df 7). A contrast whose 90\% interval lies within $\pm2.5$ points, about one standard deviation of a single candidate's gain in earlier runs, counts as practically equivalent. The margin reflects the noise of one deployment, not practical value: over 20 rounds the four source runs moved held-out success by $-4.3$ to $+7.9$ points, so a useful round gains under a point. We therefore also report the smallest margin each interval excludes, and the outcome that matters to the loop, the gain of the candidate RRSI accepts. Grouping the eight states by their four source runs is a post hoc check. The design, with an expected standard error of 0.9 points per contrast, was registered before any attribution was run on these traces, and no held-out result was looked at until all 62 deployments had finished.

\paragraph{No contrast was significant.} Of 80 drafts, 54 were deployable, 9 to 13 per group (Table~\ref{tab:repairs}); none failed the error check. No primary contrast was significant (Holm-adjusted $p=1.0$ for all six). Repairs drafted from first-write and binary-search steps were practically equivalent to those drafted from RRSI's analysis: $+0.31$ $[-0.56,+1.18]$ and $+0.29$ $[-0.82,+1.39]$ points; their 90\% intervals exclude differences beyond 1.0 and 1.2 points, not a typical round's gain. Counterfactual search was 2.14 points below RRSI's analysis $[-5.67,+1.40]$, mostly through two airline candidates that lost 23 and 14 points. The contrasts with no attribution are dominated by one candidate drafted without attribution whose write gate blocked nearly every write ($-72.5$ points; without it the group's mean gain was $+0.17$).

\paragraph{The step groups differed little in rescue score.} Post hoc, we computed rescue profiles for the 81 failing traces these proposers saw (\$5.0); only 33 were rescuable. On them the first write scored 0.27, binary search 0.41, counterfactual search 0.30 and the earliest step RRSI's digests cite 0.26 (Table~\ref{tab:repairs}), and pointer scores correlated only weakly with candidates' gains (Spearman .15 within states, $p=.14$). In no group was the mean gain above zero. Rounds rarely accepted a candidate (11 of 64), and RRSI's own rule rejected all three that lost more than 10 points, so the accepted round's gain was usually 0 and differed little between groups (post hoc, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:accepted}): against RRSI's analysis, $0.00$ to $+0.57$ points, all 95\% intervals within $\pm2.7$.

\paragraph{An oracle pointer did not help either.} To test a pointer that scores highest by construction, we ran a sixth group post hoc with the step of largest rescue gain (\$5.9), with held-out results unseen until its deployments had finished. Its candidates' mean gain was $-0.39$ points, practically equivalent to RRSI's analysis ($-0.05$ $[-1.97,+1.87]$; within $\pm2.1$ with states grouped by run). Its pointers were chosen on the replays that scored them; with fresh oracle samples and replays (post hoc, \$2.3; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fresh}) their gain on rescuable failures fell from 0.74 to 0.59, still above binary search's 0.42 and RRSI's 0.16.

\paragraph{Without reading, an oracle's diagnosis and correction did not help either.} To give proposers evidence they could not find themselves, we ran the two no-reading groups post hoc (\$12.4), keeping their held-out results unseen until all their deployments had finished. Blocking the reads did not change the repairs drafted from RRSI's analysis ($-0.05$ $[-1.31,+1.21]$ points against the same evidence with reading), and the oracle's step, diagnosis and correction did not improve on RRSI's analysis ($+0.39$ $[-0.98,+1.76]$; 90\% interval within $\pm1.5$, or $\pm2.0$ with states grouped by run), although on fresh replays its corrections kept a gain of 0.65 of their 0.85.

\paragraph{Proposers took up the evidence but did not fix the cases it named.} The proposers' logs and drafts show where the evidence was lost (post hoc). In every group that could read them, the proposer opened 97--100\% of the failing traces, and without any attribution 95--97\% of the reads of a failing trace covered the step each pointer named. Each draft lists the training tasks it expects to fix; given the oracle's evidence, drafts named 35--42\% of the traces that carried it, against 22--28\% from RRSI's analysis ($+16$ points over states, $[-6,+39]$). But in RRSI's evaluation of the drafts on the training tasks, the failing tasks a draft named improved no more than those it did not name ($-3.3$ points $[-10.0,+3.5]$ over 87 drafts; $-12.5$ $[-26.0,+1.0]$ with the oracle's evidence). The evidence was lost in turning a diagnosis into an edit that fixes even the case it targets, before any question of transfer.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the score measures.} $R_k$ belongs to a step and an oracle: how often the oracle proposes a correction, times how well its corrections work. A high $R_k$ marks an effective point of recovery for an oracle that knows the intended outcome, not where the failure began: with the grading the oracle repaired writes best (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decompkinds}), without it almost never, and auditors judged most such repairs invalid. It ranks methods consistently, but individual best rescue steps are noisy (split halves agreed on 56\%), so reusing them as per-failure labels calls for gains estimated on held-out samples.

\paragraph{Implications for self-improving loops.} In this loop the evidence interventions brought no detectable incremental benefit: proposers took up even the oracle's evidence, but their edits did not fix the cases it named, and most edits moved held-out success by less than 5 points (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:repairs}). Without a positive control we cannot separate weak attribution utility from an insensitive repair process, so a bottleneck after attribution remains a hypothesis. RRSI's re-evaluation rejected every edit that misfired broadly: the evaluation that decides which repairs to keep protected the agent more than the attribution behind them.

\section{Conclusion}

Evaluated against counterfactual rescue, attribution methods were ranked as much by the evaluation's design as by their own quality: the null control, what the oracle may know, the target the judges are asked for and how search chooses each moved the ranking. Under an oracle that reads the grading a free structural rule was competitive; its lead came from writes the oracle could repair only with the grading, by corrections auditors mostly judged invalid, judges and search matched it once aligned with the metric, and without the grading it disappeared. Neither the oracle's best step nor its diagnosis and correction detectably improved the loop's repairs, in an experiment without a positive control. Replay-based attribution benchmarks should report the null control, the oracle's information and the target with every ranking.

\section*{Limitations}
All agents, oracles and attributors use one model family (DeepSeek-V4); the second oracle is a smaller model of the same family. The failures come from one self-improvement loop and three domains, with 37 to 65 rescuable failures each, so per-domain intervals are wide. The reference rests on one kind of intervention, a single action corrected by an oracle that sees the grading: it says where a correction informed by the intended outcome helps, not where an error began. Without the grading the oracle rarely rescued writes and the ranking changed; its full profiles cover a random 100 failures, too few rescuable ones for a stable ranking, and an oracle from another model family was not available to us. A mechanical screen found few corrections that use unseen values or do more than one step, but it cannot detect a choice made with the grading. Two auditors labelled a stratified sample of 60 corrections independently (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). Agreement was substantial on validity (82\%, $\kappa=.63$) and scope ($\kappa=.90$) but low on unseen information ($\kappa=.20$; each found 4 cases, one in common). The sample is stratified, so its shares are not population estimates; of the 41 corrections without a mechanical flag, 24 and 26 were valid. Our re-scoring without state-changing $\tau^2$ corrections also drops the bookings both auditors judged valid. We release failures, profiles, replay outcomes and code, and the audit sample with both auditors' labels (without free-text notes) and the mechanical flags. Individual best rescue steps are noisy: split halves agreed on 56\%. The threshold of 0.5 and four samples per step were fixed on a 20-failure pilot. Many analyses are post hoc: the robustness checks, the observed-action control, the second oracle, the oracle without grading, the split by kind of step, the admissibility screen, the human audit and the re-scoring without state-changing corrections, the prompt variants, the judges without grading information, the informed search, search with every suspect tested, the full profiles without grading, the gold-deviation rule, the reading logs and the drafts' targets, and in the repair experiment the rescue scores of its pointers and their fresh replays, its run-grouped and accepted-round intervals, and its oracle and no-reading groups. Our registered prompts asked for the earliest decisive mistake while the metric rewards the largest gain over null, and registered search stopped at the first passing suspect, so the registered comparison understates the judges and search. Our counterfactual search labels steps by replay but trains no attributor, unlike AgenTracer \citep{zhang2026agentracer}, so its results do not speak to trained attributors. Fourteen retail failures were drawn after the others, from the two further runs of the loop that the repair experiment also used (one trace is in both). Two of 6{,}540 AppWorld replays could not run (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). Who\&When is a multi-agent benchmark with a different format; only methods without replay were run there. The repair experiment covers $\tau^2$ only, with eight states from four runs of the loop, 16 candidate slots per group and 33 rescuable failures among its 81 failing traces. Its margin of 2.5 points reflects the noise of one deployment and exceeds a typical round's gain, and its intervals widen when states are grouped by run; it rules out large differences from RRSI's analysis, not small ones that would compound over a long loop. Only 11 of 64 rounds accepted a candidate, and its contrasts with no attribution are dominated by one candidate. It has no positive control: we did not show that this proposer turns useful evidence into held-out gains at these states, so its null result cannot separate weak attribution from an insensitive repair process. The registered digests carried only the step; the post hoc groups added a diagnosis and a correction, but no group named a harness component to change, and all used one loop and one proposer model.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
All environments use synthetic users and data. Attribution methods that misdiagnose failures can lead self-improving agents to change the wrong behaviour; our results suggest that such diagnoses should be checked against simple baselines before they drive automatic changes.

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\scriptsize, breaklines, breakanywhere]

\paragraph{Robustness details.}

\paragraph{Admissibility by kind of step.} At the first write's steps on rescuable failures, no correction used an identifier, name or date the agent had not seen, 0 to 4.5\% used an unseen number (airline writes the most), and 0 to 3\% added two or more calls. At best rescue steps one of 553 corrections used an unseen identifier (an AppWorld write); two or more extra calls were most common at airline utterances (12\% of 85), where a correction replaced a message by several lookups, and absent at retail steps.

\paragraph{Intervention scope in AppWorld.} A correction replaces one code block by another of similar size: 5.4 lines against 5.3 in the observed block (median 3, 90th percentile 13) and 1.04 API calls against 0.96; 97\% have at most one call more than the observed block (or than one call). At best rescue steps 78\% of a correction's lines are new. A corrected block thus often does more than a conversational turn, but no more than the agent's own blocks do.

\paragraph{Individual profiles.} Choosing the best rescue step on two oracle samples and two null replays and re-choosing it on the other two (all 12 splits, all 300 failures), the halves agreed for 56\% of the failures both found rescuable (72\% within one step; 52--58\% by domain), and the step chosen on one half gained 0.48 on the other against 0.75 in sample. With all four samples, two independent runs agreed for 15 of 17 failures. Profiles therefore rank methods consistently while individual best steps carry noticeable selection noise.

\paragraph{Drafts' targets.} Each RRSI draft lists, for each edit, the training tasks it expects to fix. \section{Registration and Deviations}
\label{app:prereg}

The registration (methods, prompts, metrics, the primary comparison and the budget) was committed before any method ran on the main set. Changes to the attribution study, each logged before the data it affected were analysed:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Reference protocol.} The registration left the protocol to be chosen on the pilot by test-retest agreement. After the earliest-step protocol agreed on 42\% of pilot pairs, protocol~3 and the rescue-gain metrics replaced it (Addendum~1), while methods were running and before any main-set profile existed.
\item \textbf{Retail top-up.} The first loop's harnesses yielded 86 distinct retail failures under the sampling rule; 14 more were drawn with the same rule from two further runs of the loop, as registered. All methods moved by at most 0.02 when they were added.
\item \textbf{AppWorld.} Registered in Addendum~2 before any AppWorld failure was drawn. Two corrected replays of one step failed on every attempt because the oracle's code passed a literal ellipsis that AppWorld's request log cannot serialise; they count as corrections not applied. That failure's other corrections at the step rescued nothing and its largest gain was 0.375, so they could not have made it rescuable.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Repair experiment.} Registered separately, before any attribution was run on its traces and before any candidate was drafted. As checks, the RRSI-analysis group's mean gain ($-0.34$) matched that of the candidates the original rounds had drafted from the same evidence ($-0.42$), and re-deploying each incumbent moved its held-out success by $-2.5$ to $+3.3$ points against its earlier deployment, within the noise of 240 episodes. The first state served as a pilot; it exposed no bug, so its cells were kept, as registered. Two operational problems had no effect on data: a lookup by abbreviated commit hash re-invoked finished deployments, which skipped every episode as already done, and a container restart interrupted the deployments, which resumed with finished episodes kept. One secondary test changed. The registered code tested the round-level contrasts by permuting slots, but a round has one accepted candidate, so each cell's value was copied to its two slots and the permutation split the copies between groups, which is not a valid test. We noticed this after the first run of the analysis, which gave $p=.009$ for first write against no attribution, and replaced the test by an exact sign-flip test over the eight state differences ($p\ge.25$); estimates and intervals are unchanged.

\paragraph{Post-hoc analyses.} Not registered: the re-scoring under other reference choices, the null-replay rates by step position, the decomposition of the null-free score and the retest agreement among failures both runs found rescuable (Table~\ref{tab:robust}, Figure~\ref{fig:nullpos};
