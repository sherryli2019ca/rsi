\title{Where Did the Agent Go Wrong? Evaluating Failure Attribution\\ Against Counterfactual Rescue}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Self-improving agent loops start by attributing each failure to a step, usually by asking an LLM to read the trace. We evaluate attribution against counterfactual rescue: for every step we measure how much one correction raises the success of continued replays above uncorrected replays from that step, and score a method by this rescue gain at the step it names. On 300 natural failures from a self-improvement loop in $\tau^2$-bench retail and airline and in AppWorld, with registered analyses, a zero-cost rule that names the first state-changing action was competitive with every LLM method: it scored highest under the registered prompts (0.42, against 0.20--0.34), not significantly above the best, and judges asked post hoc for the quantity the metric measures tied with it. Counterfactual search did not detectably beat one judge call at seven times the cost; post hoc, giving it the judges' grading information helped about as much as its replays did. The ranking held under a second oracle, the observed action as control, split samples and a screen of the oracle's corrections, but not without null replays, which keep early steps from earning credit for chance recoveries. Repairs drafted from these attributions, or from the step of largest rescue gain, were practically equivalent on held-out tasks to those from the loop's own analysis, also when the proposer could not read the failing traces and got the oracle's diagnosis and correction instead.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Agents that improve themselves from their own failures share a first step. An analyst model reads a failed episode, decides where it went wrong, and hands that diagnosis to a proposer that edits the agent's prompts, tools or code \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,zhang2026ace,chen2026harnessfix,rrsi2026}. If the diagnosis points at the wrong step, the proposer may repair the wrong thing. Failure attribution has therefore become a task of its own, with benchmarks of human-labelled multi-agent logs \citep{zhang2025whichagent,liu2026whowhenpro}, taxonomies of failure modes \citep{cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast,zhu2025agentdebug}, and methods that replay a failure with a step changed \citep{zhang2026agentracer,ma2025dover,lin2026reflect,bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}.

Three questions are rarely asked together: is an attribution correct in the sense a repair loop needs, is it worth its cost, and does a more accurate attribution make the loop's repairs better? A human label says which step an annotator blames; a repair loop needs a step whose correction would have made the episode succeed. We evaluate replay-based methods and LLM judges against a reference defined by rescue, report cost next to the score, and hand each attribution to a working loop to measure the repairs it produces.

Defining that reference was the hard part. Naming the \emph{earliest} step whose single correction beats replays without correction was unstable (\S\ref{sec:gt}), because a failure usually has several rescuing steps and which one is found first depends on the corrections an oracle happens to write. We therefore measure a \emph{rescue profile}: for every step, the mean gain in success over null replays across four independent corrections. A method is scored by the rescue gain at the step it names, and a failure is \emph{rescuable} when some step reaches a gain of 0.5. The null replays, which continue the unchanged episode from the same step, turned out to matter most: without them an LLM judge would rank first among the methods and the rule that wins with them sixth.

We apply the profile to 300 natural failures of agents built by a self-improvement loop, RRSI \citep{rrsi2026}, in $\tau^2$-bench's retail and airline domains \citep{barres2025tau2} and in AppWorld with a code agent \citep{trivedi2024appworld}, and compare ten attribution methods, from LLM judges of different cost and design \citep{zhang2025whichagent} to counterfactual search with up to 40 replays and two structural rules. Methods, metrics and analyses were registered before any rescue profile was computed. A second registered experiment re-runs one round of RRSI at eight of its own states in $\tau^2$ with each attribution, or none, given to the proposer, and deploys every resulting repair on 240 held-out episodes (\S\ref{sec:repairs}); post hoc, we repeat it with a proposer that cannot read the failing traces. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A reference by rescue profile} with null controls. It is stable across independent reruns (of 17 retested failures that both runs found rescuable, 15 had the same best rescue step), and post hoc the method ranking held across oracle samples, thresholds, a second oracle, the observed action as control, split samples and a screen for corrections that use information the agent had not seen. Without null replays the ranking reverses, because uncorrected replays from early steps often succeed by chance (\S\ref{sec:robust}).
\item \textbf{A cost-aware comparison of ten attribution methods} on 300 natural failures. A free structural rule was competitive with all of them: the first write scored highest under the registered prompts, though not significantly above binary search ($+0.08$ $[-0.02,+0.18]$), and judges asked post hoc for the quantity the metric measures tied with it. Counterfactual search did not detectably beat a single judge call at seven times the cost; post hoc, grading information and replays each raised it by 0.06 to 0.07, to the level of the registered judges. The cheapest judge matched the most expensive, and which simple rule wins depends on the domain (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{A test on repairs.} Repairs drafted from first-write or binary-search steps, or from the step of largest rescue gain, were practically equivalent on held-out tasks to those drafted from RRSI's own analysis (90\% intervals within $\pm2.5$ points, though not within a typical round's gain), and counterfactual search's were not better. RRSI's proposers read nearly every failing trace whatever they were shown; when they could not, the oracle's diagnosis and correction at that step still gave no better repairs than RRSI's analysis (\S\ref{sec:repairs}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Failure attribution.} Who\&When introduced automated attribution of the responsible agent and step in multi-agent logs, with all-at-once, step-by-step and binary-search judges, and found step-level accuracy far below agent-level accuracy \citep{zhang2025whichagent}; Who\&When Pro revisits these labels \citep{liu2026whowhenpro}. Taxonomies organise failures into modes, including adaptive ones \citep{cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast}, and AgentDebug pairs a taxonomy with targeted feedback \citep{zhu2025agentdebug,zhu2026agentdebugx}; ErrorProbe improves diagnosis from its own history \citep{li2026errorprobe}. MP-Bench argues that a failure often admits several plausible attributions and finds that earlier conclusions about LLMs' weakness at attribution owe much to benchmark design \citep{in2026rethinking}. These evaluations score agreement with human or injected labels; we score the effect of correcting the named step, which credits every step whose correction rescues the episode, and we likewise find that how judges are asked changes their score.

\paragraph{Counterfactual attribution.} AgenTracer labels failures by replaying corrected steps of injected faults and trains an attributor on them \citep{zhang2026agentracer}; DoVer and REFLECT intervene on a trace to validate hypotheses \citep{ma2025dover,lin2026reflect}; CausalFlow and Causal Agent Replay attribute failures by counterfactual replay \citep{bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}. Our reference uses the same intervention with null replays from the same step and a full profile over steps instead of the first step that flips. The profile is a graded, sampled version of but-for causation \citep{halpern2016actual,pearl2009causality}.

\paragraph{Self-improving agents.} Reflexion, ExpEL and ACE turn failures into lessons or playbooks \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,zhang2026ace}; HarnessFix diagnoses and repairs harness flaws \citep{chen2026harnessfix}; RRSI digests failed traces, proposes harness edits and keeps them by re-evaluation \citep{rrsi2026}. Prediction of failure does not imply that intervening helps \citep{vasudev2026intervention}. We evaluate the diagnostic step these loops share, on failures one of them produced.

\section{A Reference: Rescue Profiles}
\label{sec:gt}

\paragraph{Replays.} A failed episode is a sequence of agent steps $0,\dots,n-1$. A \emph{replay from step $k$} keeps steps $0,\dots,k-1$ and the environment state they produced, and lets the same agent and user simulator continue; a \emph{corrected} replay forces a given action at step $k$ first. The agent runs at temperature 0, but replays still vary, so each outcome is a sample.

\paragraph{Oracle corrections.} For each step an oracle (DeepSeek-V4-Pro, thinking off) sees the domain policy, the tools, the episode up to the step and the task's grading criteria: $\tau^2$'s gold actions and required information, or AppWorld's unit tests with their expected values. It either judges the step correct or writes the action the agent should have taken, using only what the agent knew at that point: the criteria tell it the right outcome, not identifiers or facts the agent had not yet seen.

\paragraph{From earliest step to profile.} Our first registered protocol named the earliest step whose correction succeeded on at least 2 of 4 replays more than 4 null replays did. On 20 pilot failures, independent runs of it named the same step in 42\% of pairs, mostly because the oracle corrected a step in one run and not in another. Before computing any profile on the main set, we registered protocol~3 instead.

\paragraph{Protocol 3.} At every step $k$ we draw $K=4$ oracle samples. Each sample that corrects the step gets 2 corrected replays, and the step gets 4 null replays. The rescue gain of step $k$ is
\begin{equation}
R_k=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}c_{k,i}\bigl(\hat{p}^{\,\mathrm{corr}}_{k,i}-\hat{p}^{\,\mathrm{null}}_{k}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $c_{k,i}=1$ if sample $i$ gives an action the replay can apply, and $c_{k,i}=0$ if it judges the step correct or its action cannot be applied. $R_k$ is thus the share of samples that propose a correction times the mean gain of those corrections. A null replay lets the agent act again at step $k$. It reproduced the observed tool calls in 91--95\% of $\tau^2$ null replays and the observed code in 58\% of AppWorld ones (post hoc), so $R_k$ compares forcing the oracle's action with letting the agent act again, which in $\tau^2$ is close to keeping the observed action. A failure is rescuable if $\max_k R_k\ge 0.5$, and then its best rescue step is the step with the largest gain (earliest on ties). We also record the earliest step with $R_k\ge 0.5$. Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows one profile. On the pilot, two independent runs agreed on the best rescue step for 15 of 20 failures.

\paragraph{Scoring a method.} A method names one step $\hat{k}$. Its score on a failure is $\Rhat$, the rescue gain at that step, and 0 for an invalid step. The primary metric is the mean of $\Rhat$ over rescuable failures; it rewards naming any step whose correction helps, in proportion to how much it helps. Secondary metrics include exact agreement with the best rescue step (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:secondary}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Failures.} All failures come from RRSI \citep{rrsi2026} run as a self-improvement loop on a DeepSeek-V4-Flash agent: from the harnesses it accepted over 20 rounds, in their evaluations on training tasks and in their deployment on held-out tasks. In $\tau^2$-bench \citep{barres2025tau2} the agent serves a simulated customer through tool calls; in AppWorld \citep{trivedi2024appworld} it writes Python against the APIs of nine apps and submits with a final call. We drew 100 failed episodes per domain with a registered rule: no harness error, at most two per task and harness, tasks taken round-robin in a seeded order. They cover 36 (retail), 24 (airline) and 47 (AppWorld) tasks and average 11.7, 10.3 and 19.6 agent steps.

\paragraph{Methods.} Every LLM method except counterfactual search reads the failure as RRSI's analyst reads it: the task (in $\tau^2$ with the simulated customer's hidden instructions), the episode with numbered steps, and the grading report the agent never sees, which lists $\tau^2$'s gold actions and missing information or AppWorld's failed unit tests with their assertions. The judges therefore have the information the oracle has. Each method names one step.
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Rules}: the \emph{first write}, the first step that calls a state-changing tool or API (else the last step), and the \emph{last step}.
\item \textbf{All-at-once} judge \citep{zhang2025whichagent}: one call over the whole episode, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash, V4-Pro, and V4-Pro with thinking.
\item \textbf{Step-by-step} judge: one call per step, in order, until a step is judged decisive.
\item \textbf{Binary search} judge: halves the episode $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ times, asking which half holds the decisive mistake.
\item \textbf{Root-cause attributor}: one call asking for a root cause, the earliest wrong step and a component.
\item \textbf{RRSI trace digester} \citep{rrsi2026}: RRSI's own multi-turn failure digest; we take the earliest step it cites as evidence.
\item \textbf{Counterfactual search} (CF search), replay-based like AgenTracer's labelling \citep{zhang2026agentracer} but without grading criteria: one call names up to five suspect steps with corrected actions; suspects are tested in order with 4 corrected and 4 null replays, and the search stops at the first correction that beats null by 2 successes or when its budget of 40 replays runs out; otherwise it answers with the first suspect.
\end{itemize}
Judges are prompted for the decisive mistake, defined as the earliest step whose correction would most likely have rescued the episode (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}); post hoc reruns change this definition or withhold the grading information. Pro runs with thinking off unless stated.

\paragraph{Registration and analysis.} Methods, prompts, metrics and analyses were registered, and all methods had been run, before the main profiles were computed; no method sees a profile. The registered primary comparison is counterfactual search at budget 40 against the all-at-once judge with Pro: the paired difference in $\Rhat$ over rescuable failures, with a 95\% bootstrap interval over task clusters (10{,}000 draws) and a paired permutation test flipping signs by task, on retail and airline pooled and, for AppWorld, separately. Everything else is estimated with cluster-bootstrap intervals and no further tests; unregistered analyses are marked post hoc or exploratory. A seeded 10\% of failures in each domain got a second, independent profile. Costs are list-price dollars for tokens and replays. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} lists every change made after registration.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Profiles.} Of 300 failures, 157 were rescuable: 65 of 100 in retail, 37 in airline and 55 in AppWorld. Null replays from the same step succeeded 27\%, 18\% and 11\% of the time; without them, many rescues would be chance recoveries. A third of the rescuable failures (18, 11 and 26) had two or more steps with a gain of at least 0.5. In the retest, 27 of 30 failures got the same verdict on rescuability, 15 of the 17 that both runs found rescuable the same best rescue step (the other two one and two steps apart), and step-level gains correlated .63 to .80.

\paragraph{Under the registered prompts, the first-write rule scored highest.} Table~\ref{tab:main} gives every method's rescue gain. Pooled over the three domains, the first write scored 0.42 [0.33, 0.51]; the LLM methods scored between 0.20 (step-by-step) and 0.34 (binary search). The first write's lead over binary search was not significant ($+0.08$, exploratory, $[-0.02,+0.18]$); over the all-at-once judges it was $+0.11$ to $+0.12$. Exact agreement with the best rescue step ordered the methods the same way (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:secondary}). At the steps the methods named, the oracle proposed a correction about equally often (0.52--0.64 of samples); the first write led because corrections at its steps worked better (mean gain 0.61, against 0.34--0.51; post hoc, Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). Cost bought little: the all-at-once judges with Flash (\$0.001 per failure), Pro (\$0.003) and Pro with thinking (\$0.014) scored 0.30, 0.30 and 0.31 (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). On Who\&When our judges reproduce the published pattern (Appendix~\ref{sec:whowhen}).

\paragraph{Replays and grading information both helped search.} Counterfactual search at budget 40 scored 0.26 pooled. The registered comparison with the all-at-once judge (Pro) detected no difference, with intervals that admit 0.1 either way: $-0.061$ $[-0.146,+0.032]$ in $\tau^2$ (permutation $p=.20$, $n=102$) and $+0.025$ $[-0.087,+0.136]$ in AppWorld ($p=.69$, $n=55$). It confounds replays with information, since search does not read the grading criteria. Post hoc, we reran search's suspect call with the grading information the judges read, keeping its replays and budget (\$2.4), which completes a $2\times2$ of replays and information (Table~\ref{tab:main}, lower block). Search's first suspect scored 0.22 without the information and 0.27 with it; after replays, 0.26 and 0.35. Information and replays raised the score by similar amounts (main effects $+0.07$ $[+0.01,+0.13]$ and $+0.06$ $[+0.03,+0.10]$; replays $+0.08$ in $\tau^2$, $+0.02$ in AppWorld), with no clear interaction ($+0.03$ $[-0.02,+0.09]$). Informed search confirmed a suspect on 76--80\% of failures, against 42--73\%, with fewer replays. It matched the registered binary-search judge ($+0.01$ $[-0.06,+0.08]$) and fell short of the first write ($-0.07$ $[-0.18,+0.03]$) and of binary search asked for the largest gain ($-0.06$ $[-0.12,+0.01]$), at \$0.015 per failure against \$0.006. Withholding the information from the judges cost them about as much: 0.07 $[0.00,0.13]$ for the all-at-once judge and 0.11 $[0.02,0.20]$ for binary search. These contrasts are post hoc and descriptive, and our search is not a reimplementation of AgenTracer, which trains an attributor on replay labels. Registered search stopped improving at 24 replays (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

\paragraph{Asked for the measured quantity, judges tied with the rule.} Judges that missed the best rescue step usually named an earlier one (in retail, the all-at-once judge with Pro on 40 of 65 rescuable failures). The registered prompts asked for the earliest decisive mistake, while $R_k$ measures the gain of a correction over letting the agent act again. Post hoc, we re-ran three judges asking instead for the step whose correction would most likely rescue the task, or would most increase success over letting the agent act again (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The second, which matches the metric, raised the Pro judges by 0.07 (all-at-once 0.30 to 0.36, $[+0.02,+0.12]$; binary search 0.34 to 0.40, $[-0.01,+0.13]$; Table~\ref{tab:main}) and the Flash judge by 0.01. Binary search asked this way tied with the first write ($-0.02$ $[-0.10,+0.07]$), so the first write's lead under the registered prompts owes much to how the judges were asked.

\paragraph{The best rule depends on the domain.} The first write led in retail (0.53, against 0.14--0.33 for LLM methods) and AppWorld (0.39); in airline the all-at-once judges led (0.41--0.42, against 0.27). Most best rescue steps were writes in retail (36 of 65), usually a wrong write replaced by the right one, and utterances in airline (25 of 37), typically a refusal or a clarifying question the agent should have made before acting. In AppWorld they lay late, on average 77\% of the way through the episode, so the last step scored 0.36 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:kinds}). Binary search was the only LLM method whose score held across domains (0.33--0.35). A rule that reads $\tau^2$'s gold actions, as the judges do, scored 0.50 (post hoc; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:gold}), though the oracle corrects from the same gold actions.

\paragraph{The ranking is robust, except to the null control.}
\label{sec:robust}
The reference rests on choices: four oracle samples per step, a threshold of 0.5, null replays as the control, one oracle model, and an oracle that reads the grading. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robust} re-scores every method under other choices (post hoc; details in Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). With fewer samples, thresholds from 0.25 to 0.75, only failures with a single rescuing step, or failures chosen on two samples and scored on the other two, the first write stayed first and binary search the best LLM method (Kendall's $\tau$ of 0.78 to 1.00). With the observed action forced as the control, the judges gained most, and binary search asked for the largest gain tied with the first write (0.45 against 0.44, $-0.01$ $[-0.10,+0.07]$). A second oracle model (V4-Flash) found 120 failures rescuable instead of 157 and agreed with the first on the best rescue step for 71--72\% of those both found rescuable, less often than two runs of the same oracle did; under its profile the first write still ranked first ($\tau=.78$). The oracle may use the grading only to know the right outcome. A mechanical screen of the 4{,}795 corrections that entered a profile (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:admiss}) found an identifier, name or date the agent had not yet seen in 0.7\% and two or more calls beyond the observed step in 1.8\%; dropping them left the ranking unchanged ($\tau=1.00$). Dropping the null replays reversed it: 203 failures became rescuable, the first write fell to sixth place, and the all-at-once judge with thinking ranked first (0.42 against 0.37). Uncorrected replays from the first fifth of an episode succeeded 41--44\% of the time and from the last fifth 2--6\% (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:nullpos}), and LLM methods other than binary search named steps on average 53--67\% of the way through an episode, against 77\% for best rescue steps, so without the null control they collect credit for recoveries that happen anyway. Replay-based labels \citep{zhang2026agentracer,ma2025dover,bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car} need such a control.

\section{Do Better Attributions Give Better Repairs?}
\label{sec:repairs}

\paragraph{Design.} A rescue score is a proxy: a loop needs attributions only to repair its agent. We tested this on RRSI itself, in a separately registered experiment. We took two other runs of the loop in each $\tau^2$ domain and the harness each run had accepted by the start of rounds 5 and 15, giving eight incumbent states. At each state we re-ran one unmodified RRSI round five times, changing only the evidence the proposer gets:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{No attribution}: a note asking the proposer to read the traces itself.
\item \textbf{RRSI analysis}: the round's own analysis report and trace digests, unchanged.
\item \textbf{First write}, \textbf{binary search} (Pro) and \textbf{counterfactual search@40}: for each failing trace, the step the method named and an excerpt of it, with no reason, component or method name (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
\item \textbf{Oracle step} (post hoc, after the registered analysis): the same digest naming the step with the largest rescue gain in the trace's own profile, for the 60 traces where some step had a positive gain.
\item \textbf{No reading} (post hoc, after the third review): two groups whose proposer cannot open failing traces (a read returns a notice; passing traces stay readable), given either RRSI's analysis, or for the same 60 traces the oracle step with its excerpt, the oracle's diagnosis and the correction that rescued most often there (\emph{oracle fix}).
\end{itemize}
The proposer works from the traces RRSI builds from the incumbent's evaluation on training tasks (81 failing traces in all, 42 retail and 39 airline) and, as in RRSI, can read each of them, grading report included, except in the no-reading groups. Each round drafts two candidates and puts them through RRSI's critic, smoke test and full evaluation on training tasks, after which RRSI's own rule decides whether to accept one. A candidate is \emph{deployable} if it passes these gates with a harness error rate of at most 2\%. Every deployable candidate and every incumbent was deployed on 240 held-out episodes (retail 40 tasks $\times$ 6, airline 20 $\times$ 12).

\paragraph{Analysis.} A candidate slot's gain $G$ is its held-out success minus its incumbent's, in points, and 0 if it was not deployable, since the loop then keeps the incumbent. A contrast between two groups is the mean over the eight states of the difference in their mean gains. The six primary contrasts compare each step attribution with no attribution and with RRSI's analysis; $p$ values come from permuting group labels within states and are Holm-adjusted, and 95\% intervals from a $t$ distribution over states (df 7). A contrast whose 90\% interval lies within $\pm2.5$ points, about one standard deviation of a single candidate's gain in earlier runs, counts as practically equivalent. The margin reflects the noise of one deployment, not a threshold of practical value: an accepted round of the loop rarely gains more than a point, so we also report the smallest margin each interval excludes. The interval treats the eight states as independent, though they come from four runs of the loop, two checkpoints each; grouping them by run is a post hoc check. The design, with an expected standard error of 0.9 points per contrast, was registered before any attribution was run on these traces, and no held-out result was looked at until all 62 deployments had finished.

\paragraph{No contrast was significant.} Of 80 drafts, 54 were deployable, 9 to 13 per group (Table~\ref{tab:repairs}); none failed the error check. No primary contrast was significant (Holm-adjusted $p=1.0$ for all six). Repairs drafted from first-write and binary-search steps were practically equivalent to those drafted from RRSI's analysis: $+0.31$ $[-0.56,+1.18]$ and $+0.29$ $[-0.82,+1.39]$ points; their 90\% intervals exclude differences beyond 1.0 and 1.2 points, or 1.5 and 1.4 points with the states grouped by run, but not differences the size of a typical round's gain. Counterfactual search was 2.14 points below RRSI's analysis $[-5.67,+1.40]$, mostly through two airline candidates that lost 23 and 14 points. The contrasts with no attribution span about $\pm10$ points because of one candidate drafted without attribution, whose write gate silently blocked nearly every write ($-72.5$ points); without it, that group's mean gain was $+0.17$ (exploratory).

\paragraph{The step groups differed little in rescue score.} Post hoc, we computed rescue profiles for the 81 failing traces these proposers saw (\$5.0); only 33 were rescuable (17 retail, 16 airline). On them the first write scored 0.27, binary search 0.41, counterfactual search 0.30 and the earliest step RRSI's own digests cite 0.26 (Table~\ref{tab:repairs}): the first write was no better a pointer here than RRSI's analysis ($+0.01$ $[-0.15,+0.19]$), and binary search better but not significantly ($+0.15$ $[-0.03,+0.35]$). Across the 64 candidate slots of the step and oracle groups, the rescue score of the pointers a proposer received correlated only weakly with its candidates' gains (Spearman .15 within states, permutation $p=.14$). Most repairs did not help on held-out tasks in any group: 21--60\% of deployable candidates gained, and no group's mean gain was above zero. RRSI's own rule rejected all three candidates that lost more than 10 points, so the gain of the accepted candidate differed little between groups ($-0.10$ to $+0.47$ points; exact sign-flip $p\ge.25$).

\paragraph{An oracle pointer did not help either.} To test a pointer that scores highest by construction, we ran a sixth group post hoc with the step of largest rescue gain (0.67 and 0.81 on rescuable failures in retail and airline; \$5.9), keeping its held-out results unseen until its 10 deployments had finished. Its candidates' mean gain was $-0.39$ points, practically equivalent to RRSI's analysis ($-0.05$ $[-1.97,+1.87]$; within $\pm2.1$ with states grouped by run) and to the three registered step groups pooled ($+0.46$ $[-1.62,+2.54]$, though not once grouped by run).

\paragraph{Proposers read the traces themselves.} The proposers' action logs suggest why the evidence mattered so little (post hoc). In every group the proposer opened 97--100\% of the failing traces, 13 to 20 reads per draft, mostly of the whole trace or a long window, and without any attribution 95--97\% of the reads of a failing trace covered the step each of the four pointers named for it.

\paragraph{Without reading, an oracle's diagnosis and correction did not help either.} To give proposers evidence they could not find themselves, we ran the two no-reading groups post hoc (\$12.4), keeping their held-out results unseen until all their deployments had finished. Their proposers tried 1.5 to 2 blocked reads per draft and read 2.3 to 3.5 passing traces instead. Blocking the reads did not change the repairs drafted from RRSI's analysis ($-0.05$ $[-1.31,+1.21]$ points against the same evidence with reading), and the oracle's step, diagnosis and correction did not improve on RRSI's analysis ($+0.39$ $[-0.98,+1.76]$; 90\% interval within $\pm1.5$, or $\pm2.0$ with states grouped by run). From the oracle's evidence 6 of 10 deployable candidates gained, against 3 of 14, but their mean gain was 0.00 points, and accepted candidates gained $+0.10$ against $-0.05$ (sign-flip $p=.75$).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the score measures.} $R_k$ belongs to a step and an oracle: how often the oracle proposes a correction, times how well its corrections work. Corrections of writes worked better than corrections of utterances (in retail 0.52 against 0.13; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decompkinds}), since knowing the expected outcome suffices to write the right call, and a second oracle agreed with the first less than the first agreed with itself. A low $R_k$ can therefore mean that this oracle could not repair a step, not that the step did not matter, and a high $R_k$ marks an effective point of recovery, which need not be where the failure began. The profile is a reference for intervention, not an independent record of where the error lies; the ranking of methods, however, held across both oracles, both controls and the screen of corrections.

\paragraph{Why a free rule is competitive.} In customer service a wrong database write often decides a failure, and the first write is often that write; where the decisive mistake was conversational, as in airline, judges led. A judge's score needs a domain baseline and a definition of the target that matches its use.

\paragraph{Implications for self-improving loops.} Neither a pointer to the step of largest rescue gain, with about three times the rescue score of RRSI's own analysis on the same failures, nor that step's diagnosis and correction given to a proposer that could not read the traces, gave repairs better than the loop's own analysis (\S\ref{sec:repairs}). This suggests that in this loop the difficulty lay after attribution, in turning a diagnosis into a harness edit that helps on unseen tasks: most edits in every group moved held-out success by less than 5 points (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:repairs}). RRSI's re-evaluation rejected every edit that misfired broadly: the evaluation that decides which repairs to keep protected the agent more than the attribution behind them.

\section{Conclusion}

Against counterfactual rescue with null controls, a free structural rule was competitive with LLM attribution, and grading information helped counterfactual search as much as its replays did. Neither the step of largest rescue gain nor its diagnosis and correction improved the repairs of the loop we studied, whether or not its proposer could read the failing traces. Rescue profiles rank methods consistently across retests, oracles, controls and screens of the corrections, but only with null replays. We release failures, profiles and code.

\section*{Limitations}
All agents, oracles and attributors use one model family (DeepSeek-V4); the second oracle is a smaller model of the same family. The failures come from one self-improvement loop and three domains, with 37 to 65 rescuable failures each, so per-domain intervals are wide. The reference rests on one kind of intervention, a single action corrected by an oracle that sees the grading: it says where a correction helps, not where an error began, and the oracle repaired writes more readily than utterances. A mechanical screen found few corrections that use information the agent had not seen or do more than one step, but no human has audited the profiles; we release a stratified sample of 60 corrections with an audit sheet for this purpose. The threshold of 0.5 and four samples per step were fixed on a 20-failure pilot. Many analyses are post hoc: the robustness checks, the observed-action control, the second oracle, the admissibility screen, the prompt variants, the judges without grading information, the informed search, the gold-deviation rule, the reading logs, and in the repair experiment the rescue scores of its pointers, its run-grouped intervals, and its oracle and no-reading groups. Our registered prompts asked for the earliest decisive mistake while the metric rewards the largest gain over null, so the registered comparison understates the judges. Our counterfactual search labels steps by replay but trains no attributor, unlike AgenTracer \citep{zhang2026agentracer}, so its results do not speak to trained attributors. Fourteen retail failures were drawn after the others, from the two further runs of the loop that the repair experiment also used (one trace is in both). Two of 6{,}540 AppWorld replays could not run (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). Who\&When is a multi-agent benchmark with a different format; only methods without replay were run there. The repair experiment covers $\tau^2$ only, with eight states from four runs of the loop, 16 candidate slots per group and 33 rescuable failures among its 81 failing traces. Its margin of 2.5 points reflects the noise of one deployment and exceeds a typical round's gain, and its intervals widen when states are grouped by run; it rules out large differences from RRSI's analysis, not small ones, and its contrasts with no attribution are dominated by one candidate. The registered digests carried only the step; the post hoc groups added a diagnosis and a correction, but no group named a harness component to change, and all used one loop and one proposer model.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
All environments use synthetic users and data. Attribution methods that misdiagnose failures can lead self-improving agents to change the wrong behaviour; our results suggest that such diagnoses should be checked against simple baselines before they drive automatic changes.

\section{Registration and Deviations}
\label{app:prereg}

The registration (methods, prompts, metrics, the primary comparison and the budget) was committed before any method ran on the main set. Changes to the attribution study, each logged before the data it affected were analysed:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Reference protocol.} The registration left the protocol to be chosen on the pilot by test-retest agreement. After the earliest-step protocol agreed on 42\% of pilot pairs, protocol~3 and the rescue-gain metrics replaced it (Addendum~1), while methods were running and before any main-set profile existed.
\item \textbf{Retail top-up.} The first loop's harnesses yielded 86 distinct retail failures under the sampling rule; 14 more were drawn with the same rule from two further runs of the loop, as registered. All methods moved by at most 0.02 when they were added.
\item \textbf{AppWorld.} Registered in Addendum~2 before any AppWorld failure was drawn. Two corrected replays of one step failed on every attempt because the oracle's code passed a literal ellipsis that AppWorld's request log cannot serialise; they count as corrections not applied. That failure's other corrections at the step rescued nothing and its largest gain was 0.375, so they could not have made it rescuable.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Repair experiment.} Registered separately, before any attribution was run on its traces and before any candidate was drafted. As checks, the RRSI-analysis group's mean gain ($-0.34$) matched that of the candidates the original rounds had drafted from the same evidence ($-0.42$), and re-deploying each incumbent moved its held-out success by $-2.5$ to $+3.3$ points against its earlier deployment, within the noise of 240 episodes. The first state served as a pilot; it exposed no bug, so its cells were kept, as registered. Two operational problems had no effect on data: a lookup by abbreviated commit hash re-invoked finished deployments, which skipped every episode as already done, and a container restart interrupted the deployments, which resumed with finished episodes kept. One secondary test changed. The registered code tested the round-level contrasts by permuting slots, but a round has one accepted candidate, so each cell's value was copied to its two slots and the permutation split the copies between groups, which is not a valid test. We noticed this after the first run of the analysis, which gave $p=.009$ for first write against no attribution, and replaced the test by an exact sign-flip test over the eight state differences ($p\ge.25$); estimates and intervals are unchanged.

\paragraph{Post-hoc analyses.} Not registered: the re-scoring under other reference choices, the null-replay rates by step position, the decomposition of the null-free score and the retest agreement among failures both runs found rescuable (Table~\ref{tab:robust}, Figure~\ref{fig:nullpos}; \texttt{attrib/robustness.py}); the split of $\Rhat$ into proposals and their gains, search's first suspect, the null replays' reproduction of the observed action and the split-sample scores (Tables~\ref{tab:decomp}, \ref{tab:decompkinds}; \texttt{attrib/review2.py}); the observed-action control (\texttt{attrib/orig\_control.py}); the second oracle (\texttt{attrib/second\_oracle.py}, \texttt{attrib/groundtruth3.py} with another oracle model, sharing null replays where both oracles flagged a step);
