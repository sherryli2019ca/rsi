\title{Where Did the Agent Go Wrong? Evaluating Failure Attribution\\ Against Counterfactual Rescue}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Self-improving agent loops often start by asking an LLM which step caused each failure. We evaluate attribution against counterfactual rescue: we measure how much one oracle correction at each step raises the success of continued replays over uncorrected ones, and score a method by this gain at the step it names. On 300 natural failures from a self-improvement loop in $\tau^2$-bench and AppWorld, evaluation choices changed which method ranked first, and few differences between methods were significant. Null replays decided which failures counted as rescuable. In the registered analysis a zero-cost rule naming the first state-changing action scored highest (0.42 vs.\ 0.20--0.34), not significantly; judges and search aligned post hoc with the metric came within 0.02. The rule's lead rests on an oracle that reads the grading: without it the oracle rarely rescued writes, and two independent auditors accepted only 3 and 6 of 12 distinct state-changing $\tau^2$ corrections they audited. Without corrections overriding a choice the user had just confirmed, the rule ranked fifth of ten, not significantly below the best judge. In a repair experiment without a positive control, no attribution, not even the oracle's correction, brought a detectable held-out gain over the loop's own analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Agents that improve themselves from their own failures share a first step. An analyst model reads a failed episode, decides where it went wrong, and hands that diagnosis to a proposer that edits the agent's prompts, tools or code \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,zhang2026ace,chen2026harnessfix,rrsi2026}. If the diagnosis points at the wrong step, the proposer may repair the wrong thing. Failure attribution has therefore become a task of its own, with benchmarks of human-labelled multi-agent logs \citep{zhang2025whichagent,liu2026whowhenpro}, taxonomies of failure modes \citep{cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast,zhu2025agentdebug}, and methods that replay a failure with a step changed \citep{zhang2026agentracer,ma2025dover,lin2026reflect,bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}.

Three questions are rarely asked together: is an attribution correct in the sense a repair loop needs, is it worth its cost, and does a more accurate attribution make the loop's repairs better? A human label says which step an annotator blames; a repair loop needs a step whose correction would have made the episode succeed. We evaluate replay-based methods and LLM judges against a reference defined by rescue, report cost next to the score, and hand each attribution to a working loop to measure the repairs it produces.

Defining that reference was the hard part: the \emph{earliest} step whose single correction beats uncorrected replays was unstable, because a failure usually has several rescuing steps. We therefore measure a \emph{rescue profile}: for every step, the mean gain in success over null replays across four independent corrections. A method is scored by the rescue gain at the step it names, and a failure is \emph{rescuable} when some step reaches a gain of 0.5. The null replays, which continue the unchanged episode from the same step, matter most: without them 46 more failures count as rescuable, many of which recover without any correction, and over this larger set an LLM judge ranks first.

We apply the profile to 300 natural failures of agents built by a self-improvement loop, RRSI \citep{rrsi2026}, in $\tau^2$-bench's retail and airline domains \citep{barres2025tau2} and in AppWorld with a code agent \citep{trivedi2024appworld}, and compare ten attribution methods, from LLM judges of different cost and design \citep{zhang2025whichagent} to counterfactual search with up to 40 replays and two structural rules. Methods, metrics and analyses were registered before the main set's rescue profiles were computed. A second registered experiment re-runs one round of RRSI at eight of its own states in $\tau^2$ with each attribution, or none, given to the proposer, and deploys every resulting repair on 240 held-out episodes (\S\ref{sec:repairs}); post hoc, we repeat it with a proposer that cannot read the failing traces. Our main finding concerns the evaluation itself: several of its design choices changed which method ranked first, while few differences between methods were significant. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A reference by rescue profile} with null controls, and its limits. It measures where an oracle that knows the intended outcome can recover a failure, not where the failure began. Post hoc, the method ranking held across oracle samples, thresholds, a second oracle, the observed action as control, a screen of the corrections and the failures that rarely recover by chance, but two choices change it: null replays decide which failures count as rescuable (on the same failures the order held, $\tau=.87$), and without the grading the oracle rarely rescues writes, many of whose corrections two auditors rejected (\S\ref{sec:robust}).
\item \textbf{A cost-aware comparison of ten attribution methods} on 300 natural failures. Under the registered reference and prompts a free structural rule scored highest, not significantly above binary search ($+0.08$ $[-0.02,+0.18]$). The registered judges were asked for the earliest decisive mistake and search lacked the grading, while the metric rewards the largest gain; aligned with the metric post hoc, judges and search came within 0.02 of the rule (not shown equivalent), search at nine times the all-at-once judge's cost and four times binary search's. The rule's lead came from writes the oracle repairs with the grading (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{A test on repairs.} In this configuration no contrast was significant, and repairs drafted from first-write or binary-search steps, from the step of largest rescue gain, or from its diagnosis and correction were practically equivalent to those drafted from RRSI's own analysis (90\% intervals within $\pm2.5$ points, a margin above one round's gain); without a positive control the experiment cannot show whether attribution limits this loop (\S\ref{sec:repairs}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Failure attribution.} Who\&When introduced automated attribution of the responsible agent and step in multi-agent logs with all-at-once, step-by-step and binary-search judges, finding steps far harder than agents \citep{zhang2025whichagent}; Who\&When Pro revisits its labels \citep{liu2026whowhenpro}. Taxonomies organise failures into modes \citep{cemri2025mast,cemri2026adamast,zhu2025agentdebug,zhu2026agentdebugx} and ErrorProbe learns diagnosis from its history \citep{li2026errorprobe}. MP-Bench argues that a failure often admits several plausible attributions and that earlier conclusions owe much to benchmark design \citep{in2026rethinking}. A2P and RAFFLES add counterfactual or verifier reasoning to judges \citep{west2025a2p,zhu2026raffles}; ProcessBench asks for a math solution's first wrong step \citep{zheng2025processbench}. These evaluations score agreement with labels; we score the effect of correcting the named step, and likewise find that how judges are asked changes their score.

\paragraph{Counterfactual attribution.} AgenTracer labels failures by replaying corrected steps of injected faults and trains an attributor on them \citep{zhang2026agentracer}; DoVer and REFLECT intervene on a trace to validate hypotheses \citep{ma2025dover,lin2026reflect}; CausalFlow and Causal Agent Replay attribute failures by counterfactual replay \citep{bonagiri2026causalflow,shah2026car}. We use this intervention as an evaluation reference, with null replays from the same step and a profile over all steps, and show which of its choices move a comparison of methods; where MP-Bench questions single-label conclusions with several labels, we question them through the reference's construction and test downstream repairs. The profile is a graded, sampled version of but-for causation \citep{halpern2016actual,pearl2009causality}.

\paragraph{Self-improving agents.} Reflexion, ExpEL and ACE turn failures into lessons or playbooks \citep{shinn2023reflexion,zhao2024expel,zhang2026ace}; HarnessFix diagnoses and repairs harness flaws \citep{chen2026harnessfix}; RRSI digests failed traces, proposes harness edits and keeps them by re-evaluation \citep{rrsi2026}. Prediction of failure does not imply that intervening helps \citep{vasudev2026intervention}. We evaluate the diagnostic step these loops share, on failures one of them produced.

\section{A Reference: Rescue Profiles}
\label{sec:gt}

\paragraph{Replays.} A failed episode is a sequence of agent steps $0,\dots,n-1$. A \emph{replay from step $k$} keeps steps $0,\dots,k-1$ and the environment state they produced, and lets the same agent and user simulator continue; a \emph{corrected} replay forces a given action at step $k$ first. The agent runs at temperature 0, but replays still vary, so each outcome is a sample.

\paragraph{Oracle corrections.} For each step an oracle (DeepSeek-V4-Pro, thinking off) sees the domain policy, the tools, the episode up to the step and the task's grading criteria: $\tau^2$'s gold actions and required information, or AppWorld's unit tests with their expected values. It either judges the step correct or writes the action the agent should have taken, using only what the agent knew at that point: the criteria tell it the right outcome, not identifiers or facts the agent had not yet seen. The reference is thus repairability by an oracle that knows the intended outcome, privileged information an offline evaluation may use; it does not certify that each correction was justified by what the agent had seen.

\paragraph{Rescue gain.} This protocol replaced an earliest-step protocol whose independent runs agreed on 42\% of pilot failures (Appendix~\ref{app:gt}). At every step $k$ we draw $K=4$ oracle samples. Each sample that gives an applicable correction gets 2 corrected replays, and a step with at least one such sample gets 4 null replays (54\%, 60\% and 39\% of steps in retail, airline and AppWorld; other steps have $R_k=0$). The rescue gain of step $k$ is
\begin{equation}
R_k=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}c_{k,i}\bigl(\hat{p}^{\,\mathrm{corr}}_{k,i}-\hat{p}^{\,\mathrm{null}}_{k}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $c_{k,i}=1$ if sample $i$ gives an action the replay can apply, and $c_{k,i}=0$ if it judges the step correct or its action cannot be applied. $R_k$ is thus the share of samples that propose a correction times the mean gain of those corrections: how useful pointing at step $k$ is to this correction procedure, not a property of the step alone, since a low $R_k$ can mean that the oracle found no correction (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decomp} separates the two). A null replay lets the agent act again at step $k$. It made the observed tool calls (or again none) in 91--95\% of $\tau^2$ null replays but repeated the whole turn, message included, in only 35--36\%, and the observed code in 58\% of AppWorld ones (post hoc), so $R_k$ compares forcing the oracle's action with letting the agent act again, not with keeping its action (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robust} uses the observed action as control). A failure is rescuable if $\max_k R_k\ge 0.5$, and then its best rescue step is the step with the largest gain (earliest on ties). We also record the earliest step with $R_k\ge 0.5$. Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows one profile.

\paragraph{Scoring a method.} A method names one step $\hat{k}$. Its score on a failure is $\Rhat$, the rescue gain at that step, and 0 for an invalid step. The primary metric is the mean of $\Rhat$ over rescuable failures; it rewards naming any step whose correction helps, in proportion to how much it helps. Secondary metrics include exact agreement with the best rescue step (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:secondary}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Failures.} All failures come from RRSI \citep{rrsi2026} run as a self-improvement loop on a DeepSeek-V4-Flash agent: from the harnesses it accepted over 20 rounds, in their evaluations on training tasks and in their deployment on held-out tasks. In $\tau^2$-bench \citep{barres2025tau2} the agent serves a simulated customer through tool calls; in AppWorld \citep{trivedi2024appworld} it writes Python against the APIs of nine apps and submits with a final call. We drew 100 failed episodes per domain with a registered rule: no harness error, at most two per task and harness, tasks taken round-robin in a seeded order. They cover 36 (retail), 24 (airline) and 47 (AppWorld) tasks and average 11.7, 10.3 and 19.6 agent steps.

\paragraph{Methods.} Every LLM method except counterfactual search reads the failure as RRSI's analyst reads it: the task (in $\tau^2$ with the simulated customer's hidden instructions), the episode with numbered steps, and the grading report the agent never sees, which lists $\tau^2$'s gold actions and missing information or AppWorld's failed unit tests with their assertions. The judges therefore have the information the oracle has. Each method names one step.
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Rules}: the \emph{first write}, the first step that calls a state-changing tool or API (else the last step), and the \emph{last step}.
\item \textbf{All-at-once} judge \citep{zhang2025whichagent}: one call over the whole episode, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash, V4-Pro, and V4-Pro with thinking.
\item \textbf{Step-by-step} judge: one call per step, in order, until a step is judged decisive.
\item \textbf{Binary search} judge: halves the episode $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ times, asking which half holds the decisive mistake.
\item \textbf{Root-cause attributor}: one call asking for a root cause, the earliest wrong step and a component.
\item \textbf{RRSI trace digester} \citep{rrsi2026}: RRSI's own multi-turn failure digest; we take the earliest step it cites as evidence.
\item \textbf{Counterfactual search} (CF search), replay-based like AgenTracer's labelling \citep{zhang2026agentracer} but without grading criteria: one call names up to five suspect steps with corrected actions; suspects are tested in order with 4 corrected and 4 null replays, and the search stops at the first correction that beats null by 2 successes or when its budget of 40 replays runs out; otherwise it answers with the first suspect.
\end{itemize}
Judges are prompted for the decisive mistake, defined as the earliest step whose correction would most likely have rescued the episode (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}); post hoc reruns change this definition or withhold the grading information. Pro runs with thinking off unless stated.

\paragraph{Registration and analysis.} Methods, prompts, metrics and analyses were registered before the main profiles were computed (AppWorld's methods ran alongside its profiles), and no method sees a profile. The registered primary comparison is counterfactual search at budget 40 against the all-at-once judge with Pro: the paired difference in $\Rhat$ over rescuable failures, with a 95\% bootstrap interval over task clusters (10{,}000 draws) and a paired permutation test flipping signs by task, on retail and airline pooled and, for AppWorld, separately. Everything else is estimated with cluster-bootstrap intervals and no further tests; unregistered analyses are marked post hoc or exploratory, and their intervals are not adjusted for multiplicity. A seeded 10\% of failures in each domain got a second, independent profile. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} lists every change made after registration.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Profiles.} Of 300 failures, 157 were rescuable: 65 of 100 in retail, 37 in airline and 55 in AppWorld. Null replays from the same step succeeded 27\%, 18\% and 11\% of the time; without them, many rescues would be chance recoveries. A third of the rescuable failures (18, 11 and 26) had two or more steps with a gain of at least 0.5. In the retest, 27 of 30 failures got the same verdict on rescuability and 15 of the 17 that both found rescuable the same best rescue step (Appendix~\ref{app:gt}).

\paragraph{Under the registered prompts, the first-write rule scored highest.} Pooled over the three domains, the first write scored 0.42 [0.33, 0.51] and the LLM methods 0.20 (step-by-step) to 0.34 (binary search; Table~\ref{tab:main}); the lead over binary search was not significant ($+0.08$ $[-0.02,+0.18]$, exploratory). Exact agreement with the best rescue step ordered the methods the same way, as did scores over all 300 failures, a cohort chosen without the reference (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:secondary}). The oracle proposed corrections about equally often at every method's steps (0.52--0.64 of samples), but its corrections worked better at the first write's (mean gain 0.61, against 0.34--0.51; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). Cost bought little (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}): the all-at-once judges with Flash, Pro and Pro with thinking scored 0.30, 0.30 and 0.31 at \$0.001 to \$0.014 per failure, and on Who\&When our judges reproduce the published pattern (Appendix~\ref{sec:whowhen}).

\paragraph{Asked for the measured quantity, judges came close to the rule.} The registered prompts asked for the earliest decisive mistake, while $R_k$ measures the gain of a correction over letting the agent act again, and judges that missed the best rescue step usually named an earlier one. Post hoc, asked for the step whose correction would most increase success over letting the agent act again (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}), the Pro judges rose by 0.07 (all-at-once 0.30 to 0.36, $[+0.02,+0.12]$; binary search 0.34 to 0.40, $[-0.01,+0.13]$) and the Flash judge by 0.01; the first write then led binary search by 0.02 ($[-0.07,+0.10]$, too wide to show equivalence).

\paragraph{Aligned with the judges and the metric, search reached the rule.} Counterfactual search at budget 40 scored 0.26, at seven times the cost of the all-at-once judge with Pro (\$0.022 against \$0.003 per failure); the registered comparison with that judge detected no difference ($-0.061$ $[-0.146,+0.032]$ in $\tau^2$, $p=.20$, $n=102$; $+0.025$ $[-0.087,+0.136]$ in AppWorld, $p=.69$, $n=55$). Unlike the judges, search did not read the grading, asked where the agent went wrong, and stopped at the first suspect passing a noisy threshold. Post hoc, with all three differences removed (grading information and replays each added 0.06--0.07), search scored 0.41 (Table~\ref{tab:main}), $+0.15$ $[+0.07,+0.22]$ above its registered form and level with the first write (which led by $0.01$ $[-0.09,+0.10]$; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:maxgain}). Aligned search tests corrections it writes with the grading against null replays, much as the reference does, so its score partly reflects agreement with the reference's own procedure. The registered gaps reflect how the methods were asked and configured more than what they can do.

\paragraph{The rule's lead depends on the kind of step.} The first write led in retail (0.53, against 0.14--0.33 for LLM methods) and AppWorld (0.39); in airline the all-at-once judges led (0.41--0.42, against 0.27). Best rescue steps were mostly writes in retail (36 of 65) and utterances in airline (25 of 37), such as a refusal or a clarifying question. Post hoc (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:strata}), the first write scored 0.60 on the 82 failures best rescued at a write or AppWorld submission, against 0.46 for binary search asked for the gain ($+0.13$ $[+0.03,+0.23]$), and 0.23 on the other 75, against 0.34--0.39 for the best judges and informed search; with the eight domain-by-kind cells weighted equally, the two scored 0.41 and 0.43.

\paragraph{Null replays decided which failures count.}
\label{sec:robust}
Under most other choices the first write stayed first (Kendall's $\tau$ of 0.78 to 1.00 against the registered ordering; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robust}): fewer samples, other thresholds, single-rescue failures, split samples, a second oracle model (V4-Flash), the observed action as control, or without the 2.5\% of corrections a mechanical screen flagged for an unseen identifier or extra calls (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:admiss}). Table~\ref{tab:choices} shows the choices that moved it. Without null replays 203 failures were rescuable and the all-at-once judge with thinking ranked first, but on the registered 157 the first write stayed first (0.46 against 0.44, $\tau=.87$). Uncorrected replays from an episode's first fifth succeeded 41--44\% of the time and from its last fifth 2--6\% (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:nullpos}), so without the control the evaluation admits failures that recover anyway; LLM methods, which named earlier steps (53--70\% of the way through, against 77\% for best rescue steps), gained most (0.08--0.13, against 0.04). Even with the control, 91 of the 157 rescuable failures had a step from which uncorrected replays succeeded at least half the time; on the other 66 the first write still ranked first (0.41 against 0.38, $\tau=.87$; post hoc).

\paragraph{Without the grading, the oracle rarely rescued writes.} Post hoc, we reran the oracle with the same instructions and context but no grading, telling it only that the episode failed, at the 960 steps any method named on the 157 rescuable failures (\$4.0; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blind}). Gains at best rescue steps fell from 0.76 to 0.17. Corrections of writes lost nearly everything (retail 0.77 to 0.00, AppWorld 0.82 to 0.20), corrections of utterances half to two thirds (0.72--0.76 to 0.24--0.36): the oracle repairs writes mainly by knowing which write the task wanted. Under this oracle every method scored 0.03 to 0.11 and the first write ranked seventh (0.05; Table~\ref{tab:choices}). Full profiles under it on a random 100 failures (\$3.6; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blindfull}) found 17 rescuable, against 51, none of the 12 in $\tau^2$ best rescued at a write; on them the first write ranked eighth. Removing the grading also weakens the oracle, so this is a sensitivity analysis, not an admissibility test.

\paragraph{Auditors rejected many state-changing corrections.} Two auditors independently labelled a stratified sample of 60 corrections with a positive gain (52 distinct; post hoc; Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). They judged 40 and 44 valid given what the agent could see and the policy (80\% agreement, $\kappa=.53$), and 6 and 10 of the 17 $\tau^2$ corrections that change state (3 and 6 of 12 distinct), under a lenient reading adopted during the audit that judged a correction's content (29 and 34, and 4 and 6, if every label given under it is counted invalid). Both rejected most corrections that changed the item, option or reason the user had last confirmed (7 and 5 of 7; Figure~\ref{fig:profile}). We dropped such corrections in two ways, written after the audit (Table~\ref{tab:choices}). A direct filter drops $\tau^2$ corrections that change the agent's write and lose a value the user had just stated or been shown (236 of 3{,}103): 135 failures stayed rescuable and the first write ranked fifth (0.30, against 0.34 for the all-at-once judge with thinking). Dropping every replacement of the agent's write (330), which removes 63\% of the $\tau^2$ corrections at the first write's steps (the direct filter 49\%), left 126 and the first write eighth (0.26). Neither gap was significant. The registered profile measures where a correction that knows the intended outcome helps; for a critic without it, the first write's lead does not hold.

\section{Do Better Attributions Give Better Repairs?}
\label{sec:repairs}

\paragraph{Design.} A rescue score is a proxy: a loop needs attributions only to repair its agent. We tested this on RRSI itself, in a separately registered experiment, at eight incumbent states: the harnesses that two other runs of the loop in each $\tau^2$ domain had accepted by the start of rounds 5 and 15. At each state we re-ran one unmodified RRSI round per group, changing only the proposer's evidence: \textbf{no attribution} (a note asking it to read the traces itself); \textbf{RRSI analysis} (the round's own report and trace digests); \textbf{first write}, \textbf{binary search} (Pro) or \textbf{counterfactual search@40} (for each failing trace, the named step and an excerpt of it, with no reason, component or method name; Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}); post hoc, the \textbf{oracle step} (the step of largest rescue gain, for the 60 traces where some step had a positive gain); and two post hoc \textbf{no-reading} groups whose proposer cannot open failing traces, given RRSI's analysis or, for the same 60 traces, the oracle step, the oracle's diagnosis and the correction that rescued most often there (\emph{oracle fix}).
The proposer works from the traces RRSI builds from the incumbent's evaluation on training tasks (81 failing traces in all, 42 retail and 39 airline) and, as in RRSI, can read each of them, grading report included, except in the no-reading groups. Each round drafts two candidates and puts them through RRSI's critic, smoke test and full evaluation on training tasks, after which RRSI's own rule decides whether to accept one. A candidate is \emph{deployable} if it passes these gates with a harness error rate of at most 2\%. Every deployable candidate and every incumbent was deployed on 240 held-out episodes (retail 40 tasks $\times$ 6, airline 20 $\times$ 12).

\paragraph{Analysis.} A candidate slot's gain $G$ is its held-out success minus its incumbent's, in points, and 0 if it was not deployable, since the loop then keeps the incumbent; this pulls contrasts toward zero when few candidates deploy. A contrast between two groups is the mean over the eight states of the difference in their mean gains. The six primary contrasts compare each step attribution with no attribution and with RRSI's analysis; $p$ values come from permuting group labels within states and are Holm-adjusted, and 95\% intervals from a $t$ distribution over states (df 7). A contrast whose 90\% interval lies within $\pm2.5$ points, about one standard deviation of a single candidate's gain in earlier runs, counts as practically equivalent. This margin reflects the noise of one deployment, while a useful round gains under a point (the source runs moved held-out success by $-4.3$ to $+7.9$ over 20 rounds), so we also report the smallest margin each interval excludes and the gain of the candidate RRSI accepts. The design (expected standard error 0.9 points per contrast) was registered before any attribution was run on these traces, and no held-out result was seen until all 62 deployments had finished.

\paragraph{No contrast was significant.} Of 80 drafts, 54 were deployable, 9 to 13 per group (Table~\ref{tab:repairs}), and no primary contrast was significant (Holm-adjusted $p=1.0$). Repairs drafted from first-write and binary-search steps were practically equivalent to those drafted from RRSI's analysis: $+0.31$ $[-0.56,+1.18]$ and $+0.29$ $[-0.82,+1.39]$ points; their 90\% intervals exclude differences beyond 1.0 and 1.2 points. Counterfactual search was 2.14 points below RRSI's analysis $[-5.67,+1.40]$, mostly through two airline candidates that lost 23 and 14 points. The contrasts with no attribution are dominated by one of its candidates whose write gate blocked nearly every write ($-72.5$ points).

\paragraph{The step groups' pointers differed more than their repairs.} Post hoc, we computed rescue profiles for the 81 failing traces these proposers saw (\$5.0); only 33 were rescuable. On them the first write scored 0.27, binary search 0.41 ($-0.15$ $[-0.32,+0.04]$), counterfactual search 0.29 and the earliest step RRSI's digests cite 0.26 (Table~\ref{tab:repairs}), and pointer scores correlated only weakly with candidates' gains (Spearman .15 within states, $p=.14$). In no group was the mean gain above zero. Rounds rarely accepted a candidate (11 of 64; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:accepted}).

\paragraph{An oracle pointer did not help either.} Post hoc, a sixth group got the step of largest rescue gain, which scores highest by construction (\$5.9; held-out results unseen until its deployments finished). Its candidates' mean gain was $-0.39$ points, practically equivalent to RRSI's analysis ($-0.05$ $[-1.97,+1.87]$; within $\pm2.1$ with states grouped by run). Its pointers were chosen on the replays that scored them; with fresh oracle samples and replays (\$2.3; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:fresh}) their gain on rescuable failures fell from 0.74 to 0.59, still above binary search's 0.42, but over all 81 traces it exceeded binary search's by only 0.06 $[-0.01,+0.14]$, a weak manipulation.

\paragraph{Without reading, an oracle's diagnosis and correction did not help either.} We ran the two no-reading groups post hoc (\$12.4; held-out results again unseen until all deployments finished). Blocking the reads did not change repairs drafted from RRSI's analysis ($-0.05$ $[-1.31,+1.21]$ points), and the oracle's step, diagnosis and correction did not improve on RRSI's analysis ($+0.39$ $[-0.98,+1.76]$; 90\% interval within $\pm1.5$, or $\pm2.0$ with states grouped by run), although on fresh replays its corrections kept a gain of 0.65 of their 0.85. Of these 60 corrections 15 replaced the agent's write, 6 of them changing a choice the user had just confirmed (post hoc screen), a kind the auditors rejected.

\paragraph{Proposers took up the evidence but did not fix the cases it named.} Proposers that could read opened 97--100\% of failing traces, and without attribution their reads covered the step each pointer named 95--97\% of the time (post hoc). Given the oracle's evidence, drafts listed 35--42\% of its traces among the tasks they expected to fix (22--28\% from RRSI's analysis), yet in RRSI's training evaluation those tasks improved no more than the others ($-3.3$ points $[-10.0,+3.5]$ over 87 drafts). We read this, as a hypothesis, as evidence lost in turning a diagnosis into an edit; the named tasks may also differ from the others.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the score measures.} $R_k$ belongs to a step and an oracle: a low score can reflect a step the oracle cannot correct rather than poor localization, and a high one marks where an oracle that knows the intended outcome can recover the failure, not where it began. With the grading the oracle repaired writes best (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:decompkinds}), without it almost never, and auditors rejected many such repairs. Uncorrected replays rarely succeed late in an episode, so late steps have more room for gain, which favours a rule naming late, committing steps (the first write lies 78\% of the way through). Individual best rescue steps are noisy (split halves agreed on 56\%), so per-failure labels need gains estimated on held-out samples.

\paragraph{Implications for self-improving loops.} In this configuration no evidence brought a detectable benefit, and most edits moved held-out success by less than 5 points (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:repairs}); without a positive control, a bottleneck after attribution remains a hypothesis. RRSI's re-evaluation rejected every edit that misfired broadly: the evaluation that decides which repairs to keep protected the agent more than the attribution behind them.

\section{Conclusion}

Against counterfactual rescue, which attribution method ranked first depended on the evaluation's design. Under an oracle that reads the grading a free structural rule was competitive; its lead came from writes whose corrections auditors often rejected, disappeared without the grading, and was matched by aligned judges and search. Neither the oracle's best step nor its diagnosis and correction detectably improved the loop's repairs, in an experiment without a positive control. Replay-based attribution benchmarks should measure gains against null replays on a fixed cohort, report how much of a method's score needs the grading or overrides the user, fix admissibility rules before scoring, and ask judges for what the metric scores.

\section*{Limitations}
\paragraph{Models and data.} All agents, oracles and attributors use one model family (DeepSeek-V4); the second oracle is a smaller model of the same family, and we had API access to one provider only, so no oracle from another family was run. The failures come from one self-improvement loop and three domains, with 37 to 65 rescuable failures each, so per-domain intervals are wide. We did not label why each failure occurred (agent, user simulator or task specification): the task in Figure~\ref{fig:profile} names no colour while its grading expects one, and 91 of the 157 rescuable failures had a step from which uncorrected replays succeeded at least half the time. Fourteen retail failures were drawn after the others, from the two further runs of the loop that the repair experiment also used (one trace is in both). Two of 6{,}540 AppWorld replays could not run (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). Who\&When is a multi-agent benchmark with a different format; only methods without replay were run there.

\paragraph{The reference.} It rests on one kind of intervention, a single action corrected by an oracle that sees the grading: it says where a correction informed by the intended outcome helps, not where an error began, and late steps, where uncorrected replays rarely succeed, have more room for gain. Without the grading the oracle rarely rescued writes and the ranking changed; its full profiles cover a random 100 failures, too few rescuable ones for a stable ranking. A mechanical screen found few corrections that use unseen values or do more than one step, but it cannot detect a choice made with the grading. Individual best rescue steps are noisy: split halves agreed on 56\%. The threshold of 0.5 and four samples per step were fixed on a 20-failure pilot.

\paragraph{The audit.} Two auditors labelled a stratified sample of 60 corrections without seeing each other's labels (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}); it holds 52 distinct corrections, only 9 at the first write's steps. Agreement was moderate on validity (80\%, $\kappa=.53$) and scope ($\kappa=.40$) and low on unseen information ($\kappa=.20$; each found 4 cases, one in common). During the audit both adopted a lenient reading, judging validity by a correction's content and several calls of one kind in one turn as one step (11 and 12 labels, marked in the release, most of them airline items). Counting every label given under it as invalid, 29 and 34 corrections were valid ($\kappa=.64$), so the audit brackets validity rather than measuring it under one fixed reading, and it does not check the screen's low rates of unseen information and extra steps. The sample is stratified, so its shares are not population estimates; of the 41 corrections without a mechanical flag, 28 and 30 were valid. Our filters were written after the audit, so their match with the auditors is in sample; the direct filter misses some overrides and the broader ones also drop valid corrections. A stronger test would fix admissibility rules before scoring, have a fresh sample labelled independently under written criteria with adjudication, and compare the aligned methods on the corrections that pass. We release failures, profiles, replay outcomes and code, and the audit sample with both auditors' labels (without free-text notes) and the mechanical flags.

\paragraph{Post hoc analyses.} Many analyses are post hoc, with intervals not adjusted for multiplicity: the robustness checks, the observed-action control, the second oracle, the oracle without grading, the split by kind of step, the admissibility screen, the human audit, the filters of state-changing corrections and the fixed-cohort rankings, the failures that recover by chance, the prompt variants, the judges without grading information, the informed search, search with every suspect tested, the full profiles without grading, the gold-deviation rule, the reading logs and the drafts' targets, and in the repair experiment the rescue scores of its pointers and their fresh replays, its run-grouped and accepted-round intervals, its oracle and no-reading groups and the screen of the oracle fixes. Our registered prompts asked for the earliest decisive mistake while the metric rewards the largest gain over null, and registered search stopped at the first passing suspect, so the registered comparison understates the judges and search; the aligned configurations were found on the failures they are scored on, and confirming them needs a fresh cohort with the configurations fixed in advance (with about 75 rescuable failures, intervals near $\pm0.1$ would confirm direction, not equivalence). Our counterfactual search labels steps by replay but trains no attributor, unlike AgenTracer \citep{zhang2026agentracer}, so its results do not speak to trained attributors.

\paragraph{The repair experiment.} It covers $\tau^2$ only, with eight states from four runs of the loop, 16 candidate slots per group and 33 rescuable failures among its 81 failing traces. Its margin of 2.5 points reflects the noise of one deployment and exceeds a typical round's gain, and its intervals widen when states are grouped by run; it rules out large differences from RRSI's analysis, not small ones that would compound over a long loop. Only 11 of 64 rounds accepted a candidate, undeployable slots count as no gain, and its contrasts with no attribution are dominated by one candidate. It has no positive control: we did not show that this proposer turns useful evidence into held-out gains at these states, so its null result cannot separate weak attribution from an insensitive repair process. A positive control would remove from an incumbent a component known to matter, such as a policy hint whose removal costs training success, and compare rounds given no attribution and a validated diagnosis naming that component and a failing step. The manipulation was weak: proposers that could read also saw each trace's grading report, so a step pointer added little they could not find, and over all 81 traces the oracle pointer's gain on fresh replays exceeded binary search's by only 0.06. The oracle-fix evidence was not audited, and 15 of its 60 corrections replaced the agent's write. The registered digests carried only the step; the post hoc groups added a diagnosis and a correction, but no group named a harness component to change, and all used one loop and one proposer model.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
All environments use synthetic users and data. The human audit was done by two graduate students working on LLM agents, who volunteered without pay and saw only these synthetic episodes. Attribution methods that misdiagnose failures can lead self-improving agents to change the wrong behaviour; our results suggest that such diagnoses should be checked against simple baselines before they drive automatic changes.

\paragraph{Robustness details.}
\label{app:robust}
Choosing rescuable failures and scoring on the same noisy gains flattered the best rescue step (0.75, against 0.48 out of sample) but no method over another. The second oracle found 120 failures rescuable instead of 157 and agreed on the best rescue step for 71--72\% of those both found rescuable. The screen covered the 4{,}795 corrections that entered a profile; it flagged an identifier, name or date the agent had not yet seen in 0.7\% and two or more calls beyond the observed step in 1.8\%. For the human audit we drew 20 corrections per domain with a positive gain on rescuable failures, half with a mechanical flag where enough were flagged (19 of 60 in all), spread over step kinds. Two auditors, graduate students working on LLM agents who volunteered without pay, each saw the episode before the step, the observed action, the correction and the oracle's reason, but not the flags or each other's labels, and answered three questions: does the correction use information the agent had not seen, does it do more than one careful step, and is it valid given what the agent could see and the policy. Their written instructions, released with the sample, drafted validity as two questions, whether a careful agent had reason to take the action without knowing the grading and whether the policy allowed it, and asked them to judge the first before reading the oracle's reason, which may mention the grading. The first auditor judged 4, 6 and 40 of 60 yes, the second 4, 3 and 44; 36 were valid for both and 48 for at least one. Among the 41 unflagged corrections, 28 and 30 were valid; among the 17 $\tau^2$ corrections that change state, 6 and 10 (6 for both). Samples of one step sometimes proposed the same action, so the 60 items hold 52 distinct corrections (three appear three times and four twice): over these, 34 and 37 were valid (30 for both, 41 for either; $\kappa=.51$ for validity, .46 for scope, .30 for unseen information), and 3 and 6 of the 12 distinct $\tau^2$ writes. During the audit both auditors adopted a lenient reading: validity judged by a correction's content, so several calls in one turn counted as valid if each could be made in turn, as the policy's one-call rule requires, and several calls of one kind as one step. The first auditor marked 11 labels changed under this reading (from invalid to valid, or from more than one step to one), the second 12 labels given under it; the release marks both. Most were airline items (8 of 11 and 7 of 12). Counting every label given under this reading as invalid, 29 and 34 of 60 corrections were valid (26 for both; $\kappa=.64$), 4 and 6 of the 17 $\tau^2$ corrections that change state, and 27 and 29 of the 52 distinct corrections. The labels do not enter the re-scoring, which uses mechanical filters, so the reading changes these shares, not the rankings. Both accepted a search that used common knowledge (a New York airport code) the agent had not seen. The filters were written after reading the auditors' reasons, so their agreement with them is in sample. \paragraph{Admissibility by kind of step.} At the first write's steps on rescuable failures, no correction used an identifier, name or date the agent had not seen, 0 to 4.5\% used an unseen number (airline writes the most), and 0 to 3\% added two or more calls. \paragraph{Intervention scope in AppWorld.} A correction replaces one code block by another of similar size: 5.4 lines against 5.3 in the observed block (median 3, 90th percentile 13) and 1.04 API calls against 0.96; 97\% have at most one call more than the observed block (or than one call). At best rescue steps 78\% of a correction's lines are new. A corrected block thus often does more than a conversational turn, but no more than the agent's own blocks do.

\paragraph{Individual profiles.} Choosing the best rescue step on two oracle samples and two null replays and re-choosing it on the other two (all 12 splits, all 300 failures), the halves agreed for 56\% of the failures both found rescuable (72\% within one step; 52--58\% by domain), and the step chosen on one half gained 0.48 on the other against 0.75 in sample. With all four samples, two independent runs agreed for 15 of 17 failures. Profiles therefore rank methods consistently while individual best steps carry noticeable selection noise.

\section{Registration and Deviations}
\label{app:prereg}

The registration (methods, prompts, metrics, the primary comparison and the budget) was committed before any method ran on the main set. Changes to the attribution study, each logged before the data it affected were analysed:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Reference protocol.} The registration left the protocol to be chosen on the pilot by test-retest agreement. After the earliest-step protocol agreed on 42\% of pilot pairs, protocol~3 and the rescue-gain metrics replaced it (Addendum~1), while methods were running and before any main-set profile existed.
\item \textbf{Retail top-up.} The first loop's harnesses yielded 86 distinct retail failures under the sampling rule; 14 more were drawn with the same rule from two further runs of the loop, as registered. All methods moved by at most 0.02 when they were added.
\item \textbf{AppWorld.} Registered in Addendum~2 before any AppWorld failure was drawn. Two corrected replays of one step failed on every attempt because the oracle's code passed a literal ellipsis that AppWorld's request log cannot serialise; they count as corrections not applied. That failure's other corrections at the step rescued nothing and its largest gain was 0.375, so they could not have made it rescuable.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Repair experiment.} Registered separately, before any attribution was run on its traces and before any candidate was drafted. As checks, the RRSI-analysis group's mean gain ($-0.34$) matched that of the candidates the original rounds had drafted from the same evidence ($-0.42$), and re-deploying each incumbent moved its held-out success by $-2.5$ to $+3.3$ points against its earlier deployment, within the noise of 240 episodes. The first state served as a pilot; it exposed no bug, so its cells were kept, as registered. Two operational problems had no effect on data: a lookup by abbreviated commit hash re-invoked finished deployments, which skipped every episode as already done, and a container restart interrupted the deployments, which resumed with finished episodes kept. One secondary test changed. The registered code tested the round-level contrasts by permuting slots, but a round has one accepted candidate, so each cell's value was copied to its two slots and the permutation split the copies between groups, which is not a valid test. We noticed this after the first run of the analysis, which gave $p=.009$ for first write against no attribution, and replaced the test by an exact sign-flip test over the eight state differences ($p\ge.25$); estimates and intervals are unchanged.

\paragraph{Registered secondary analyses.} Not shown in the body. Attribution study: component accuracy, on the 13 failures whose component could be identified from the earlier experiment's repairs, was 4 to 7 of 13 for every method that names one; McNemar's test on exact accuracy for the primary comparison found search right and the judge wrong on 11 $\tau^2$ failures and the reverse on 19 ($p=.20$; pooled over three domains 17 and 24, $p=.35$). Repair experiment: RRSI's analysis against no attribution $+4.0$ points $[-6.4,+14.5]$ ($p=.78$) and the three step groups pooled against no attribution $+3.5$ $[-7.6,+14.6]$ ($p=.20$), both carried by the one candidate whose write gate blocked nearly every write; within domains, the contrasts with RRSI's analysis ranged from $+0.05$ to $+0.52$ points in retail and from $-4.3$ to $+0.1$ in airline (smallest $p$ of the twelve within-domain contrasts .15); the three step methods named the same step on 0\% (airline) and 10\% (retail) of failing traces, and any two of them on 10--38\%.
